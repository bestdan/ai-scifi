# Human feedback — Raking Light (calibration-01)

The human's read of `final.md`, gathered by interview. It runs alongside the
lens reviews in `reviews/`, and it records how the human reacted, in the
human's own words. The orchestrator writes the notes up but doesn't add its
own critique.

Tag every note **[story]**, meaning the text should change, or
**[process: stage N]**, meaning the pipeline should have caught it at stage N.

## 1. First impressions

What the human said before rereading `MORNING.md` or the reviews: what the
story is about, where the read flowed or snagged, and how the ending landed.

**Orientation was hard.** "There was a lot of names very quickly." The
characters arrive with nothing to anchor who each one is or what role they
play, so the human struggled to keep track of them through the rest of the
story.

**The world-building was good ideas, delivered rough.** "I had to infer so
much that I wasn't sure I really understood what was going on." The human
enjoys the intellectual concepts most of all, so the story should sometimes
be clearer about what the reader is meant to understand or take away.

**The central idea didn't come through.** Asked for the story's central idea,
the human would say: "some sort of thing where people metamorphise into new
versions of themselves over a period and there's some sort of external record
they have to keep, but I'm not really sure what the point of all that is."
The story didn't read as an interesting view of a different, coherent
society.

**The length may be the cause.** The human suspects the short length (2,511
words) made it hard to get everything across.

**What worked:**

- **The prose.** "A lot of the writing was actually really good." The
  phrasing gave the human a sense of being in a different place, in a
  different world.
- **The relationships.** The set-up of the characters, how they care for each
  other and how they relate, was well done.
- **The turns of phrase.** There were "some really lovely turns of phrase and
  ways of expressing things."

**The main stakes didn't land.** Asked what Oyelaran's role was and what had
to happen when the nine days ran out, the human had "no real idea." The
pieces were there: "the idea of a keeper, but also somebody giving arguments
or a judgment, like the terms were there, but they were not put together in a
way that I could understand."

This was an unprimed read. The human hadn't read `MORNING.md` before reading
the story.

**This disagrees with the panel's reader.** `MORNING.md` reports that on
draft 2, a fresh First Reader "followed the treaty." The human, reading the
polished version, did not.

**The premise, stated plainly, still doesn't grab the human "as written."**
Shown the `MORNING.md` summary, the human chose (b), an idea that doesn't
grab even when it's clear, and qualified it with "as written." What's missing
is the motivation and the context:

- Why did the polities come to this agreement?
- What is at stake if she makes the judgment, or if she is persuaded?
- How did the metamorphosis come about?
- How integral is the metamorphosis, to the treaty or to anything else?

**The story bible already answers most of these.** The human's questions
match what `04-bible.md` deliberately keeps off the page.

| The human's question | `04-bible.md` |
|---|---|
| Why the agreement? | Polities that can rewrite their own values can't make a credible promise, so they escrow the peace in an unpersuadable human. ("Why the polities did it": a successor judge's conscience can't be gamed.) |
| What's at stake? | A polity found faithless forfeits the Disputed Volume, a star system both want. Arguing to the Keeper voids the treaty (§ 9). |
| Where does the metamorphosis come from? | It's an engineered return of childhood plasticity, about every 25 years. The bible heads it "familiar to the characters, never explained." |
| How integral is it? | The term was set to end just after her turn, so a judge nobody could know in advance decides. That is the reveal, and no reader caught it. |

The answers existed by stage 4. They were kept off the page on purpose,
under the bible's "explained only through the treaty text and the clerk" and
"never explained" rules, and inside a 2,500-word budget.

**With the answers in hand, the idea works.** Given the bible's answers, the
human found the premise "definitely a lot better and more interesting," so
the honest classification is closer to (a): a good idea, with too much of it
hidden. The human still wants to understand more of the set-up, especially
what it means for the polities to rewrite their own values.

**The diagnosis: too short for the ambition.** "Twenty five hundred words was
just too short for such an ambitious set of ideas." The brief set 2,500 words.
That is below the 3,000-word floor for a short story in `PROCESS.md`'s
Lengths table.

**The comparison is Greg Egan.** The story reminded the human of Egan's work,
which "expects or requires a lot of the reader to understand or infer from
unstated things."

**The human's rule for inference: it has to pay off, and the author always
confirms it.** The human likes having to infer, "but it definitely needs to
pay off." Part of the pleasure is working something out before the author
says it, "but I think it's safest if the author always makes it explicit
eventually to make sure that readers get the payoff."

In practice, the story can withhold something only for a while. Every
inferred element, whether the novum, the stakes or the twist, has to be made
explicit somewhere on the page by the end.

- **The 3 a.m. paragraph:** keep it as one dense paragraph, or split it after

- **The stage 3 pick:** B, *The Keeper's Successor*, over A, *The

them could be answered. Here line-level polish was working on a story whose
core hadn't come across.

human found it too hard to follow.

## 5. Process takeaways

The process-tagged notes, gathered here for the agent working on
`process/PROCESS.md`. These are what the human's feedback points to. They
are not changes anyone has made yet.

1. **Every inference pays off explicitly.** [process: stages 4, 6, 9 and the
   style guide] The text may withhold the novum, the stakes or the twist for
   a while, but by the end each one is stated on the page. The bible's
   "never explained" and "explained only through…" rules break this as
   written. Every answer to the human's "why" questions was in
   `04-bible.md`, and none of them reached the reader.
2. **Length follows ambition.** [process: stage 0] 2,500 words was too short
   for two nova, two polities and a twist. The brief overrode the
   3,000-word floor in `PROCESS.md`. Either enforce the floor or have the
   brief check the novum load against the word budget.
3. **Fewer names, introduced slowly, each with an anchor.** [process: stages
   5–6] A character or invented term arrives with its role attached, and the
   opening scene carries few of them. The human followed the characters in
   the scenes with fewer people.
4. **The First Reader isn't a strong enough test of clarity.** [process:
   stage 7] It "followed the treaty" on draft 2. The human, reading the final
   version, did not. Consider a comprehension check: a fresh reader with
   only the manuscript writes down the premise, the stakes and the twist,
   and the orchestrator compares that with the bible. A gap blocks polish.
5. **The panel over-scores comprehension and under-scores prose.** [process:
   stage 7, rubric] The human scored Consequence and Character 2 against the
   panel's 4, and Prose 5 against the panel's 3–4.
6. **Polish protects lyricism as well as cutting it.** [process: stage 9 and
   the style guide] The human wants more flowery, poetic lines when they
   earn their place. The banned-list pass and the "zinger" cuts push the
   other way. The protected lines in section 4 show what earns its place:
   concrete physical images and moments between people, not abstract
   stacked constructions.
7. **Feeling isn't understanding.** [process: rubric] The mood, texture and
   loss landed (Novum delivery 4.5, "got the vibe") while the logic didn't.
   The rubric should separate "did the reader feel it" from "could the
   reader explain it."
8. **The human reads blind.** [process: gates] The human wants to read the
   next story without knowing its idea in advance. That conflicts with "the
   human decides at every gate" (`AGENTS.md`, `PROCESS.md`), since the gates
   at stages 2 and 3 show the premise. Some arrangement has to be chosen:
   the orchestrator acting as proxy at the idea gates, as it did for
   calibration-01, or gates that hide the premise.
