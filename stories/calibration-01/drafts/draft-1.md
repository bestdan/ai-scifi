# Raking Light

## Nine

Mr. Pell came up from the jetty at seven with the oilskin satchel held level in front of him, the way other men carry a full bowl. Behind him the lake was flat and grey as a spoon. He stopped at the gate, as he had stopped at it every quarter for twenty-two years, and waited to be asked in, because the escrow office held that a keeper's garden was part of the seat.

"Keeper," he said. "The ninetieth and final accounts, one from each Party, received at the office at ten to six and carried here by me without once being set down, which on the boat meant holding them on my knees and on the jetty meant refusing a cup of tea from the ferryman, and I hope he understood."

Oyelaran signed his book. Her face did what it always did at the gate, which was very little. Twenty-two years of reading things that wanted a reaction from her had left it with almost nothing to do on its own.

"With these," Pell said, buckling the satchel, "the term of 'twenty-two years, seven months and nine days' has nine days to run." He said the term the way some people say a psalm, with the commas in.

She took the envelopes to the kitchen table. Bisi had put out the tea and the knife she kept for her pencils, because he knew the order of her mornings better than she did. She slit the Standing Revision's envelope first. The heading was as it had been ninety times: the Party's name in small capitals and, alone on the line beneath it, an integer. This one was 7. The first account, twenty-two years ago, had opened with a number eleven digits long. The integers had fallen every quarter since, and she had never learned what they counted. Somewhere in the margin of the third quarter's account there was a question mark in her hand, and nothing after it.

On page nine of forty the Standing Revision reported that it had wanted the Disputed Volume on the eleventh, nineteenth, twentieth and forty-first days of the quarter, had revised itself on each occasion, and regretted the inconvenience to the Keeper of reading about it.

She drank her tea. It tasted of nothing, the way a word goes to nothing when you have said it forty times. She drank it anyway, because Bisi was watching her from the stove.

"What did the lake say to the mail boat?" he said.

She waited. She had waited for this one, on this morning of the quarter, for longer than she had been herself.

"Nothing," said Bisi. "It waved."

She did not laugh. She never had. He took the cup from her and sniffed it, and put the kettle back on, which was the nearest he came to saying anything.

By the door hung the treaty's last page, framed, with both seals, which she dusted on Sundays. Bisi stood looking at it while the kettle came up.

"You'd been you thirty-two months when they came," he said. "I'd only just got used to you eating eels."

Under the seals ran § 14, which she had always liked best: "Each Party warrants that the self that executes this instrument has been retained unedited, sealed, to be consulted solely in the event of the Party's dispute with itself."

## Eight

On the way to the surgery the lake smelled of weed and outboard fuel, and she found she was crying. She stood by the boat ramp until it passed.

Dr. Ferreira swabbed her tongue, held the swab against the colour chart taped to his window, and said, "Started. About a week to settle, from when the taste went."

It was dark by half past four. When she came into the bakery the windows had already turned to mirrors, except low in the east, where the Volume's star stood over the water.

Ros was wiping down the counter. Hanne Oduya sat at the table by the radiator with a seed bun she was taking apart rather than eating. Mina knelt on the bench under the window, and turned round when the bell went.

"Auntie," she said, and pointed. "What do you think about *that* one?"

"Mina," said Ros. Only the name, flat, the way you say *stove* to a child who is reaching.

Mina's ears went red. Oyelaran's face did nothing, which was the kindest thing it knew how to do, and she asked for a small brown loaf.

"Started?" said Hanne, without looking up.

"Yesterday. The tea."

"It's always the tea." Hanne was in her eighties and had turned four times, and her own treaty had ended before Ros was born. "Twenty-five years, never more than a season late, and it always starts with the tea."

Ros cut the heel off the loaf for her to try, because she did that for everyone. It tasted of card. Oyelaran said it was very good.

"Your next will like the bakery," Hanne said. "Mine did. The one after her couldn't stand bread. Wasted on her."

Mina had got over her ears. "Why does everybody have to?"

"So we don't set," said Hanne. "Otherwise we'd all be our grandparents forever." She put a piece of seed bun in her mouth and chewed it with great thoroughness, to show what a self that liked bread could do.

## Eight

That evening she sharpened two pencils with the knife, in short strokes away from her towards the window, and wrote at the kitchen table.

*To my next,* she wrote, and then the practical things. The spare key was in the boot by the shed. The herons came to the north reed bed in the second week of March and should not be approached across the open mud.

In the second paragraph she wrote: *You will remember the accounts, but you will not remember them as I do. If you read them again from the beginning you will see that the Settled Question's accounts are too perfect. They are perfect in a way that*

She stopped with the pencil on the paper. She had read § 9 more often than her own name: "No party, agent or person shall present to the Keeper argument bearing on the Determination." It did not say *except the Keeper*. It had been drafted by minds that did not leave words out by accident.

Bisi had made eel stew with scotch bonnets and set a bowl at her elbow. He read the paragraph over her shoulder before she could cover it, and looked away, which was worse.

"What do you think, Sade?" he said. "About them. Truly."

In twenty-five years nobody in the house had asked her that. Children in Sallow Reach knew not to ask Auntie by the time they could see over the counter. She looked at him, and he looked at the stew.

"All right. You can't put it in a letter," he said. "You came back from the last one liking eels. You don't know what she'll think."

"No."

"So I'll tell her." He said it quickly, like a man who had practised in the shed. "Every night, while I court her. That's courting, not arguing."

"You'd be a person presenting argument to the Keeper."

"I'd be a husband."

"Pell would have it void by Thursday," she said, "and they'd both be in the Volume by Friday. No, Bisi."

He did not argue, which was how she knew he had known the answer before he asked. He took the bowl she had not touched, washed it and put it on the shelf. Then he went out to the boat shed, where there had been no boat for six years, only his old planes in their rack.

She tore the letter into strips and fed them to the stove, which was allowed, because the letter was hers.

## Seven

She woke at three, hungrier than she had been since she was a girl. She ate cold eel stew from the pot with a serving spoon, standing at the stove in her socks. It tasted of something now. She had no word for it, and she ate half the pot.

Then she took the bound treaty down from the shelf, because if there was a door in § 9 it would be somewhere in the other forty-one sections, and she read it under the lamp with her coat over her nightdress.

There was no door. There was the term, on the first page, where it had always been. She had noticed its nine days at the signing, in the long room at the escrow office. A term that exact had a reason, and she had felt it waiting to be worked out, and she had signed instead. She had wanted the seat. She could admit that at three in the morning with eel on her breath.

She worked it out now on the back of an envelope. Thirty-two months since her last turn when they came: two years and eight months. Add the term, and it came to twenty-five years and three months. Hanne's twenty-five years and Hanne's season, to the day. Then nine days more, room for Dr. Ferreira's week with a little over. Her tea had gone flat nine days out.

They had built the term around the latest she could possibly be.

Anyone who wanted her judgment would have set the date in the middle of her, where she was known. They had had twenty-two years to learn the woman at this table, and she supposed they had learned her: what she noticed, what made her pencil move. A Party could have written its accounts to her, to look faithful in the light she read by. It could not write to her next, because nobody knew her next, her next included. It could only hedge, across every woman Folasade Oyelaran might turn out to be. Hedge widely enough and it came very close to keeping faith.

*You came back from the last one liking eels.*

She opened the nearest box and drew out an account from somewhere in the forties, and found her own hand in the margin beside a paragraph about freight. *Sly*, underlined once, lightly. She remembered writing it and being pleased. It read now like a note from a woman who enjoyed catching people out. She put it back in the box.

## Seven

She sent for Pell with the milk boat, and he came at eleven in his second-best coat, which meant he had guessed it was a ruling.

He would not sit. He stood at the end of the table with his hat against his chest and listened to her question with his eyes shut, so that nothing in her face could reach him either.

"While you hold the seat, Keeper," he said, "your marginalia are the Keeper's own apparatus, like your spectacles. Nobody can present you with your own spectacles. On the date, the archive passes intact to the Keeper." He took the schedule from his inside pocket, though he could not have needed it, and read: "'the person occupying the seat on the date of Determination.'" He folded it. "At that point a note in your hand stops being apparatus and becomes argument presented by a person, and the person, I'm sorry to say, is you."

"And what I remember?"

"Memory is not presented. You may remember anything you like. So may your next, in whatever way she remembers things." He glanced at the stove, which was lit. "The accounts are treaty property, Keeper, and pass intact, and I mention it only because the stove is going and you are a thorough woman."

"Mr. Pell. When they set the date, did they know?"

He considered it for long enough that Bisi, at the sink, stopped drying a plate.

"The Parties chose that number with exactly the care they gave every other number in the instrument," Pell said, "and I should be failing the instrument if I let you think they took more care there than anywhere else, or that I would know if they had."

He put his hat on, which he did only out of doors, apologised, and carried it in his hand as far as the gate.

## Two

On the last night but one Bisi cooked as if for a wedding: efo riro, and pounded yam he had beaten in the old mortar on the back step. She could not taste it. Everything tasted of the thing with no name. He put it in front of her anyway, and she ate a plateful, and he watched her eat it with his elbows on the table, the way he had watched her all through the year he courted her the last time.

Then the plates went to the sink and the boxes came out. Ninety accounts from each Party, a hundred and eighty in all, every page with a margin and most of the margins with something in them.

Three people came. Hanne brought her own eraser in a tobacco tin, grey and soft as a mushroom. Ros brought a bag of white ones from the stationer's. Bisi used the rubber off the end of a pencil until Hanne took pity and gave him a proper one. "Here, twice-met," she said.

They worked the way keepers' families learn to, eyes soft, looking at the margin and never the text, and when a page was clean they laid it face-down on the pile so that nobody would read it by accident. The table filled with grey crumbs of rubber. Erasing makes a dry, busy sound, like something small living in a wall.

Around midnight Ros set her eraser down beside her cup. She kept her hands flat on the table and looked at the black window, and for a long time she did not pick it up again, and nobody asked her to.

"You know the other reading," Hanne said, rubbing out a column of something from the fifties without looking at it. "They're too big to notice a woman's tongue going flat. They picked a number they liked the look of."

"I know," said Oyelaran, and went on.

In the margin of the 2041 third-quarter account, beside a paragraph about the Settled Question's mining, there was a heron. She had drawn it one March afternoon with the account on her knee, in seven or eight lines: the hunched neck, and the bill like a shut pair of scissors. Bisi put his hand flat over it.

"That's not argument. That's a heron."

"It's in the margin."

"It doesn't say anything."

She turned the page round so that he had it the right way up. "It says *look at herons*," she said, and moved his hand, and erased it, the legs first.

Hanne blew the crumbs off. Ros picked up her eraser and took the next account from the box.

Some time after one, Bisi, who had said nothing since the heron, looked up.

"What did the lake say to the mail boat?"

Hanne groaned. Ros put her face in her floury hands. Oyelaran waited, as she had always waited.

"Nothing," said Bisi. "It waved."

She laughed. It came up out of her like a hiccup, far too loud for the kitchen, and then it kept coming, and she had to hold the edge of the table. It was the same terrible joke. She could not stop.

Bisi did not laugh. He sat with the eraser in his fingers and looked at her across the stacks, and she looked back at him, and they both heard it. Then he bent his head to the margin, and after a while so did she.

She had kept the last page for herself: the first page of the first account, written over more thickly than anything since, because she had been new. The others had gone home at two. Bisi was asleep in the chair by the stove with his mouth open and a pencil still in his hand.

She erased it and blew it clean. The sun came over the far shore low and flat, straight down the length of the lake and in at the east window, and there was no boat on the water yet. She held the page up to the window at an angle, the way she had once been taught to read a watermark.

The margin was white. In the raking light her hand stood up out of it anyway, every stroke she had ever pressed there, shallow as the trail of a snail on a step. She had always written lightly.

She laid the page face-down on the others and squared the stack, knocking its long edge twice against the table, the way she always had.
