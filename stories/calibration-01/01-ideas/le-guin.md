# Idea cards: Le Guin (the Anthropologist)

Two cards, per the calibration-01 brief. Both start from the lens's ideation
question: what basic human question does this society answer differently?

**Blind spots, stated up front.** These cards are thin on hard mechanism and
lean toward slow, gathering-shaped stories with a risk of moral instruction.
Egan or Watts should push on the biology. Banks should check that something
actually happens in 2,500 words.

## Card 1: The Taking Ground

- **Novum:** Each watershed's people carry an engineered gut consortium bred
  to that valley's soil, and a newborn's kin is whichever household's ground
  its gut "takes" to at the seeding. You are raised by the people who work
  the earth your body accepts.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kinship and place have merged into one category, and
  birth parents are no longer parents by default.
- **How it works:** Restoration-era communities bound themselves to land so
  they could not strip it and move on. Away from your ground's microbes you
  lose the ability to digest most food within weeks. Infants are dosed with
  soil from several fields, and one strain wins.
- **First-order consequence:** Babies routinely go to other households, and
  that grief is ritualized, expected and shared.
- **Second-order consequence:** Fields are kin. Selling or flooding a field
  orphans everyone who took to it.
- **Third-order consequence:** Nobody can emigrate, marriage is
  geography, and the word for "mother" means "the field".
- **The story:** Hesket, an earth-reader, runs the seedings. Her daughter's
  infant takes to the low meadow, which the valley has voted to give to the
  river next spring. Hesket can misread the result. Over one seeding day she
  decides whether to lie, and whose grief to choose.
- **Why this lens:** who raises the children, and who pays for the
  ambiguous utopia.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Always Coming Home* for the place-bound valley;
  Tepper's *The Gate to Women's Country* for children handed between
  households. The microbial mechanism has no close match that I know of.

## Card 2: Wintering

- **Novum:** At terminal decline the old are put into a reversible torpor
  and kept warm in the house for years. They can be woken once, for about a
  day, and the waking is what kills them. Their language has no word for
  "dead" until that day is spent.
- **Futurity:** F3. Death has stopped being an event and become a household
  decision and a shared resource.
- **How it works:** Metabolism runs at about a twentieth of normal, fed
  through a gut line. Revival burns every reserve the body has left, and the
  neural restart cascade only runs once. The winterer sets terms beforehand
  ("wake me for…"), and the family can break them.
- **First-order consequence:** Back rooms full of breathing elders. A plate
  is set for them at meals, and someone tends them for decades.
- **Second-order consequence:** Inheritance waits for the waking, so the
  waking becomes a lever in land disputes, marriages and feuds.
- **Third-order consequence:** The young measure love by restraint: keeping
  a winterer unspent is devotion, and spending one is a scandal or a
  sacrament.
- **The story:** Odd Marrit has tended her grandfather for eleven years. He
  asked to be woken when she married, and she never has. Her brothers need
  his testimony on a boundary only he knows. The story covers the gathering
  the night before, and the one day.
- **Why this lens:** what death means, an ordinary day, and the carrier bag
  over the spear.
- **Suspected prior art:** the real Toraja practice of keeping the dead at
  home as "the sick"; Vonnegut's "2BR02B" only loosely. The one-use waking
  seems uncharted, but the Cartographer should check.
