# Idea cards: Chiang

Two cards, per the calibration-01 brief. Each card names its philosophical
question and the form that would enact it.

**Blind spots, stated up front:** both cards are low on external conflict and
plot momentum, and neither has an antagonist. Both lean on interior choice and
a measured register. Banks and Watts should push on whether either one moves
fast enough for 2,500 words.

**Discarded before writing:** a card about a fetus briefly made lucid so it
could consent to being born. Akutagawa's *Kappa* already has the unborn asked
whether they want to be born, so it was too close a match.

## Card 1: The Ratification

- **Question:** can you make a promise on behalf of the person you will become?
- **Novum:** adults go through a *rebase* every twenty-odd years. A medical
  procedure reopens childhood neural plasticity, so memories survive but values
  and temperament re-form from scratch. The law treats each era as a new
  person, and marriages, debts and vows lapse unless the new self ratifies them
  within a year.
- **Futurity:** F3. "Self" now means a series of tenants in one life.
- **How it works:** the procedure trades rigidity for renewal. Without it, late
  life calcifies. The cost is that what you remember stops being what you are
  committed to.
- **First-order consequence:** a life becomes three or four successive persons.
- **Second-order consequence:** pre-rebase selves write ratification letters,
  which amount to lobbying their own heirs.
- **Third-order consequence:** loyalty to your dead former self becomes a
  recognized virtue, and also a recognized pathology.
- **The story:** Maren, eight months past her rebase, remembers loving her wife
  Tuva completely and feels none of it. Tuva won't court her, because that
  would be seducing a stranger. Maren has to choose between ratifying out of
  fidelity to her former self and letting the marriage lapse honestly.
- **Form:** the former self's letter, addressed in second person, with the new
  self's present-tense marginal notes pressing against it.
- **Why this lens:** personal identity, with a feeling that comes straight out
  of the concept: being the heir to your own life.
- **Suspected prior art:** Parfit, in philosophy rather than fiction. Egan's
  "Learning to Be Me" (but that is about substrate, not values). The *Eternal
  Sunshine* family (but there the memories are erased, and here they are kept).
  Moderate distance.

## Card 2: Foundling Thoughts

- **Question:** is a belief yours if you can't say where it came from?
- **Novum:** humans are engineered with a *provenance sense*. Every belief
  carries a felt texture of its source, such as a voice, a page or a
  conclusion you reasoned your way to. A belief with no felt source, a
  *foundling*, registers as foreign, like an inserted thought.
- **Futurity:** F3. The boundary between self-originated and received thought
  is now perceptible, which changes what "a mind of one's own" means.
- **How it works:** tagging at memory encoding, inherited through the germline.
  Real source amnesia still happens, so foundlings still form.
- **First-order consequence:** lies and rumour lose much of their force.
- **Second-order consequence:** foundlings are medicalized. Clinics excise
  them, and *tracers* hunt down their sources.
- **Third-order consequence:** genuine originality feels exactly like a
  foundling, so inventors and artists are the people who can tolerate
  alienation from their own minds.
- **The story:** Odile is a tracer. Her client, the composer Bram, carries a
  foundling melody and will have it excised unless she traces it. Odile can
  find nothing. She can implant a false provenance, which would let him keep
  the melody but make it feel secondhand forever. The alternative is to tell
  him it is his, which in this culture sounds like a diagnosis.
- **Form:** Odile's case file, whose entries gradually lose their sourcing.
- **Why this lens:** knowledge and testimony, treated as a thought experiment
  with humane stakes. It has no villain, and the stakes are quiet ones.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "Liking What You See" (an elective
  perceptual change, in documentary form). Egan's "Axiomatic" (implanted
  belief). Neither is about where a belief came from. Moderate-to-far distance.
