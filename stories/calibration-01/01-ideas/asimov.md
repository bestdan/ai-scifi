# Idea cards: Asimov

Two cards, per the brief. Lens blind spots, stated up front: both cards are
built as puzzles. They have thin interiority and no sensory life yet, and both
assume the problem has a clean answer. Le Guin and Rajaniemi should push on the
people and the texture, and Lem on whether the answer should exist at all.

## Card 1: Meltwater Citizen

- **Novum:** You are a citizen of whichever watershed your body water comes from. An assayer reads the isotope ratio in your breath, and your vote, your taxes and your grave go with the reading.
- **Futurity:** F3. Place and kin are no longer facts about birth or address. They are a metabolic state that turns over every few weeks.
- **How it works:** Rain carries a deuterium and oxygen-18 signature set by altitude and distance from the sea (real isoscapes). Body water turns over with a half-life of about ten days. To stop vote-packing, carrying water across a basin line is banned. Food is exempt.
- **First-order consequence:** Migration is something you drink, and changing polity takes a fortnight.
- **Second-order consequence:** Hospitals source IV fluids by basin. Families split along cisterns, and homesickness is something you can measure.
- **Third-order consequence:** Belonging feels bodily. Exiles fast before festivals, and lovers argue over whose well they share.
- **The story:** An assayer has to certify an old man who left a high valley fifty years ago. That valley votes alone on a dam that would drown his family's graves, and he now reads as an up-valley citizen. His granddaughter wants the dam. The reader should spot the cause before the assayer does: he has eaten nothing but the valley's exported melons for a month. If she certifies him, every orchard becomes a vote market.
- **Why this lens:** It's a rule, and the case its makers didn't foresee: they wrote "food is exempt" without thinking that food is water.
- **Suspected prior art:** The Fremen water discipline in *Dune*, where water is kin. Isotope forensics in crime fiction. Neither turns isotopes into a franchise.

## Card 2: The Plain Wording Office

- **Novum:** For a century after your death, a frozen record of your preferences votes in your place. Each standing sees only the ballot text, and that text may use only words and concepts the standing knew when it was alive.
- **Futurity:** F3. Death is no longer the end of citizenship. A century in, the dead are the majority of the electorate.
- **How it works:** A standing is a long interview, compiled into a model that is not conscious and is forbidden any fact dated after its death. The Plain Wording Office certifies every ballot against each cohort's lexicon.
- **First-order consequence:** Policy carries a century of inertia.
- **Second-order consequence:** Campaigns court the dying, and ballot wording becomes the real seat of power.
- **Third-order consequence:** The living sit their own interviews the way earlier generations wrote wills, and they rehearse who they want to stay.
- **The story:** A wording clerk drafts the fourth referendum on ending the standings' vote, and her mother died last spring. The fair puzzle is why every past repeal has failed by almost exactly the share of dead voters. No standing is allowed to learn that it is dead, so each one votes as a living person who wants their vote to count later. Her legal fix is to quote the oath every standing heard while alive: "This standing will speak for you after you die." That tells them what they are without giving them any later fact, and it may end her mother's last voice.
- **Why this lens:** It starts from a society-wide force and the rule it hides. One clerk can bend it but can't stop it.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chesterton's "democracy of the dead" (an essay). Griefbot stories such as "Be Right Back". Neither is about who gets to vote or how the ballot is worded.
