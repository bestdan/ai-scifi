## The one thing
Cut the second half of the 3 a.m. derivation. Keep the arithmetic and "They had built the term around the latest she could possibly be." Let Hanne's "other reading" be the only gloss on what it means.

## Notes

1. **Scene "Seven" (3 a.m.):** The drafter's worry is right, but it is narrower than the whole scene. The arithmetic ("Hanne's twenty-five years and Hanne's season, to the day") is a clue, and she works it out because she has a reason to. The paragraph after it is the theorem delivered to the reader: "Anyone who wanted her judgment would have set the date in the middle of her, where she was known... Hedge widely enough and it came very close to keeping faith." Everything the reader needs is already in the line above it. The reader should do this inference; the story's pleasure is a fair-play solution, and here the narrator solves it for us. It also weakens Hanne later, because her "They picked a number they liked the look of" is meant to hold the doubt, and by then the story has argued the doubt away. If you cut the paragraph, keep "You came back from the last one liking eels." That line carries the argument better than the prose does.

2. **Day-count:** It earns its place because the headings are the term and its hidden margin, the nine days of room. A reader who notices the jump from Seven to Two feels the date arriving. That is the form serving the idea. It does not yet enact it: the count is the Parties' clock, and nothing in the form is about the unknown successor. Pell's "nine days to run" in scene one also does the first heading's work, so one of them is redundant. Fix by trimming Pell, not the heading.

3. **Cost on the page:** Paid. The 2041 heron is the best moment in the story: "'It says *look at herons*,' she said, and moved his hand, and erased it, the legs first." A margin note becomes an argument because of the clause, and the erasure has weight. Ros setting her eraser down and not being asked to pick it up is the same cost without a word spoken.

4. **Feeling from idea:** Mostly yes. The laugh at "It waved" is the strongest case: the first-night joke she has never laughed at gets a response because her values have already started to move. The story never says so. The crying at the boat ramp is an exception, a feeling attached to the premise rather than produced by it.

5. **Ending:** "In the raking light her hand stood up out of it anyway" leaves the trace unresolved. It is her hand and not an argument, and the story does not say whether the clause cares. That is the deepening. The closing "She had always written lightly" tells the reader the image's point; end on the snail-trail simile or on the squared stack.

6. **The unnecessary passage:** In the bakery, Mina's "Why does everybody have to?" and Hanne's "So we don't set... we'd all be our grandparents forever." Everyone in the room has lived the rebase. A child is a pretext for explaining it, and Hanne's real contribution ("Your next will like the bakery... Wasted on her") has already done the work. Cut the exchange and keep the seed-bun chewing.

## What to protect

- Pell's non-answer ("I should be failing the instrument if I let you think they took more care there than anywhere else").
- The unchanged joke, with only the listener altered.
- The heron.
- The tea going flat as the first sign of the turn.

## Blind spot disclosure

I favour the solved clue over momentum and distrust any scene that states the idea. Banks might want more of Pell's wit and less cutting; Watts might say the Parties' motive stays too benign.
