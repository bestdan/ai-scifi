# Stage 4 stress test: the Le Guin lens (the Anthropologist)

This is a critique through a lens built from Le Guin's fiction and essays. It is not Le Guin and doesn't speak for her.

**Blind spots.** I'm slow, and I favour gathering over mechanism. I may undervalue the treaty's game theory and over-reward warmth. I tip into instruction. Egan should check that the § 9 and turn-timing logic holds. Banks should check that the comedy has teeth.

## Notes

1. **[must-fix] Sallow Reach has no children, and the old are only Oyelaran.** The bible's town is keepers, retired keepers and spouses, and the one child exists only in a taboo line. Put a child on stage. At the bakery, a girl of about eight asks Oyelaran, "Auntie, what do you think about the stars?" Everyone freezes kindly. Oyelaran answers about bread. That one beat gives you the taboo, the town's tenderness and its cost. Also decide who the children are. Do keepers' children grow up cloistered from their parents' thoughts?

2. **[must-fix] Bisi is a saint.** He is "wrong about nothing", "treats it as weather" and is endlessly patient. That is a function: the ratifying spouse. Give him one want that costs someone. Suggestion: he likes the woman she is now and doesn't want her to turn. He has been cooking her favourite foods, not for her sake but to keep her. In one scene he breaks the town taboo and asks, "What do you think?" He apologises with a joke, and the joke is bad. Oyelaran should be slightly cruel back once. A small flaw makes them both people.

3. **[must-fix] The erasing night is sentimental as written.** A chorus of kind neighbours with erasers is the story clapping for itself. Earn it with friction in a single scene. One neighbour, a keeper herself, hesitates because erasing someone's twenty-two years is a violation. A pencilled heron sketch turns up, and the clerk has to rule whether a heron is argument. The neighbours turn the pages face-down so nobody reads them. Graphite grooves stay, so the kindness is incomplete and costs something. Cut the crowd to two or three named people.

4. **[should] Who pays is not yet on the page.** The visible cost is Oyelaran's notes, but she signed, saw the odd date and let it go. Let her own that, briefly. Then show the second payer: the spouse who can't ask what she thinks, and the keeper's children. A single image will do, such as Bisi eating alone at the table through the nine days. The star system at stake ought to hold something, even a mention of people on the other side of the "quiet" Volume, or the peace is paid for by nobody.

5. **[should] The invented words imply a culture only partly.** *Twice-met* and the taboo are strong. *Keeper* and *accounts* are plain. Add one or two coinages that show the turn's grammar: a word for one's own earlier self ("the before", used like a relative); "ratify" turned into everyday talk ("He ratified me over soup"); a courtesy phrase for the day someone turns. Use them without gloss. There is also the thing nobody can say: a sentence for "I will always" doesn't exist here. They say "I will, until the turn."

6. **[could] Names.** *Folasade/Sade* and *Adebisi/Bisi* are accurate Yoruba names and diminutives, and "Auntie" is respectful. But *Okafor* is Igbo, so give a line about how the families mixed. *Halloran-Ise* is a mixed double-barrel that reads as designed, and *Ise* may not be what you intend. Hyphenated surnames across the board read as a gesture. Vary: one plain single surname, one given name only. Keep the town mixed but not all Nigerian-coded.

## Must-fix summary

- Put a child and a second old person on stage.
- Give Bisi a self-interested flaw.
- Add friction to the erasing night and cut the crowd.
