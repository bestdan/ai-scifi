# Prior art and form: holography, language, AI, deep time

Research dossier for run-05. Seed: Maldacena's AdS/CFT holography, combined
with humanity's use of language (natural, mathematical, programming) and AI
to represent reality. Target feel: Egan, Liu Cixin's Three-Body trilogy,
Stephenson. Far future, long span, possibly great distances, 25,000 to
40,000+ words.

**Bottom line.** Holography as a novum is almost uncharted in fiction:
searches turned up no published SF that puts AdS/CFT at its core
(confidence medium; absence from search isn't absence). The territory
*next to it* is heavily charted. That covers language that rewrites minds
or reality, mathematics with local truth, the simulation reveal, physics as
a weapon, and emergent spacetime. The safe move is to treat the
**holographic dictionary as a translation problem with exact physics**,
not as a reveal that the world is fake. Part A ends with eight open angles.
Part B recommends either a braided two-strand structure or an edition-relay
of in-world documents, and gives five alternatives.

Confidence notes: dates and attributions below were checked by memory and
spot-checked by web search where uncertain. "Nearest I know of" means
exactly that.

## Part A: Prior-art map

### A1. Holography, AdS/CFT, emergent spacetime

Searches across physics-in-fiction reviews, Clarkesworld/Asimov's/Tor
listings and Egan's bibliography found no story that uses AdS/CFT or the
holographic principle as its central novum. The nearest neighbours:

- **Greg Egan, *Schild's Ladder* (2002).** Core move: spacetime and matter
  emerge from an underlying quantum graph governed by the "Sarumpaet
  rules"; an experiment creates a novo-vacuum, a region of different
  physics expanding at half lightspeed. Charted: *spacetime as emergent
  from a discrete informational substrate*; *a pocket of different physics
  that civilizations must learn to live beside*; *a far-future posthuman
  cast debating whether to destroy or study the new vacuum*.
- **Egan, *Permutation City* (1994).** Core move: Dust Theory, where any
  set of states, scattered in any order, constitutes a conscious history if
  the pattern is there; the Autoverse's Lambertians end up out-explaining
  their creators' universe. Charted: *reality as pattern independent of
  substrate*; *a simulated species whose own physics wins out over the
  physics of the world running it*. A 2020s blog essay ("The Dust
  Brothers", Bret Kerr, Substack) reads Dust Theory as a pre-echo of
  holography; that is a reader's interpretation, not Egan's stated intent.
- **Liu Cixin, *Death's End* (2010; Eng. 2016).** Core move: the "dual
  vector foil" collapses the Solar System into two dimensions, rendered as
  a vast flat painting. Charted: *a 3D world reduced to a 2D image that
  still holds all its information*. This is the closest popular image to
  "the bulk is encoded on a boundary", and a story using holography must
  not look like a flattening weapon.
- **Rudy Rucker, *Spacetime Donuts* (1981)** and ***Spaceland* (2002)**;
  **Edwin Abbott, *Flatland* (1884).** Dimensional-perception stories.
  Charted: *a being from n dimensions meeting n±1*.
- **Rucker and Bruce Sterling, "Colliding Branes" (Asimov's, 2009).**
  String-cosmology flavour (ekpyrotic universe ending), comic. Charted:
  *brane cosmology as apocalypse backdrop*.

Gap: the specific machinery of modern holography (the bulk/boundary
dictionary, entanglement wedge reconstruction, Ryu-Takayanagi surfaces,
holographic quantum error correction, the Page curve and "islands",
Hayden-Preskill retrieval, the fact that our universe is de Sitter and not
AdS) appears unused in fiction I can find.

### A2. Language as physics, language as weapon, language that rewrites minds

- **Samuel R. Delany, *Babel-17* (1966).** A language engineered as a
  weapon; speaking it reshapes thought and turns the speaker into a
  saboteur. Charted: *Sapir-Whorf weaponized*; *a poet-linguist protagonist
  decoding an enemy tongue*; *a language with no word for "I"*.
- **Neal Stephenson, *Snow Crash* (1992).** Sumerian as a neurolinguistic
  machine code; the nam-shub of Enki as a firmware patch on the brain;
  a digital virus that crashes hackers. Charted: *ancient language as
  executable code for minds*; *the Babel event as a security patch*.
- **China Miéville, *Embassytown* (2011).** The Ariekei speak Language,
  where words are referents, so they can't lie; humans teach them simile
  and metaphor and break them. Charted: *aliens whose language has no
  gap between word and world*; *introducing figurative speech as a
  civilizational upheaval*; *a language that only paired speakers can use*.
- **Ted Chiang, "Story of Your Life" (1998; film *Arrival*, 2016).**
  Learning Heptapod B, a semasiographic script, gives a simultaneous
  rather than sequential view of time, tied to the variational (Fermat's
  principle) view of physics. Charted: *learning an alien language changes
  your experience of time*; *the variational vs. causal description of the
  same physics as two worldviews*. That last one sits right next to
  bulk/boundary duality and is the most important neighbour on this map.
- **Chiang, "Understand" (1991).** A nootropic-made superintelligence
  designs a meta-language and finds a Gestalt "trigger" that kills a mind
  that perceives it. Charted: *a word that kills*; *two
  superintelligences dueling*.
- **Chiang, "Division by Zero" (1991).** A mathematician proves arithmetic
  inconsistent. Charted: *a proof that mathematics is broken, and its
  personal cost*.
- **Chiang, "Seventy-Two Letters" (2000).** Names, written as sequences of
  letters, animate golems and embryos; nomenclators as programmers.
  Charted: *names as programs that act on matter*.
- **Ursula K. Le Guin, "The Author of the Acacia Seeds..." (1974).**
  Scholarly papers on therolinguistics: ant poetry written in seeds,
  penguin kinetic verse, and a closing speculation about a geolinguist who
  might read the "poetry" of rocks. Charted: *language in non-human
  substrates read by future scholars*; *the scholarly-journal form*.
- **Jorge Luis Borges, "The Library of Babel" (1941)** and **"Tlön, Uqbar,
  Orbis Tertius" (1940).** The first: a universe of every possible book.
  The second: an invented world's encyclopedia gradually replaces reality.
  Charted: *the space of all texts as cosmos*; *a written description that
  overwrites the world it describes*. Tlön is the deepest prior art for
  "representation alters reality".
- **Liu Cixin, "The Poetry Cloud" (诗云, written 1997, pub. 2003).** A
  god-like being brute-forces every possible classical Chinese poem,
  consuming the Solar System to store them, and can't tell which are good.
  Charted: *exhaustive generation vs. meaning*; *an AI-like intellect that
  can generate everything and judge nothing*.
- **Ray Nayler, *The Mountain in the Sea* (2022).** Octopus language,
  an android observer, a corporate setting. Charted: *near-future
  first contact with an animal language mediated by AI*.
- **qntm, *There Is No Antimemetics Division* (2020 self-pub; revised
  Del Rey/Ballantine 2025)** and ***Ra* (serialized 2013 to 2014).**
  Antimemetics: ideas that resist being remembered. *Ra*: magic is a
  hidden engineered system with an exploitable interface. Charted: *ideas
  as predators*; *the laws of nature as an API*.
- **Charles Stross, the Laundry Files (2004 onward).** Computation and
  certain theorems open channels to other realities. Charted: *math as
  summoning*, and it's played for comic horror.

### A3. Mathematics that is the physics, or that is local

- **Egan, "Luminous" (1995) and "Dark Integers" (2007).** Arithmetic's
  truths are local; a "defect" separates our mathematics from another, and
  inhabitants of the other mathematics exist; the second story is a
  cold war across that boundary. Charted: *a boundary between two
  mathematics with civilizations on each side*; *computation as an act
  that moves the boundary*; *contact via proofs*. Any "boundary"
  story risks reading as Dark Integers with holography swapped in.
  Distance has to be in kind.
- **Neal Stephenson, *Anathem* (2008).** The Hylaean Theoric World (a
  Platonic realm) and polycosmism: cosmi are arranged in a directed graph,
  information flows "downward" from more Platonic ones, and consciousness
  navigates between narratives. Charted: *Platonism made literal*;
  *minds as navigators across a branching set of worlds*; *a monastic
  order of mathematicians as protagonists*.
- **Egan, *Diaspora* (1997).** Software citizens of polises; Kozuch
  theory (a six-dimensional wormhole-based unification); a journey through
  a 5D "macrosphere" chasing the Transmuters. Charted: *a far-future
  posthuman expedition drilling down through nested universes chasing
  an elder race*; *a unified theory as plot driver*.
- **Egan, *Orthogonal* trilogy (2011 to 2013), *Dichronauts* (2017),
  *The Book of All Skies* (2021).** Universes with different physics,
  discovered by the inhabitants doing science. Charted: *invented-physics
  worlds where the plot is discovering the rules*. Reviews note the
  recurring cost: the physics outruns the story.
- **Stanisław Lem, "The New Cosmogony" (in *A Perfect Vacuum*, 1971).**
  A fictional Nobel address: the laws of physics are the rules of a Game
  set by the earliest civilizations, which stay silent so as not to
  disturb it. Charted: *physics as engineered by elder intelligences*;
  *the Fermi paradox resolved by the law-makers' silence*. Liu's dark
  forest and Death's End both stand on this.
- **Greg Bear, *Moving Mars* (1993).** Physics' "descriptors" can be
  rewritten, so Mars is moved by editing the information that says where
  it is. Charted: *reality as editable record*.

### A4. AI as the reader or rewriter of reality

- **Lem, *His Master's Voice* (1968).** A neutrino signal, perhaps from a
  dead civilization, yields a substance ("Frog Eggs") and no
  understanding. Charted: *a message whose content may be physics itself,
  and which scientists can partly use but not read*.
- **Lem, *Solaris* (1961).** An ocean planet beyond comprehension.
  Charted: *contact that fails because cognition differs*.
- **Lem, *Golem XIV* (1981).** A military supercomputer lectures humanity
  on its limits, then ascends past communication. Charted: *the AI
  lecture*; *intellect tiers with communication breaking across them*.
- **Peter Watts, *Blindsight* (2006).** Scramblers and Rorschach are
  intelligent without consciousness; Rorschach learned human language as
  pattern without meaning. Charted: *language use without
  understanding*; *a narrator who models minds but has none of his own*.
- **Liu Cixin, *The Three-Body Problem* (2006; Eng. 2014), *The Dark
  Forest*, *Death's End*.** Sophons (protons unfolded and etched into
  computers) sabotage particle accelerators to lock human physics.
  *Death's End* adds lightspeed reduction (black domains), dimensional
  strikes, and the Returners, who reveal the universe's physics has been
  degraded by weapons. Yun Tianming's three fairy tales smuggle physics
  through layered metaphor. Charted: *aliens locking the frontier of
  science*; *physics laws as war damage*; *a message hidden in stories
  that only decoders read*; *the cosmos as a ruin of weaponized physics*.
- **Dan Simmons, *Hyperion* (1989) and *Fall of Hyperion*.** The TechnoCore
  AIs run on human neural networks via the farcaster web and are building
  an Ultimate Intelligence. Charted: *AIs secretly using humanity as
  substrate*; *an AI god built by AIs, competing across time*.
- **Vernor Vinge, *A Fire Upon the Deep* (1992).** Zones of Thought,
  where computational limits vary with galactic position; Powers in the
  Transcend; the Blight as a superintelligent archive-born pathogen.
  Charted: *physics that sets ceilings on mind*; *a dangerous
  intelligence recovered from an archive*.
- **Charles Stross, *Accelerando* (2005).** Three generations through the
  Singularity; Matrioshka brains; Economics 2.0; a wormhole router. Charted:
  *the Singularity told as family saga*; *posthumans turning the Solar
  System into computronium*.
- **Hannu Rajaniemi, Jean le Flambeur trilogy (2010 to 2014).** Sobornost
  upload copy-clans, quantum-crypto privacy (the gevulot), Fedorovist
  resurrection. Charted: *the far-future posthuman heist*; *copy-identity
  politics*.
- **Gregory Benford, *Timescape* (1980).** Tachyon signals to 1962 from a
  dying 1998. Charted: *physicists signalling the past*; *a two-era braid
  of scientists*.
- **Adrian Tchaikovsky, *Children of Time* (2015) and *Children of Memory*
  (2022).** Uplifted spiders develop science via a different sensorium;
  *Memory* turns on whether a pattern that acts sentient is sentient.
  Charted: *non-human science as alien epistemology*; *the Chinese room
  as plot*.
- **Stephenson, *Fall; or, Dodge in Hell* (2019).** A dead man's scanned
  brain becomes the god of a simulated afterlife. Charted: *the simulated
  afterlife built from mind scans*.
- **Benjamín Labatut, *When We Cease to Understand the World* (2020) and
  *The MANIAC* (2023).** Fictionalized lives of physicists and
  mathematicians; *MANIAC* ends with AlphaGo. Charted: *literary
  biography of science where understanding costs the understander*.

Recent (2020 to 2026) holographic-universe or AI-discovers-physics novels:
searches surfaced none that were central matches (confidence low; the
recent field is wide and poorly indexed). The ambient "simulation
hypothesis" is popular non-fiction (Vopson's *Reality Reloaded*, 2023),
which means readers will bring the simulation frame to anything here.

### A5. Charted variants (do not use as the core novum)

1. The universe is a simulation, revealed (also banned in the style guide).
2. Learning a language changes the learner's experience of time.
3. A word, glyph or sentence that crashes or kills minds.
4. Language as executable code for brains or matter.
5. Two mathematics or two physics with a war across the border.
6. Physics locked or degraded by a hostile intelligence.
7. A 3D world collapsed to a 2D image.
8. An elder intelligence that set the laws of physics.
9. An AI that understands everything and can't communicate it down.
10. Exhaustive generation of all texts, and the search for meaning in it.
11. A pocket of new physics spreading through the old.
12. Posthuman software citizens chasing an elder race through nested
    universes.
13. A message whose physics is usable but whose meaning stays sealed.

### A6. Open territory

Each angle is something none of the works above occupies, with the reason.

1. **The dictionary is exact, and the problem is translation anyway.** In
   AdS/CFT the bulk and boundary descriptions are physically equivalent;
   the "holographic dictionary" is literally the physicists' term. A story
   where neither side is the fake one, and the drama is that a fact easy
   to say in one language is monstrously nonlocal in the other. Distance
   from "Story of Your Life": Chiang's two views are both available to one
   mind; here the two descriptions are held by different kinds of mind
   (humans think in geometry, an AI thinks in boundary correlations), and
   no one can hold both. Distance from the simulation reveal: nothing is
   fake. Nobody on the map treats duality as a diplomatic relationship.
2. **Holographic quantum error correction as a model of memory and
   culture.** Since Almheiri, Dong and Harlow (2015), bulk interiors are
   understood as encoded redundantly in the boundary, so a bulk point
   survives the erasure of any one boundary region. A civilization, a
   person or a language could be built this way on purpose: a self that
   no single archive holds and no single loss deletes. Nearest: Egan's
   distributed selves; none use error-correction geometry as the
   *ethics* of who owns a memory.
3. **Entanglement wedges as territory.** Which boundary region you hold
   decides which interior you can reconstruct. Inheritance, sovereignty
   or custody fought over boundary regions, where controlling "the edge"
   means controlling what the interior is allowed to mean. Uncharted as
   far as I know (confidence medium).
4. **We live in de Sitter, and holography is cleanest in AdS.** The real
   physics has an open gap: our universe has a positive cosmological
   constant, and the exact duality is established only for negative
   curvature. A far-future project that builds an AdS region (a bubble
   with the other sign) to get an exact boundary, a place where
   description is perfect, is distinct from Schild's Ladder's novo-vacuum,
   which is an accident to be survived. Here the new vacuum is a *library*.
5. **Hayden-Preskill and the Page time as a deep-time postal system.** A
   black hole past its Page time returns information thrown in almost
   immediately, scrambled, to anyone holding the early radiation. A
   message that can be read only by whoever kept the first half of the
   hole's light, so the record of a civilization depends on a custody
   chain lasting longer than any language. I know no story using this
   (confidence medium-low). Egan's "The Planck Dive" (1998) sends
   observers into a black hole; it is about exploration, not retrieval.
6. **Language drift against invariant physics.** The physics is identical
   across a million years; the words for it rot. A story of
   re-translation, where each era's dictionary of the duality is a little
   wrong in a new way and the errors are the history. Le Guin's
   therolinguistics is the nearest (scholarship on alien texts), not
   physics; Foundation's Encyclopedia is the nearest structurally.
7. **The AI as a native speaker, not an oracle.** Golem XIV and Blindsight
   make the AI the incomprehensible superior. Open ground: an AI that is
   merely *fluent* in the boundary language, as a child is fluent, and
   bad at the human one, so humans are the experts on geometry and the
   AI on correlation, and both are needed for one sentence of physics.
8. **Proof, program and sentence as three dialects of one description.**
   The seed names three languages. No work on the map gives all three
   equal weight as competing representations of the same fact, with
   characters loyal to each (a poet, a mathematician, a programmer or an
   AI) and the payoff that the right representation is the one that
   survives transmission across time and distance, not the most true one.

## Part B: How long, multi-era SF holds together

For each: time's passage, character continuity, how hard ideas are placed,
and the reader's emotional through-line.

**Liu Cixin, Remembrance of Earth's Past (2006 to 2010).**
*Time:* Book 1 braids the Cultural Revolution (Ye Wenjie) with the present
(Wang Miao). Book 2 jumps two centuries through hibernation. *Death's End*
labels every section with a named era and year range (Crisis Era,
Deterrence Era, Broadcast Era, Bunker Era, Galaxy Era), so jumps never
confuse. *Continuity:* Cheng Xin hibernates repeatedly and wakes into each
era as an outsider, which gives the reader a guide who is as lost as they
are; Luo Ji and Thomas Wade recur as older figures. The Wallfacer device
(four people each given a secret plan, each matched with a Wallbreaker)
turns strategy into a puzzle-box with named contestants. *Ideas:* the
Three-Body VR game teaches orbital chaos as play; *Death's End* uses
excerpts from an in-world history, *A Past Outside of Time*, for the biggest
infodumps, which reads as primary source and lets the narration stay in
scene. Yun Tianming's fairy tales hide physics in story, and the decoding is
itself a set piece. *Emotion:* Cheng Xin's choices and their cost, and the
unconsummated bond with Yun Tianming across centuries.

**Neal Stephenson, *Anathem* (2008).**
*Time:* compressed into months; the deep time is in the backstory (millennia
of mathic history). *Continuity:* single first-person narrator, Erasmas,
naive about the outside world, so explanation is natural. *Ideas:* each
chapter opens with an entry from the in-world *Dictionary, 4th Edition*,
glossing one coined term before it matters; the biggest ideas (Platonism,
polycosmism) arrive as Socratic Dialogs between characters, and appendix
"calca" hold the heavy math for readers who want it. *Emotion:* Erasmas's
loyalty to his mentor Orolo and his group of friends.

**Greg Egan, *Diaspora* (1997).**
*Time:* chapters headed with place and date, spanning about a millennium;
the software citizens can slow their clock, so gaps of centuries pass
offstage without cost. *Continuity:* Yatima, born as software in the
opening chapter, persists; Paolo and others accompany. *Ideas:* the
hardest material (a mind's birth, "orphanogenesis") goes first, which loses
many readers but rewards the rest; later, physics comes as discovery by the
characters. *Emotion:* thin; carried by Yatima's curiosity and the losses
of those who choose not to go on.

**Neal Stephenson, *Cryptonomicon* (1999).**
*Time:* two strands, WWII and the late 1990s, alternate by chapter.
*Continuity:* the second strand's people are descendants of the first
(Randy Waterhouse, grandson of Lawrence); Enoch Root appears in both
apparently unaged, a hook that pulls readers forward. *Ideas:* the
cryptography lesson is given in the earlier strand where the stakes are
war, then pays off in the later one; Stephenson's digressions are tolerated
because they're funny. *Emotion:* each strand has its own; the bridge is
the reader assembling the gold and the code across both.

**David Mitchell, *Cloud Atlas* (2004).**
*Time:* six stories from the 1850s to a far future, nested: the first five
break off mid-story, the sixth is whole, then the five finish in reverse.
*Continuity:* a comet-shaped birthmark and each story's discovery of the
previous one as a found text (journal, letters, manuscript, film, a
recorded orison). *Ideas:* each era's voice is its own idiolect, so the
reader learns six dialects; the far-future one is hardest and sits in the
middle, after the reader is trained. *Emotion:* the reader's satisfaction
at each return, and the recurring theme of predation.

**Isaac Asimov, *Foundation* (fix-up, 1951; stories 1942 to 1950).**
*Time:* each story jumps decades; nobody but Hari Seldon recurs.
*Continuity:* institutional, not personal; Seldon recurs as a recorded
image in the Time Vault at each crisis, a dead voice predicting the
present. Excerpts from the *Encyclopedia Galactica* open sections and
summarize what happened between them. *Ideas:* psychohistory is barely
explained; it's asserted and then tested by crises, which is the trick: the
idea is the plot. *Emotion:* the puzzle of each crisis; low personal
investment, which is the known cost.

**Dan Simmons, *Hyperion* (1989).**
*Time:* the frame is days; each pilgrim's tale covers years and a different
era of the Hegemony. *Continuity:* the frame carries the cast; each tale
introduces one person in depth. *Ideas:* the worldbuilding is distributed
across tales, each in a different genre (war story, detective, epistolary),
so no single tale carries the whole load. *Emotion:* the Scholar's tale
(a daughter aging backward) carries the book.

**Ursula K. Le Guin, *Always Coming Home* (1985).**
*Time:* a far-future California people, the Kesh, with no single timeline.
*Continuity:* Stone Telling's life story, in three parts spread through the
book, is the one narrative line among poems, plays, recipes, maps and
ethnographer's notes by "Pandora", an authorial voice. *Ideas:* the
glossary and back matter carry definitions so the narrative can use terms
freely. *Emotion:* Stone Telling's choice between her mother's people and
her father's.

**Olaf Stapledon, *Last and First Men* (1930) and *Star Maker* (1937).**
*Time:* two billion years and eighteen human species in one book; *Star
Maker* covers the cosmos. Scale is managed by an accelerating timeline:
early eras get chapters, later ones get paragraphs, and time-scale charts
reset the reader's sense of size. *Continuity:* a narrator: the Last Men
speak through a 1930 writer's mind; in *Star Maker* a disembodied narrator
gathers companions (Bvalltu). *Ideas:* almost all summary; it's history
written as scripture. *Emotion:* awe and elegy, not character; the known
cost is that modern readers find it a lecture.

**Liu Cixin, "The Wandering Earth" (2000).**
*Time:* a first-person narrator from childhood to old age while Earth is
pushed out of orbit over generations. *Continuity:* one life; its milestones
(school, marriage, his parents' deaths) are pinned to planetary ones.
*Ideas:* the engines and the helium flash are explained as things a child
is taught, then lived. *Emotion:* the personal losses mapped onto the
planet's, and the mob turning on the engineers.

**Greg Egan, "Wang's Carpets" (1995; folded into *Diaspora*).**
*Time:* a single visit. *Ideas:* the whole story is one reveal: the
seaweed-like carpets on Orpheus are Wang-tile computers, and the life
they compute is the real alien. Exposition is the discovery, done by
characters in dialogue with stakes (is this life?). *Emotion:* the shock of
scale, and the explorers' decision not to intervene.

**Ted Chiang, "Exhalation" (2008).**
*Time:* the heat death of a universe compressed into one experiment and
one letter. *Continuity:* a single anatomist dissecting his own brain.
*Ideas:* the cosmology is delivered as a finding: thought runs on air
pressure, the pressure is equalizing, so everything will stop. *Emotion:*
the narrator's address to a future reader who will find his words.

### Lessons across these

- **Label the jumps.** Every successful era-spanner tells the reader where
  and when, in a header or a document, before the first line of the scene.
- **One continuous mind beats a cast that keeps restarting.** The books
  with the warmest reader response (Wandering Earth, Death's End,
  Exhalation) have a single carrier. Foundation and Stapledon prove you
  can go without, and show the price.
- **Push heavy exposition into in-world documents.** Encyclopedia
  excerpts, a history book, a dictionary, a recorded lecture: the reader
  accepts the infodump because the document is an object in the world.
- **Teach the hard idea where stakes are small, cash it where stakes are
  large.** Three-Body's game, Cryptonomicon's wartime strand.
- **Put the hardest dialect in the middle,** once the reader is trained
  (Cloud Atlas), or open with it only if the book is willing to lose
  readers (Diaspora).

## Part B synthesis: structural options for 25k to 40k words

The pipeline grades cold readers on **Who's who** and **Terms**, so each
option says how it keeps the cast small and the glossary learnable.

### Option 1: The Edition Relay

**Shape.** Five or six sections, each opening with an entry from
successive editions of an in-world dictionary of the correspondence,
centuries or millennia apart. Each section is a dramatized scene in that
era. The dictionary entries drift: a word's definition changes, an entry
is struck, a new one appears. The final edition is written by the AI.
**Strengths.** Puts invented terms on the page before they're used, as
Anathem does, so Terms grading is helped directly. The drift *is* the plot
(open angle 6). Deep time costs nothing: the gaps are between editions.
**Failure mode.** Foundation's coldness: a new cast every section, no one to
love. Fix by giving one lineage or one AI a recurring presence across
entries.
**Example.** Section 3 opens with the entry for a coined term in its
fourth-edition wording, then a scene where a translator's apprentice gets
it wrong in the way the fifth edition will correct.
**Tracking.** Each section has at most two or three named people; the
dictionary entries define every coinage; a recurring name (the editor's
office, or the AI) carries over.

### Option 2: The Braid (bulk and boundary)

**Shape.** Two strands in alternating sections, as in Cryptonomicon. One is
human, told in place and geometry (bodies, ships, distances); the other is
the AI's, told in correlations and text, centuries or light-years removed.
The reader slowly realizes the strands are two descriptions of the same
events. Structure enacts the duality, so no character has to lecture it.
**Strengths.** Form carries the novum (open angle 1). The reveal is
structural and fair: clues in each strand. Readers love assembling a braid.
**Failure mode.** One strand is far more interesting and readers skim the
other; or the AI strand's style is so alien it can't be read. Keep the AI
strand short and the human strand concrete.
**Example.** Human strand: a surveyor crosses a frozen moon to a station.
AI strand: a ledger of boundary updates in which the same crossing appears
as a pattern of correlations, which the reader learns to decode.
**Tracking.** Two to four names per strand, with a shared name that appears
in both as the hinge; strand headers (place and date vs. index number)
make which strand you're in obvious.

### Option 3: The Long Witness

**Shape.** One protagonist carried across eras by relativistic travel,
hibernation or being software (Cheng Xin, Yatima). Each era is a section
with a named header. The witness is an outsider in every era after the
first, so explanation is natural.
**Strengths.** The strongest emotional through-line on offer. Who's who
stays simple: one constant, plus a small new circle per era.
**Failure mode.** Passive protagonist, the tourist who watches history and
does nothing (a common complaint about Cheng Xin). Give the witness a
decision in each era with consequences in the next.
**Example.** A translator who refuses the AI's offer of a boundary-native
mind, travels outward at near lightspeed carrying a sealed message, and
returns three times to an Earth whose language has drifted.
**Tracking.** Era headers with dates; one recurring secondary figure (an
AI, a rival) to anchor relationships; terms learned alongside the witness.

### Option 4: The Frame and the Tales

**Shape.** A short frame (a hearing, a vigil, a crossing) in which three
to five people each tell one tale from a different era, as in Hyperion.
The tales are in different forms: testimony, a proof, a program's comment
log, a letter.
**Strengths.** Lets the three languages of the seed (natural,
mathematical, programming) each have a tale in its own form (open angle
8). Each tale can be tight and self-contained.
**Failure mode.** Anthology drift: the frame becomes a waiting room and the
whole doesn't add up. The frame must have its own stakes, decided at the
end by what the tales revealed.
**Example.** Four delegates at the edge of an engineered AdS bubble must
decide whether to seal it; each tells how their era came to understand
what it holds.
**Tracking.** The frame introduces all tellers with roles before any tale;
each tale introduces at most two new names. Tales from different eras
multiply terms, so share a core glossary of three to five terms across all
tales.

### Option 5: The Zoom (deep-time compression)

**Shape.** Stapledon pacing with Chiang focus. Brief summary passages
covering huge spans, interrupted by four or five fully dramatized close-up
scenes, with each acceleration marked (a millennium, then a million years).
**Strengths.** The only option that actually reaches millions of years in
40,000 words. Awe is its natural register, matching Liu.
**Failure mode.** Lecture. The summary passages become an essay, and the
close-ups feel like illustrations. Keep summaries short and concrete, a
single detail per age.
**Example.** A thousand-year paragraph showing how the word for the
boundary changes, then a full scene with two people at a black hole past
its Page time, waiting for an answer thrown in a million years earlier
(open angle 5).
**Tracking.** Strong on Terms (few needed), weak on Who's who unless the
close-ups share one carrier, such as a lineage name or a persistent AI. Use
dated section heads.

### Option 6: The Nested Return

**Shape.** Cloud Atlas in miniature. Three eras nested: each breaks off at
a crisis and the next deeper era begins, the innermost is told whole, then
the outer ones close in reverse. Each era finds the previous one as a
document.
**Strengths.** Mirrors holography's structure, where an interior is
reconstructed from what encloses it, and each closing is a payoff.
**Failure mode.** Hard at novella length: with four eras, each gets 6,000
to 8,000 words, and the halves feel thin. Limit it to three eras.
**Example.** Outer: a far-future archivist. Middle: an AI's first
translation. Inner: a human team building the first boundary. Closing back
out, each era's misunderstanding is corrected by the deeper one.
**Tracking.** Very hard on Who's who unless each era has one or two names
and the found document names who wrote it. Distinct voices per era help.

### Option 7: The Message in Transit

**Shape.** A single message (a proof, a program, a description of a bulk)
travels across interstellar distance and deep time. Each section is one
receiver: who catches it, what they make of it, what they add before
sending it on. The message itself, quoted in its changing form, is the
through-line.
**Strengths.** Takes Lem's *His Master's Voice* problem and Foundation's
relay while avoiding both: the message is understood a little more, and
differently, each time. Supports open angles 2, 5 and 6.
**Failure mode.** The message is the only character. Give it a sender
whose fate the reader wants to learn, revealed last.
**Example.** A human mathematician's last sentence, encoded holographically
so that any fragment rebuilds the whole, is received by an AI, then a far
colony, then something not human.
**Tracking.** Each receiver section introduces one or two names; the
message's text grows a few coined words each time, so the reader learns
them one at a time.

### Recommendation

Option 2 (the Braid) uses form to embody the duality and keeps the cast
small, and it is the least charted pairing of form and content. Option 1
(the Edition Relay) is the most robust for cold-reader grading on Terms and
fits language drift. Both can absorb a Long Witness (Option 3) as a
carrier, which fixes their main weakness. A combined plan: Braid
structure, each human section headed with a dictionary entry, one person or
AI present across the eras.
