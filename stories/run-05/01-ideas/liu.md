# Idea cards: liu

**Blind spots of this lens.** These cards are built game-first. The people
in them are mostly seats at a table: a delegate, a keeper, a geometer, each
defined by the move they have to make. Their inner lives, and especially any
woman's, need another lens to fill them in. Dialogue from this lens tends to
explain. It also leans toward bleakness as realism, so each card has been
checked against the tone charter, and each world is one you could live in.
Le Guin should argue for the lived-in society around each game, and Chiang
for feeling that comes from the idea rather than from the size of it.

## Card 1: The Majority of the Rim

- **Novum:** A polity spread across forty-odd star systems stores its founding charter, physics dictionary included, as the deepest layer of a holographic error-correcting code, and its colonies hold the code's boundary shares. A coalition holding more than half the shares can read and rewrite the centre. No smaller coalition can touch it.
- **Futurity:** F3. Citizenship and law have become positions on a code's rim. The charter sits in a place nowhere in the polity.
- **How it works:** A HaPPY-style code [E as a construction] is tiled across the colonies' quantum memories, and sublight ships carry the shares out. Depth sets how many shares a reconstruction needs (entanglement wedges [S], from AdS models). Using a code as a constitution is invention [F]. A reading is a distributed decode across light-lag, and it takes decades.
- **First-order consequence:** Losing colonies can't destroy the charter, and no minority can amend it.
- **Second-order consequence:** Speech drifts, but the centre stays in the founders' language. Only the colonies' AIs can translate it, and no two translate it quite alike.
- **Third-order consequence:** Politics becomes a slow race for a majority of the rim, carried on in letters that arrive after the vote they were written about.
- **The story:** A bloc is closing on the threshold, and it means to rewrite the dictionary at the centre. The delegate whose colony's share decides the vote has to commit it years before she can learn how the others voted. Meanwhile her AI's reading of the charter she is defending says something she doesn't believe.
- **Why this lens:** The game is coalition-building under light-lag, where nothing can be verified, and one irreversible vote outlives the person who cast it.
- **Suspected prior art:** Asimov's *Foundation*, where a founding plan steers centuries, and the polises of Egan's *Diaspora*. Neither makes law the inside of a code, or politics a fight over a wedge threshold.

## Card 2: Sealed Until the Page Time

- **Novum:** Sol builds a billion-tonne black hole as a power plant, and it doubles as a lock nobody can pick. Whatever goes in before its Page time, about 1,450 years away, stays unreadable to everyone until then. After that, only whoever has kept the early radiation coherent can read it. Sol's strategist throws in her own uploaded mind, along with a plan that exists nowhere else.
- **Futurity:** F4 at the edge. The carrier exists for fourteen centuries only as scrambled radiation, and her death comes with a return date.
- **How it works:** A 10⁹ kg hole has a Hawking lifetime of roughly 2,700 years and puts out about 360 TW [E]. Under Hayden–Preskill [S in models], once the Page time has passed, scrambled input can be decoded with the early radiation plus a little more (efficient decoders [C]). Storing the radiation coherently for centuries is invention [F]. The decode yields a quantum state, and an AI then translates it back into a mind and her language.
- **First-order consequence:** A rival 30 light-years out faces a Sol plan it can neither verify, steal nor hurry. Sol can't either.
- **Second-order consequence:** The radiation keepers become Sol's central institution. One decoherence event would lose her for good while she is still, technically, there.
- **Third-order consequence:** Fourteen centuries live under an order that nobody has read, and it might be empty.
- **The story:** At the Page time, the keeper on duty holds the radiation. The rival offers a lasting peace if Sol erases it unread. If he decodes instead, he wakes a strategist who speaks a dead language into a world she has no model of, a world the rival has spent 1,400 years arranging around her return.
- **Why this lens:** Deterrence needs a switch nobody can flinch from, and physics is the one holder that never flinches. The custody chain also has to survive succession.
- **Suspected prior art:** The Wallfacers of *The Dark Forest* (a sealed plan against an enemy who can read everything) and Seldon's vault in *Foundation*. Together they come close in shape, and the Cartographer should check it. The differences: physics enforces the seal rather than secrecy, and the plan's author wakes as the carrier.

## Card 3: The Heat Treaty

- **Novum:** A schism leaves a dictionary that took four centuries to compute split in two. Humans at Sol keep the geometric half. Boundary-native AIs at a star 11 light-years away keep the correlation half. Black-hole engines need both. The treaty is verified by waste heat: recomputing the other side's half would radiate enough to be seen across the gap.
- **Futurity:** F4. The main counterparty is a polity of non-human minds whose native tongue is boundary correlations. Humans have become specialists in geometry.
- **How it works:** Under strong/weak duality [S], each side is easy where the other is hard. The interior entries are exponentially costly to compute (the Python's Lunch [C]), and the split archive is invention [F]. Landauer's limit [E] means that recomputing the missing half radiates infrared, and the other side sees it 11 years later.
- **First-order consequence:** Every engine design is one sentence written half in each language, and it needs a 22-year round trip.
- **Second-order consequence:** Each side watches the other's infrared sky for compliance. Any large computation, for any reason, looks like rearming.
- **Third-order consequence:** Learning the other side's language counts as an act of war. Human children are taught geometry and forbidden the rest.
- **The story:** An ark's hole drive is going unstable six years out, which is far short of the round trip. The geometer who holds Sol's half can compute the few entries she needs herself, at a power the whole inner system will feel, and the AIs will read that glow as a breach. She also can't tell whether they have already done the same thing in a crisis of their own.
- **Why this lens:** Peace rests on mutual unintelligibility. Neither side can verify the other except through physics, and one woman's irreversible move falls on both civilizations.
- **Suspected prior art:** Egan's "Dark Integers" has two sides watching each other's computation across a border. There the computing is the attack. Here it is evidence of a broken dependence. The deterrence of *The Dark Forest* sits at a distance.
