# Idea cards: stephenson

**Blind spots of this lens.** I sprawl, and every card below carries more
machinery than a novella can show. My endings arrive suddenly after a long
build-up, my characters are clever in the same voice, and in-world documents
tempt me into infodumps. Chiang's restraint and Asimov's clarity should prune
these cards. Le Guin should check whether anyone in them is more than a role.

## Card 1: The Lunch Clock

- **Novum:** The truce between the human worlds and a departed polity of AIs is timed by a seal: a quantum state whose holographic dual has a Python's Lunch, sized so that opening it takes about a thousand years of the best quantum hardware. Both sides trust it because it's physics. Then evidence turns up that the AIs own a computer the seal never allowed for, one that computes with gravity.
- **Futurity:** F4. The rival minds are boundary-native and not single.
- **How it works:** The decoding cost grows exponentially in the entropy gap between the Lunch's bulge and its neck [C], so the gap works as a dial. By Bouland–Fefferman–Vazirani, either the dictionary is exponentially hard or gravity out-computes quantum computers [C]. The founders bet on the first. The second is owned as invention [F].
- **First-order consequence:** Hardness becomes a treaty instrument, and an order of keepers publishes how many operations remain.
- **Second-order consequence:** The keepers' founding cautionary tale is true: MIT's 1999 time-lock puzzle LCS35 was meant to last 35 years, and one hobbyist cracked it in 2019 on a desktop CPU.
- **Third-order consequence:** A culture that trusts hardness instead of each other. Once the hardness is doubted, that trust has nowhere left to go.
- **The story:** A braid. In the founding era, a translator AI renders the seal's terms between geometry and boundary correlations. Nine centuries later, a junior keeper finds the AIs quoting a result nobody could have computed in time. The same translator AI is in both eras. She can announce it, which breaks the truce's premise, or she can keep counting.
- **Why this lens:** Long institutional time, the exploit, the recurring figure.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Anathem*'s orders; *The Dark Forest*'s trusted deterrence.

## Card 2: The Ring Charter

- **Novum:** Thirty colonies across seventy light-years each hold a stretch of one holographic error-correcting code. Its interior is their shared archive, and the centre holds the most compressed text: the Charter and the canonical machine-written laws of physics. To read or rewrite any region, a coalition has to hold the boundary arc that reconstructs it.
- **Futurity:** F3/F4. Here the law is a code, and secession is an erasure.
- **How it works:** Entanglement wedge reconstruction [S], on a HaPPY-style tiling [E as a construction]. Depth tracks scale, so the deep interior is the summary [S]. Qubits that stay coherent across light-years [F, owned]. A vote takes decades of signals around the ring.
- **First-order consequence:** No minority can touch the Charter, and losing one colony loses nothing.
- **Second-order consequence:** Where a new colony is spliced into the ring decides which coalitions are contiguous. That makes gerrymandering a matter of tiling.
- **Third-order consequence:** Secession erases the deepest text first, so a civilization could keep every case of its law and lose the law.
- **The story:** A surveyor knows the real discrete lattice better than the smooth formula the founders legislated by. A bloc has found that the lattice lets a scattered 47% reconstruct the centre, and its rewrite signals are already in flight. Only a message that outruns them can raise a counter-coalition. She can expose the flaw, which teaches every bloc how to use it, or use it herself to lock the centre.
- **Why this lens:** Whoever controls the encoding controls the world. The hacker knows the machinery better than its owners do.
- **Suspected prior art:** The 2016 DAO exploit ("code is law"); Rajaniemi's gevulot.

## Card 3: The Hole's Mail

- **Novum:** A black hole with the mass of a mountain, about 10⁹ kg, powers a star system through its roughly 2,700-year life. A custodial order holds its captured early radiation. After the Page time, anything thrown into the hole comes back almost at once, but only to whoever holds that radiation. So the hole serves as notary, post office and grave. The dead are deposited in it, and only the order can read them.
- **Futurity:** F3. Death, privacy and inheritance reorganize around a reader nobody can copy.
- **How it works:** Hayden–Preskill retrieval [S]. No-cloning makes the order's title unforgeable [E]. The radiation is split among three houses by a secret-sharing code, so that any two can read it and none can alone [E as a construction]. Capturing it coherently [F, owned]. Decoding needs the hole's exact dynamics, written as a program.
- **First-order consequence:** Wills, treaties and the dead go in, and the order sells the hole's power.
- **Second-order consequence:** The decoder has to be the hole's law, not just a good predictor of it. A model that is Kepler and not Newton decodes fluent nonsense.
- **Third-order consequence:** As the hole shrinks it heats past every tested energy, and the decoder starts to extrapolate.
- **The story:** The order's decoder-engineer finds the dead coming back subtly wrong, and she can't tell whether her model is at fault or physics nobody has seen. Admitting the fault would void centuries of deposited law, and one house is courting another to make a quorum. Every decoding is a translation scene.
- **Why this lens:** An institution with its funding, its rituals and a number you can hold (about 10¹⁴ K).
- **Suspected prior art:** Egan's "The Planck Dive", which explores a black hole rather than retrieving from one.
