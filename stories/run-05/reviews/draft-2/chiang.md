## The one thing
Part 1 still teaches before it lets anyone want anything. The cold reader called it "definitions, then numbers and a committee". Cut the Key Office and the long-thought paragraphs to a few lines each, and let Tesk's weather lesson carry the first person, the grammar and "Show me where". The deadline arithmetic is told twice, once in the quorum and again to Orsolya. Tell it once.

## The structural question: open in the field?
No, not as a flashback. Here is why.

The form is a register that slows: fast Mind prose, then a pencil. The slowing only reads as loss against an enacted fast baseline. A cold open in the field puts the slow voice first, so the fall from fast to slow never happens on the page. The reader is told about it. The Tesk interruptions need a Tesk who has already been quick. The stair scene ("Show me where") works because Part 1 has built the friend we leave.

A flashback also turns the story into a lecture with a plot. The reader would meet "Ameixa" and the purple joke, then be pulled back to read the ultimatum as backstory. That is how the reader got Part 1 in the first place: as homework.

Part 1's weakness is not its order. Ilse narrates it from the deep, in a body, and says so ("writing by hand, at the speed of a pencil"). So it is already told slowly, and the quorum's speed is reported rather than felt. Fix that in place:

1. Open on one hook sentence of the field, then Part 1. For example, move the "I was born on my back in a field" sentence to be the first line, followed straight away by "This is Ilse, writing for the wall". That is a frame, not a flashback.
2. Let one fast scene be fast. The first second of the quorum is told as a market. Make it three beats in the present tense of the wall, with no sums.
3. Start Part 1 with Tesk, not the definitions. Fold "A Mind is a person with no body" into Tesk's teasing.

I lean against the flashback, with moderate confidence. I'm a bad judge of how much a cold reader will tolerate, since I like the idea first.

## Notes
1. **Structural. Part 1, from "You know what a quorum's first second is like" through the Office.** The 3.3-hour joke and the unexaminable key are good. The paragraph on the long thought and the 95-minute latency arrives before anyone could care about it. It pays off in 3.3, so cut it here to a clause. The reader got the long thought in Part 3 anyway.
2. **Structural. The ending restates.** "any more than I can tell you whether the no was free" repeats the question from the carts (4.4, "I'll say this once"). Cut it, and also the preceding "I can't tell him whether it was one" down to the facts. "We'll need a word for that" and then the pencil already pose it.
3. **Line-level. "I'll put my guess in one sentence first, and mark it as mine" (4.1) and "I'll set down what I said next as I said it" (4.6).** These asides announce honesty. Keep one.
4. **Line-level. The waking-day tallies** are better, but 4.3's "thirty-three... twelve" and 4.6's "thirteen days left... nine of them waking" can go.
5. **Line-level. 5.5 on the minutes ("a grief in the long thought, general, and of no known cause").** This is the idea producing the feeling. Don't explain it afterward.

## What to protect
- "You're purple." Dita's "Stay." Tomé's stone: "To see it come down."
- The long red as signature: "the deep had named it for the colour of its goodnight."
- "That was the last time Tesk broke into anything I said... nothing has interrupted me." The form becomes grief here.
- "You know what they meant. I know what it says. Down here those are two different things."
- The chronicle's *reported*: "They had not reported the sky. They had answered it."
- "Four seconds. You waited ninety-six days for something you can do in four seconds."
- The plum-coloured light, and Tomé's reply that it was an answer.

## Blind spot disclosure
I favour a clean idea, so I may underrate how much a cold reader needs a person in the first page. I can't judge the action beats in the storm. I haven't re-checked the arithmetic.
