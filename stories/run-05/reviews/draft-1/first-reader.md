# First Reader report: draft 1 ("To Be Read Slowly")

I read it once, straight through, up to the Drafter's notes.

## Where I got lost

1. **The opening letter and the first few pages.** I didn't know what "the wall", "a Mind", "the pocket" or "the deep" were. I read the rival's letter, then a speaker who says she is writing "for the wall", and I had to wait several pages to learn that the wall is where the narrator lives and the deep is a country at the bottom. I worked it out slowly from the heat arithmetic and the paragraph about the dissenters who went down. For the first page or two I was guessing.

2. **The dates and the clock ("W 312.0", "W 313.63", "12.04", "forty-nine of theirs").** I followed the shape: the day is short and their time is slow. I did not follow the figures. I took the sums on trust, and for the rest of the story I mostly just counted "days left" along with Ilse. I never worked out for myself why 1.63 years came to forty-nine days.

3. **The paragraph about the Key Office rule** ("I'd set the rest down as a joke about the Office, except that it turned out to be a habit of ours"). I couldn't tell what "the rest" referred to, or what the joke was. I understood only that nobody had ever tested the key. I got that from a later line, not from this one.

4. **The long thought, when it was first introduced.** I didn't know what it was or why it mattered. I took it as a kind of shared mood. It stayed vague until Ilse said "the long thought is you" in Part 3. I worked it out there, and from then on it landed.

5. **The first Tesk interruption** (the italic line in the middle of her sentence, "They won't want a place on the wall"). I didn't know whether it was a thought, a note or a voice. I worked it out when the box arrived and she explained the sixteen-minute delay. From Part 2 on, I liked it.

6. **Part 2, the box recording and the "six days and not a word" interruption.** I had to stop and work out who was answering what and when. Mostly I got there, but the delay arithmetic is hard to follow.

7. **In Part 3, why Pressa's charcoal proves anything.** I followed the idea that it spread everywhere at once and was cold, so it was the deep. I got lost in "three things that can make a wall shudder". I believed it without really following it.

8. **Part 4, Pressa's long paragraph about why they can't just prove it.** It is about what can and can't be proved. I took it as: they can only accept bounded proofs, and a proof doesn't end the asking. I'm not confident I have that right.

9. **Part 5, "The strike was rejected, three hundred and ninety to seventeen".** I didn't know what "strike" meant here. I guessed it meant the motion to send the key first. I worked that out from the sentence before it, in the earlier paragraphs.

10. **Part 5, "It also offered the way back down."** I couldn't tell what "it" was. I guessed it was the quorum, and worked that out from the paragraph before.

11. **The ending's reasoning about the cession.** The pocket's word is given only by a gathering, and the rival would see it coming. I got the gist. I'm not sure why this is supposed to satisfy a rival that wanted a proof, and the story itself says it doesn't.

## Where I got bored

- **Part 1, the paragraph that sets out the deadline sums** ("The rival transmits the key at W 321.0..."). I was waiting for somebody to walk into a room. I skimmed the figures.
- **Part 1, the three hours of waiting for the Key Office, and the seven causes.** I wanted to be in the deep. The list of causes in particular made me feel that I was being given homework.
- **Part 1, Tesk's lesson on the three kinds of weather.** I was interested at first, then impatient. I liked the line about "coldly" and "suddenly". The lesson ran longer than I wanted.
- **Part 3 and the start of Part 4, the long run of dates and "days left" tallies.** The count of waking days came round again and again. I started waiting for it.
- **Part 4, the day at the gathering.** I mostly enjoyed it. The long run of food and arguments in the middle was a bit of a wander, but Maria falling asleep mid-sentence rescued it.
- **Part 5, the five-step proof.** I read it and nodded. It did not move me. I was waiting for the people again.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **Dita and the field.** "You're purple." The children running, the shouting of "Desce!", her putting my hands flat on the ground and saying "Stay", and then naming her "Ameixa". That is where I started to like the book.
- **Tomé throwing the stone straight up on the walk home**, "To see it come down", and never looking bored by it.
- **"Aqui-fundo"**, the word with no counterpart on the wall, and the palm flat on the ground.
- **Tesk's red from below**: "it looks like a signature." The weather-reader knowing whose evening it was. That turned the whole setup over for me.
- **The patch misread.** Ilse thinks the sky is pointing at her house. Tomé tells her it is taking light from someone else. "You know what they meant. I know what it says."
- **Graça meeting her at the door**: "Ilse Varga is under a stone over the ridge. I helped dig it." Then, "She was his mother."
- **Bálint at his mother's grave, watching the red**: "She said she wanted to see who'd made it finish." And "You've got her face wrong at the mouth." This was the best thing in the book for me.
- **Maria do Céu falling asleep mid-sentence** and waking to finish it: "and never sent."
- **Rosa**: "So it's boring, but fast." And later arguing for the goat for an hour and then going back to looking bored.
- **"Four seconds."** The halt in the storm, after ninety-six days, was the worst moment of the story for me. I felt it.
- **Bálint's death**, found at his mother's stone, and Maria's one filled basket.
- **Graça at the grave: "Don't. Not in that face."**
- **The goat vote and the goat's abstention.** Delightful.
- **The pale plum-coloured light at the end**, and Tomé: "That was a reply." And that Ilse can't say.
- **The ending, pressing her palm flat on the ground and staying.**

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared most about **Graça, Bálint, Dita, Tomé and Rosa**. Next came **Pressa**, who I came to love once it was in the pond with its feet in the water and its hands black with charcoal. I cared about **Maria do Céu** a lot for the short time she was there. I cared about **Ilse** less than I did about the people around her. Early on she is clever and a little smug, and I only came round to her when she was clumsy and hungry in the field, and again at the grave when she couldn't say she was sorry.

I cared about **Tesk** too, and the quiet loss of it near the end hurt: no more long reds, and nobody answers when she says "I'll be here".

**Would I have kept reading past page 2?** Honestly, I'd have been on the edge. The first section is a lot of letters, sums and unfamiliar words, and I'm not an SF reader. What kept me was the market comparison, the voice ("to be read slowly", "the only way I have left"), and the promise that she was in a body now and writing by pencil. I would have kept going, but I wasn't hooked until Dita's face appeared in Part 2. If I'd put it down before that, I'd have missed everything I loved.

## The question I'm left with

Was the no free? Ilse says she doesn't know, and neither do I. The deep said no, but three hundred years of someone else's evenings may have written it. And the plum-coloured light at the end: was it a reply, or only the wall's mood turning, with the authors on the wall giving themselves a reason? I'm also left wondering whether the key will come, and whether, if it does, the people will have been right to stay.
