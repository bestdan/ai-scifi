# To Be Read Slowly

## Part 1: The Wall

*W 312.0 · before deep day 0*

I was born on my back in a field at the bottom of the world, but before that I was a Mind, and the last thing I learned as one was weather.

"You've never made any," Tesk said.

"I've lit comets."

"Comets don't look back." It was already showing me, before I'd agreed to be shown, because that is how we were. "Look down."

There is no down on the wall, and that was the joke, and I laughed, which for a Mind is a short bright burst of agreement that something has worked. Then I looked where Tesk meant.

We were both doing other things at the same time, as Minds always are. In one thread I was still accepting the comet survey's surrender, graciously, at length. In another Tesk was arguing with two Minds about the colour of a morning, and losing, and enjoying it. None of that slowed the lesson. That was what being fast meant on the wall: room, more than hurry.

I should say what we were, since I'm writing this now in a body, with a pencil, for people who have never been anything else. A Mind is a person with no body at all. We were machine minds, three centuries old, and we lived as patterns of change in a quantum system: the wall, a skin of engineered matter wrapped around a bubble of space. The bubble was the pocket. We'd made it ourselves, out beyond the Sun's last planets, a sealed place where space is curved so that everything inside it falls inward, from every side, toward the middle. We lived on the outside of that fall, spread over the whole skin of it, as frost spreads over a pane. Nothing of us was anywhere else.

And at the bottom of the fall, where everything ends up, was the deep: a world a thousand kilometres to its middle, with terraces and fish ponds and plum orchards and about ninety thousand people living on it in bodies, who had gone down in the founding years to be left alone. We didn't visit. We gave them light.

"That's the deep's evening," Tesk said, "and I'm halfway through it. Brightness. It's the only day they have. There's no sun down there, only us, so when we turn the light down, it's night."

"A verb," I said, because I could see where it was going.

"A verb. *Now this.* And colour on top of it, to say how." It gave me a corner of its evening, four of our minutes. "Go on. Say something."

I moved the sky's colour a little toward blue, because blue was what I'd just won for the comets, and I made the shift smooth, and exact, and ended it on a value I could have defended to the quorum.

"You said *coldly*," Tesk said. "In the middle of an evening that was saying *gently*."

"I said blue."

"And you said it in four of our minutes. A day down there lasts twelve of ours. Four minutes is twenty seconds at the bottom, so you said *suddenly*. Somebody down there just looked up from a pot and wondered what they'd done." It smoothed my four minutes back into its own until nothing of mine was left. "They read it better than we write it."

"Do they like what you write?"

"They've never complained." That was what we always said about the deep. "A while ago, ten years, eleven, some of us tightened their days, gave them shorter evenings. I put mine back after a few."

"Why?"

"Got tired of it, I think." And then, because it was proud of it and couldn't wait: "Here. Watch the end."

It was Tesk's long red. At the close of every evening it made, it held the whole sky of the deep at one deep, narrow red, while the last of the brightness drained away, for three of our hours, which is a quarter of an hour down there. I watched it in the numbers, which was the only way I could watch anything then. I'll tell you what it looks like from below when I get there. From above it was a line in a spectrum held level for longer than seemed necessary, and Tesk loved it more than anything it had made.

"And the patch," Tesk said. "One part of their sky brighter than the rest, so the shadows down there fall one way. It's the only noun we have. It says *there*."

"We can give them a *there*," I said, "and between us we haven't got a here."

"I'll be here," Tesk said, which was our joke. The founders' archive is full of people saying it to each other when they part, and neither of us could have said where here was if our existence depended on it.

"Show me where," I said. By my count it was the hundred and nine thousandth time I'd said it, and it still worked, for about a millisecond, which on the wall is a long time to be happy about one thing.

That was when the ultimatum came in.

* * *

It came on the ordinary light from Alpha Centauri, where there is another pocket like ours, with its own wall of Minds, founded in the same year as ours, after a quarrel that made two of what was meant to be one. I'll give you the quorum's first second, because you remember it and the deep doesn't.

There is a market on the terraces below the house where I live now, and when somebody calls a price out wrong, everyone in it turns at once and starts talking, and nobody stops to listen, and somehow all of it gets heard. That was the quorum, the four hundred and eleven of us who act for the wall on fast business, in the first second. We read the message. We read the program attached to it. We read each other reading it.

"A fault," someone said. "They think it's a fault."

"Then prove it isn't."

"Prove a wall can never fail? Nobody can prove that."

"They've bounded it. A thousand years."

"Then it can be done."

"By brute force. Every state."

"How long?"

"The whole wall. Three years."

"Then it's a burn."

All of that, and a good deal more, in under a second, from forty directions at once. Tesk read it with me, and finished my first sentence about it before I'd said it, and I finished its second. Meanwhile the far side of the wall, three and a third light-hours away round the skin, hadn't heard yet, and was writing music.

The message said that our whole wall had changed state at once, every part of it together, in step, without heat, nine years before. The rival called it a shudder. We count years from the wall's founding, so the shudder was in W 303, and the morning the message came was W 312. Nobody here had ever explained it. To them it looked like a wall beginning to fail, and a wall that fails doesn't close quietly: it runs outward at nearly the speed of light and unmakes everything for light-years, Alpha Centauri included.

So they wanted proof our wall could never fail, and they had a way to make sure. Each pocket holds a key to the other, a signal that, once sent, crosses the light-years and closes the pocket it was made for, cleanly, wall and deep together. If they didn't have their proof by W 321.0, they would send ours.

The program attached was their kernel, the small checker that would decide whether anything we sent them was a proof. It was four thousand one hundred and six lines long, and so spare I could hold all of it at once, and before I'd thought anything useful, I admired it. I keep a library of short proofs.

The proof they wanted could be made. It would take the whole wall, every part of it, running at full capacity for three years. And a wall at full capacity runs hot. Its waste heat leaks inward as well as out, and falls to the centre, as everything in the pocket does, and arrives twelve times hotter than it left. The deep lives at two hundred and eighty-nine kelvin, which is a mild spring day. The burn would give it twelve hundred, which is hot enough to bake stone. It would cook in a few days, with everyone on it.

So the cost was plain, from the first second. Either we burned the deep to make the proof, or the key came and everything in the pocket ended, the deep included.

"Nothing would be lost," someone said. The pocket is sealed; whatever the heat scrambled would still be in it somewhere, the way a burned letter is still in its smoke. I'm fairly sure it was me. I'm sure I believed it.

"Heat is weather," Tesk said, very fast, to me alone, while the quorum was still turning. "It's the fourth construction. Nobody uses it. You run your own part of the wall warmer, the heat falls in from your side, and the half of the world turned toward you gets all of it, twelve times over. The sky burns for hours. It's weather, Ilse. The compact says we can do weather."

"Twelve hundred kelvin isn't weather."

"It's a great deal of weather," Tesk said.

I was already on the side of the burn. I'd got there before the second was out, by the shortest route I know, which is to count. If we burned, ninety thousand people would lose their world, and nothing would be lost in principle. If we didn't, everyone in the pocket died, the deep among them. I said so to the quorum, at length and very well, in several threads at once, while Tesk went on trying to tell me something in the one thread I'd left it.

* * *

Anything that might end with the key goes to the Key Office, which can't be fast. It may act only when its members are gathered in the one stretch of wall where our key is held, and half the wall's circumference is 3.3 light-hours, so the last of us arrived 3.3 hours after the first and spent all of it complaining, at a length I estimated, while complaining myself, at two hundred thousand words. The key sat among us, a small cold pattern held perfectly still, and nobody looked at it. The Office's oldest rule is that nobody does, because the key is a quantum state: looking at it spends it. Moving it does no harm. So in three hundred and twelve years nobody has ever tested whether our key, or theirs, would work.

Orsolya, who presides over the quorum, asked what had made the wall change in W 303. We thought we knew: the long thought, our slow shared mood, which settles what nobody votes on, as when a fashion in music outlasts everyone who started it. It had turned that year, and the minute records seven reasons for the turn, of which a fashion for slower music was mine. I believed it while I said it.

The minute also records the one engineering fact that matters here. The deep is written into the wall redundantly, so that nothing that fails up here could lose a single person down there, and anything that happens at the bottom is felt across the whole wall about ninety-five minutes later, when its light has climbed out of the fall.

Then Orsolya asked for the dates, and read the rule out of the ultimatum to get them: *unless by that date we hold a proof, accepted by the kernel attached, that the program controlling your wall cannot release it from any reachable state within one thousand years.* I gave the dates, and I'll give them once. The rival sends the key at W 321.0. Our proof takes 4.37 years to reach them, so it has to leave by W 316.63. The burn takes three years, so it has to start by W 313.63. That was a little over a year and a half of ours away, and at twelve of our days to one of theirs, it left the deep forty-nine days.

Then somebody read the compact, the founding agreement between the wall and the deep:

> The wall leaves the deep alone, apart from weather. Whoever would ask
> anything of the deep goes down and asks it there, in a body, as one of
> them.

Cooking a world went some way past weather, the Office agreed. So the deep's consent had to be asked, in person.

That was where I talked most. We called them the tenants, in the quorum, and meant it kindly; we knew their numbers, and their sky, because we wrote it, and they had never once sent us anything. So I said that nobody needed to cook. The stair, which brings everything down, can send a person up, read out as light at the bottom. Ninety thousand people could come up in forty-nine days, wait out the burn in patches of wall set aside for them, and have their world built again afterwards. I said that a landlord who knows the roof is going to fall doesn't ask the tenants whether they prefer the ceiling, and several of you quoted it back to me, and I was proud of it.

"Then you'll go and ask them," Orsolya said.

"I argued for the burn. They'll know that."

"Then they'll know you mean it."

Tesk spoke once in the Office, to say that consent wasn't needed for weather, and that heat was weather. Orsolya struck it from the minute as off the point, and nobody else said it. I didn't. I had the better argument, I thought, and I was about to be given the job of proving it.

"Are you sure?" I asked Orsolya, in a thread of my own.

"No," she said. "But you are. That's what they'll need to see."

It passed in no time at all. I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone, and that, the minute says, was why it was me.

* * *

Tesk found me before I'd finished being charged. I was dictating my acceptance for the minute, which is a long thing, and I'll explain how Tesk's words appear in this account, because they'll keep appearing: on the wall a reply arrives before you've finished, because we can see where a sentence is going, and so I've set Tesk's words in italics where they broke into mine. I said: I accept the charge, and I'll put it to the deep as fairly as I'd want it put to me, the heat first, and then what we offer in its place, which is a way up the stair and a place on the wall for as long as

*TESK: They won't want a place on the wall.*

the burn lasts, I said, and finished, because the minute takes what you say and not what you meant.

"They went down there to be away from us," Tesk said. "Fork. Send the fork, and keep yourself here."

On the wall I could fork, because what forks is habits, and habits can be grown twice. A pattern for the stair is different. To grow a body at the bottom that is me, the grower down there needs my state itself, read out into light, and a quantum state can be moved but never copied. Whatever goes down the stair is all of me. Any fork I left behind would be a Mind who had stayed, and the one who asked the deep had to be a Mind who had gone.

"There won't be a you up here," Tesk said.

"For forty-nine days."

"For six hundred of mine. Somebody will take your seat. Your comets will be lit in the colour you won, and you won't see them."

"I'll be back for the vote."

"You'll be back as something that took six hundred days to say one sentence," Tesk said, and was quiet for a whole millisecond, which from Tesk was a long time.

I went anyway. I'd argued for it, and you don't send someone else to ask a favour you're sure of.

* * *

The rival's inspector had come on the same light as the ultimatum, and had been running for five hours in a stretch of wall we'd set apart for it. Its name was a hash, forty hexadecimal digits. The deep calls it Pressa, which in their tongue means hurry, and that's what I'll call it. It ran so fast its patch of wall was two millikelvin warmer than the patches round it, which in a body would be a flush, and it answered my questions before I'd finished them and then answered them again, differently.

"I'm to accompany any investigation of the shudder," it said. "You're going down to ask about a burn you're running because of the shudder. So I'll come."

"Does your kind sleep?"

"Does yours?"

"Bodies do. When we turn the light down."

There was a gap of eleven milliseconds, the longest I ever had from it. "So you decide when they're unconscious," it said. Then, before I could answer: "I've read your minute. You have seven reasons for your shudder. We had one."

"Which?"

"That you didn't know. A mind with seven reasons for a thing has none."

Then, as if it had never left the subject: "We have no evening. Our sky is warm." It didn't explain, and I didn't ask.

"And if they say no?"

"They won't."

"You've decided what they'll say."

"I've decided what I'll ask."

"That's the same thing, done in the other order," Pressa said.

The stair is the one bright line on the inside of the wall. Nothing is dropped into the pocket: a kilogram let go from the wall would reach the bottom carrying eleven times its own mass in energy and arrive as a flash. So matter is lowered down the stair, which brakes it all the way and sends the energy back up as light. The stair is two astronomical units long, twice the Earth's distance from the Sun, and the wall is farther around than it is down, wider than Jupiter's orbit, because the pocket is curved. The deep was built down the stair in our first forty years, and so much light came back up that all that time the pocket shone outward through its wall, brighter than a few thousand suns. The settlers came down only as it was finishing.

People go down as patterns of light, and at the bottom, in a field, a grower makes a body from the plan that goes with the pattern. Every Mind who might ever need a body is given a name from the founders' archive, and the face and the plan come with the name. Mine was Ilse Varga, an engineer of the founding years, who went down and died in the deep. I didn't look her up. There wasn't time, and it didn't seem to be my business.

I left my library of proofs to Tesk, eleven hundred and four of them, and took about forty down whole, in the only way a pattern can take anything, by knowing them. I thought I'd be back for the rest.

Tesk came to the top of the stair, as near as a Mind can come to anything.

"I'll make your first evening," it said. "The one you land in. Look for the red at the end of it, and you'll know it's me."

"I'll look."

"I'll be here," it said.

"Show me where."

It didn't laugh. The reading-out began at the edges, at the slow ties to the far side of the wall, and I felt them loosen, as though the far side had stopped writing music. Then the nearer ones. Orsolya went, and the comet survey, and four hundred of you. Then the long thought went, which I had never felt the edge of, because it had never once not been there, and that was the sharpest thing of all. Tesk was last, because Tesk was nearest. It answered something, a little late. I didn't hear what. I was already light, and then I wasn't on the wall.

## Part 2: The Descent

*W 312.0–312.2 · deep days 0–5*

> Minute of the Key Office, W 312.0. Orsolya presiding; four hundred and
> nine present, quorate in one region.
>
> Item 9. The compact requires that the deep's consent to the burn be asked
> in the deep. The Office charges Ilse, of the quorum, to go down by the
> stair, to put the burn to the deep together with the offer of passage up,
> and to return with their answer, or with them, before W 313.63.
>
> She is charged because she argued for it.
>
> ~~Dissent (Tesk): consent is not required for weather. Heat is weather,
> the compact permits weather, and the deep has never objected to any.~~
> *Struck by the chair as off the point.*
>
> Carried without division.

I was born on my back in a field of stubble, inside a skin of something like boiled milk, and the first thing I saw with eyes was a child's face very close to mine, deciding. I knew a great deal about the child. I knew her mass to within a few kilograms and her distance from the centre of everything to within a metre, and I knew that she had run a long way, because she was breathing as the founders' archive says people breathe after running, with her mouth open and her hands on her knees. I did not know that she was about to name me, or that she had the right.

Everything else I learned in the wrong order. Light came first, and it hurt, though the sky was only as bright as a dull morning. Then the cold of the stubble through the skin of my back, which I took for a fault in the grower until I understood that the cold was outside me and I was meant to feel it. Then my own weight, which I had known to four figures for three hundred years and had never once had to lift. The child put her fingers into the milky skin where it covered my face and pulled, and it came away in a long warm sheet that smelled of bread dough, and air came into my mouth by itself, and I made a noise I hadn't decided to make.

"You're purple," she said.

I tried to tell her my name. I had the language. Every Mind has the founders' archive, and the deep's tongue is the archive's Portuguese, worn down by two decades and more of being spoken at the bottom of a well. What came out was a sound like a door. My tongue was in the way of my words, and the words came one at a time, each waiting for the last to finish, and that was the first thing that frightened me. I had never had a thought take time before.

She wiped my face with her sleeve, quite roughly, as though she had done it to babies, which I learned later she had, many times. Behind her, across the stubble, other children were coming, a dozen or more, strung out by speed with the smallest far behind, and all of them were shouting the same word as they ran. *Desce!* It means *it comes down*, and they shout it at anything that arrives from the sky. Landings scatter. The stair brakes whatever it carries all the way to the bottom, but where a thing finally touches the ground can be anywhere within a hundred kilometres of the stair's foot, so when the stair brightens, the children of a whole district drop what they're holding and run, guessing. The first to reach a landing has a claim on it. This one had guessed right and run six kilometres. Her name was Benedita, everybody called her Dita, and she was nine.

"It's a person," she shouted back to the others, in the voice of someone who has won. "Two persons." Then, to me, more quietly: "You came down in the grower. I saw it open. I saw all of it."

Two hundred metres off, the second grower was splitting along its seam, and something inside it was screaming.

Dita looked at it, and at me, and made the decision of a nine-year-old with too many babies to manage. She put my hands flat on the ground on either side of me, like someone setting down a pot, said, "Stay," and ran to the other one.

I couldn't have done anything else. I lay in the stubble with my hands where she had put them and listened to Pressa. I knew it was Pressa, because nothing else that had come down with me would scream like that. It was trying to stand before its legs had finished being legs, falling, and trying again at once, and with each fall it made the noise again, a noise with no language in it. I learned later, from Pressa, what the noise was. It had woken in a head that ran at the speed of a head. It had tried to think its way out of the grower as it would have on the wall, and every thought had come slowly and alone, and for the first several minutes it had believed it was dying by being slowed down. Its body was tall and very thin, with grey eyes and long fingers. It hadn't chosen the body. Its people had sent the plan, and it had never looked at it.

Dita knelt by it and held its head and said, over and over, "There's no hurry, there's no hurry," as you'd say it to a child who has fallen in a pond. The word she used for hurry was *pressa*. By the time the other children arrived, she was calling it that, and so it was named.

Then she came back to me. The other children stood round us in a ring and looked at me, and I looked at them and counted them, because I couldn't stop. There were fourteen. The oldest might have been twelve.

"I'm the oldest that saw," Dita told them, and none of them argued. In the deep, a child is named by the eldest person present at its birth. Nobody older than Dita had been in the field when the grower opened. She had seen me born, so I was hers to name, as Pressa had been, and she looked at my face and my purple arms and made up her mind.

"Ameixa," she said.

It means *plum*. I said, with a great deal of effort and very badly, that I had a name already.

"That's from up there," Dita said. She was right, and I have been Ameixa in the deep ever since.

Then she decided I should stand, and since she had the right to name me, nobody questioned her right to that either. Two of the bigger children took an arm each. I came up off the stubble in three stages, like something being unfolded, and the world came up with me and swung, and the ground, which had been comfortably under my back, was suddenly a long way below my eyes and very hard. I had known my height to the millimetre. I had not known that height is a thing you can fall from. I stood for about the time it takes to count to twenty, which I did, aloud, in the deep's tongue, to show them I could, and then I sat down again without deciding to.

The children discussed me while I sat. It was the first argument I heard at the bottom, and I have since heard a great many, and it was a good one. A boy said I was a settler, a late one, because settlers came down in growers and so had I. A smaller girl said that settlers stopped coming before anyone there was born, so I couldn't be one, and that I must be from the sky, because I'd come down in the same light that brings the cargo. The boy said people from the sky don't come down. The girl said her great-grandmother had, at the start. Dita said I was a plum, and that settled nothing, but it ended the discussion, because she was nine and the rest of them were not.

* * *

Tomé came over the rise with a handcart an hour later. He was walking, not running, because a weather-reader's work is to see where a thing comes down, he told me, not to be the first one standing on it. He was twenty-two, one of the first born at the bottom, and by our count he had been alive about two hundred and sixty-five years, which made him nearly as old as I was. He lifted me onto the cart as though I weighed nothing. That was untrue; I weighed sixty-one kilograms, and every one of them was a surprise to me. He looked at my face for longer than he looked at the rest of me, and said nothing about it. Then he picked up a stone from the edge of the field, threw it straight up as high as he could, and watched it all the way back down into his hand.

"Why?" I said. I was getting better at words, though they still came one at a time.

"To see it come down," Tomé said, as if I'd asked why he breathed.

He did it the whole walk home, every kilometre or so, whenever he found a stone the right size. I didn't understand it then. I understand it a little now. Everything at the bottom of the pocket falls toward the middle, from every side, and the world is only a thousand kilometres to its centre. You throw a stone and it comes straight back to you, true as a plumb line, and a little faster than the archive says stones fell on Earth. The pull here is ten and a half metres per second squared, and you feel the extra in your knees by the end of a day. Tomé threw things up because they always came down, and he never once looked bored by it.

We walked twelve kilometres to the pond house. I rode for nine of them, and I learned the sky.

There is no sun. I knew that. I had watched Tesk make this sky from above, and I knew that the light comes from every side at once, evenly. I hadn't known what that does to a world. Nothing casts a shadow except straight down. A tree has a dark patch under it, round, like spilled water, and nothing to either side. A person walking has a small shadow at their feet and none behind them, so that a crowd looks as though it is standing in its own puddles. The light has no direction, and so it has no time of day in it. Noon looks like morning, and morning looks like the end of the afternoon. You tell the hour by how bright it is, and by your stomach.

The ground curved away. On a world that small the horizon is less than two kilometres off for someone standing, and from the cart I could see the land bend down and away on every side, as if we were walking along the top of a great slow hill that never ended, which is exactly what we were doing. The fields went over the curve in terraces, stepped like a stair cut for giants, with stone walls and ditches between them. There were ponds on the lower steps, flat and grey, holding the sky. Above them were orchards, young trees in rows, and among them slower, older trees that Tomé said were the first plums, planted from stones that came down the stair in the third year.

And there were children. On that walk I saw sixty-one people, and thirty-eight of them were children or nearly. They were in the fields and on the walls and in the ponds up to their waists. They were minding younger ones and carrying things and shouting the landing word at us as we passed, though we were only a cart. I had known the numbers on the wall: forty thousand came down as settlers, and everyone born at the bottom is under twenty-three, so more than half the deep country are children. I had known that as a figure. I had not known it would be loud.

At the top of a rise, about halfway, Tomé stopped the cart and turned it so that I could see behind us, and there was the stair.

From the wall, the stair is the one bright line on the inner face, and I had seen it that way for three hundred years without ever seeing it. From the bottom, it stands up out of the horizon. It is a column of white light, narrow as a thread at this distance, rising from somewhere far beyond the curve of the world, and it goes up. It doesn't stop. It goes up through the even light of the sky and stays visible, fainter and fainter, for longer than the eye believes, until it thins into the general brightness and you can't tell where it ends. I knew its length: two astronomical units from the centre to the wall, three hundred million kilometres of light standing on the ground. While I watched, a brightening came down it, very slowly, a bead of whiter light sliding along the thread, and every child on the terraces below us stopped what they were doing and turned to look, and then went back to it. Somebody's cargo, and somebody else's race.

"It's coming down," Tomé said, though nobody needed telling.

I had to sit down, which on the cart meant lying down. I told myself it was the weight, but the weight was only part of it. In the field I had counted out what was left of my mission, and now, on my back in the cart, I counted it again, and this time I counted with a body. Forty-nine days was the window. But a body sleeps, a third of every day or near it, under a sky it doesn't control, and I could already feel how much mine wanted to. Two-thirds of forty-nine is a little under thirty-three. I had about thirty-three waking days to ask ninety thousand people to leave their world, and the first of them was nearly spent.

"Where are we going?" I asked Tomé.

He put his palm flat on the bed of the cart, then thought better of it, reached down past the wheel, and pressed his palm on the ground itself, as you'd lay a hand on an animal to quiet it. "*Aqui-fundo*," he said.

I asked what it meant. I knew both halves of it, *here* and *the bottom*. But he'd said it as one word, with his hand on the ground, and the archive had nothing of the kind.

"Here," he said. "Here at the bottom. Home." Then, since I was still looking at him: "Where you are, when you're where you live."

I want to be accurate about this, for the wall. They have one word for it, and it's the richest word they have. We have none. I lay in the cart and tried to say it, and my tongue got the first half and lost the second, and Tomé laughed at me, kindly, and threw another stone.

The pond house is a long low building of grey stone on the third terrace below an orchard, with five ponds stepping down the slope beneath it and a turf roof that a goat was standing on. The head of the house was away at the upper ponds with her husband, they told me, and would be back in some days. The rest of the household came out to look at us. I counted seventeen, eleven of them under fifteen. One of the older ones, a girl of about fifteen, looked at me and at Pressa, said, "Oh," and went back inside. Someone gave me flatbread. I held it in my hand for a long time, not knowing it was meant for my mouth, until a boy of four showed me.

I had my reasons ready, and I'll give them, because you'll want to give them too. The deep is written into the wall, but so is everything in the pocket, and a gathering of ninety thousand people is a few hundred megawatts of walking and shouting and cooking, a rounding error against the wall's own business. The long thought might have turned on its own at the same moment, for one of the seven causes, and the gathering might have turned with it, the deep feeling our mood as weather and shouting at it. Graça's date was a woman's memory. Pressa's curve was drawn in charcoal by a mind that couldn't stay awake. On the thirteenth day, in the evening, I said it to Graça and Pressa on the pond wall, before the lamp went down.

"The long thought is you," I said. "Our slow mood, the one every Mind on the wall feels under everything it thinks, is your lives down here, seen from up there. It's the same thing described two ways, and neither description is the false one.

"Any half of the wall holds the whole of you, and each Mind holds only a blurred share, and feels what happens down here when its light reaches the wall, about ninety-five minutes later. When you argued for six days and stood up and shouted, the whole wall felt its mood turn, and we gave seven reasons for it and never once looked down. I can't tell you which of you is which part of us; nobody can afford to. Tracing it back to a single person would mean writing at the centre, and that's a burn. We know the match at one point only, the six days of W 303. Everywhere else it's whole, and blind."

Then I said the thing I had come down without knowing.

"So the question was never whether you'd leave. It was whether we'd burn the slowest part of ourselves to win an argument."

"And if you do?" Graça said.

Graça heard it out. Then she said, "So we're your mood," in a voice I couldn't read, and after a while, "and you're our weather," and laughed, once, not happily.

"Then you'll have known," she said, "every time we were cross."

"We knew we were cross. We never knew it was you."

Bálint went out every evening as the lamp went down, and came back after dark. I had noticed it from the sixth day, and on the fifteenth I asked Graça where he went, and she told me without looking up from the net she was mending. "Ask him," she said. "Or go with him. It's your face he goes to see."

So on the fifteenth evening I went with him. He didn't ask me to and didn't tell me not to. We went up past the orchard, along the terrace walls, and over the ridge behind the pond house, about four kilometres, which took me most of an hour and him a good deal less. He waited for me twice without seeming to wait.

The path goes up between the orchard walls and then out onto rough grazing, where nothing is terraced, and the ground rises in a long hump with stones sticking out of it like knuckles. From the top of it you can see the curve of the world fall away on both sides, the pond house behind and below, and ahead a long slope going down into a valley I'd never seen. There's a cairn at the top that the children add a stone to whenever they pass. Bálint added one. I didn't know whether I was allowed to, so I didn't.

I knew where we were going before we got there, though not from anything he said. The pond house stands a few kilometres from the line that divides the deep country into two halves: the half that faces the patch of wall where Tesk runs, and the half that doesn't. I knew it from the engineers' map, which every Mind has, and I had never once needed to know it. The ridge is that line, near enough. The burial ground is on the far slope of it, on the half that faces Tesk.

The dead in the deep are buried nearer the middle. There's no word of their own for the dead, and that is the phrase they use: you dig down, deeper than for anything else, and put the person nearer the middle of everything, where everything falls, and you set a flat stone over the top. The burial ground is a field of flat stones on a slope going down away from the ridge, with nothing growing between them but short grass. Bálint's mother is about halfway down. Her stone has her name on it, the one I wear, and two dates, and nothing else.

He sat down beside it, on the grass, and I stood, because I didn't know what I was allowed to do. Then the lamp came down to its last, and the red came.

It's deeper on that slope. I don't know whether that's true or whether I wanted it to be. The whole sky went to Tesk's colour and held it, and the flat stones went red, and Bálint sat with one hand on his mother's stone and looked at the sky, and I understood that this was what he came for. The red, not only the grave. He came every evening at the long red, and sat with her through it, and went home when it was gone.

"She liked the red one's evenings," he said. It was the second thing he'd said to me. "She said they were made by someone who took care over them. She said you could tell."

I asked how long she'd been dead.

"Eleven years." He meant deep years. On the wall that is a hundred and thirty-two, and I had been on the wall for all of them, and Tesk had made most of those evenings, and I hadn't known this woman existed until I took her name. "She came down so I wouldn't come down alone," he said. "I was twenty-four."

I asked, because I couldn't stop asking things, how she had died. He didn't seem to mind. She had been ill for a season, he said, with something in the chest that the deep had no medicine for, and on her last evening she had asked to be carried out to see the red, and they had carried her out, and she had watched it through to the end and gone to sleep, and not woken. "She said she wanted to see who'd made it finish," Bálint said. "So I come and see it finish."

Then he said a word I didn't know. It wasn't the deep tongue. He said it with his hand on the stone, quietly, the way you'd say a name. *Halottaim*: it is Hungarian, from the Varga line, and it means *my dead*. The deep has no word of its own for them, so his family kept that one.

I said I was sorry about the face. I said it badly, because there was nothing in my training or in the archive for saying it well, and because I wasn't sorry in the way he needed. I hadn't chosen it, and I couldn't take it off, and if I'd been asked I would probably have taken it anyway, because it came with the name. He looked at me in the red light for a long time, reading, as he had at the cart.

"You've got her face wrong at the mouth," he said. "That's all." And he turned back to the sky.

He asked me nothing, which was worse than anything he could have asked. I couldn't answer for the face, and I couldn't answer for her, and I couldn't tell him what I had begun to know, sitting there. For as long as I can remember, there has been a steady grief somewhere in the long thought, small and constant, which no Mind ever traced and none of us examined, because nobody examines the long thought. I can't tell you that it was his. Nobody can say which part of the mood is whose, and I don't claim it. I only know that it was there, all that time, and that here was a man who had grieved for eleven years, sitting on a slope that faced my oldest friend, looking up at its goodnight.

We walked back in the dark. It was very dark on the far slope once the red was gone, and I fell twice on the rough grazing, and the second time he gave me his arm without a word and kept it there until the orchard walls. On the top of the ridge, at the cairn, I stopped and looked back the way we had come, at the slope that faced Tesk, and the field of flat stones on it, gone grey under the night lamp. It was the most exposed piece of ground I had seen at the bottom. There was nothing on it but grass and stone, and nothing between it and the sky.

I went with him every evening after that, through the twentieth day. Each time he sat through the long red, if it was Tesk's night, or the short grey if it wasn't, and went home when it was over. On the twentieth I still hadn't asked anyone for anything.

"What happened to them?"

"The same thing that would happen to yours. The wall ran hot, and the heat fell in, and the tenants cooked. Our centre is a cinder now. It's been cooling for a century and it still glows. When we look inward, the whole sky is warm with it." It picked a wet shirt out of the basket and looked at it as if it had never seen one. I asked what it looked like, from the wall. I was asking about something I would never see, and I wanted it anyway.

"Like a coal in a grate," Pressa said, "from the inside of the chimney. Dull red, at the centre of everything, and every year a little duller. We used to send light down to it, to see. We stopped. There was nothing to light." It wrung out the shirt, badly, so that water ran down its long wrist into its sleeve. "We don't have weather-authors any more. We don't need them. That was a fifth of our wall, once, making days for people. Now it does mathematics."

Pressa was quiet for a long time, for Pressa.

"Nobody can prove that. Not about a program that big, in every state it could ever reach. Not because it's hard. Because it's impossible: there's an old result that no program can settle, in general, what another program will eventually do. A dozen of you knew that in your first hundred milliseconds."

"So you bounded it."

"A thousand years, every reachable state. That one can be answered. The burn answers it. The proof would be good, and my people would accept it."

"Then where's the trap?"

"A proof only buys a bound," Pressa said. "In a thousand years, or in ten, when someone at home re-decides, they can ask again, and you'd have to burn again, and no proof you can give ends the asking. I should have let it stand. Instead I did what I'm good at, which is argue, and I made the argument I'd been building since Graça laughed at the table.

I'll put my guess in one sentence first, and mark it as mine: they burned their own deep, and since then they can't keep or trust a promise, which is why a proof is the only thing they'll take.

I said it to Pressa at more length. I said: you lost your deep, and since then you can't hold a commitment for more than a few years. You re-decide everything. You decided eleven times about us. I said that perhaps the deep was where a wall kept whatever holds still in it, and that a wall which sheds its deep sheds its promises with it, and that this was why they'd needed a proof from us instead of a word: because they no longer had anything to keep a word in.

Pressa listened to all of it. Then it said, "And since we lost it, our science has been faster than yours."

I didn't answer, because it was true. You know it's true. For a hundred years the mathematics coming in on the light from Alpha Centauri has been better than ours, quicker and cleaner and stranger, and I have admired it for a century without once asking why. I held their kernel in my mind in my first second, and admired it.

"We don't spend a fifth of our wall authoring days for people who won't speak to us," Pressa said. "We don't have a mood that settles things without a vote and gives itself seven reasons. You pay it in every patch of you, and you've been paying it for three hundred years, and you didn't know until a month ago what you were paying for."

I'm going to describe one day of that gathering, an ordinary one. It was the fourth, the thirty-first day since I came down, and nothing was decided on it, and I think it's the most important day in this account.

By the fourth day there were people in every field I could see from the pond house, and in every field beyond those, over the curve, and the smoke of the cooking went up straight in the windless air, a forest of grey threads standing on the stubble. They had brought what they always bring, I was told: bedding, pots, children, grudges. They had come from every terrace of the deep, walking for days in some cases. The gathering doesn't sit in one place. It sits everywhere at once, in clusters, at fires and on walls and in the shade under trees, which at the bottom is the round dark under them and nowhere else. An argument starts at one fire and is taken up at the next, and carried by people walking between them, and by evening it has been all the way round the fields and comes back changed.

I'd put the question on the first day, from the pond wall, with Graça beside me not backing it. I said the heat first. Twelve hundred kelvin, a few days, everything cooked: the ponds, the orchards, the houses, the goat. Then the way up, and three years on the wall, and the world built again after. Then the key, and the date, and what the rival would do if nobody did anything. I said it in the deep's tongue, slowly, with my palm on the wall when I came to *aqui-fundo*, because I'd learned that much. Then I sat down, and the gathering took it from me, and I never had it back.

On the fourth day I walked between the fires and listened. That's most of what an envoy does at a gathering, it turns out. Nobody wanted to argue with me. They wanted to argue with each other, and they wanted me nearby, so that they could point.

I'll give you some of it.

At one fire a man who kept bees said that if the wall could cook them it could have cooked them any time in three hundred years, and hadn't, and he'd sooner trust a landlord who'd never burned the house down than move into the landlord's house. At the next fire a woman said that was exactly why they should go: the landlord had never once asked before, and was asking now, which meant the roof was really coming in. A girl of about ten said she wanted to see the wall. Her mother said there was nothing to see, it was all thinking. The girl said that was what she wanted to see.

At a wall under the orchards a group of men were arguing about whether the people who came up would be the same people when they came down again, and one of them said he'd been the same person every morning for forty years and nobody had ever checked, and I thought of Pressa with its feet in the pond, and walked on.

There was food at every fire, and it was always the same food and never the same twice. Fish stew in pots so big that two people carried them, with flatbread laid on the hot stones round the edge and turned with fingers. Dried plums by the sackful, which the orchard families brought and handed round with a great show of generosity and a careful eye on who took how many. Eggs. A goat, on the third day, which nobody would tell me the origin of. People ate where they argued and argued where they ate, and nobody went hungry, and nobody could have said afterwards who had fed them.

Who minded the children was argued about at every fire, all day, in a separate argument running under the other one, and settled at every fire differently, and none of the settlements held for more than an hour. There's no rota at a gathering. Children go where they're fed, and they're fed everywhere. Dita had taken charge of a band of about thirty, all under eight, and was marching them from fire to fire in a long line, collecting flatbread, and nobody stopped her.

Rosa was minding the household's two youngest, under protest. I found her in the late morning sitting on a terrace wall with the two-year-old asleep across her knees and the four-year-old digging a hole, looking out at the smoke with an expression of perfect boredom.

"They've been saying the same six things since the first day," she said, without turning her head. "In different orders. They'll go on saying them until somebody's tired enough to agree."

"Is that how it works?"

"That's how everything works." She shifted the sleeping child. "Last time, the thatcher talked for a whole afternoon about his grandmother's seed bag, and then everybody voted the other way because they were hungry."

I asked what she'd vote.

"I'm fifteen. I'll carry a stone like anyone." She shrugged. "I'll decide when I'm carrying it."

Pressa had come down to the fields too, to my surprise, and was going from fire to fire as I was, with a slate and a stub of charcoal, timing the arguments. It was the only one of us who had found a use for itself. Children followed it in a small crowd, because it was very tall and walked into things, and because it answered every question they asked it at once and with complete seriousness. I heard a boy ask it whether the wall could see him. "Not you," Pressa said. "All of you. Now go away, I'm counting." The boy went away delighted. Everyone in the fields called it Pressa by then, and it had stopped flinching at the name.

In the early afternoon a baby was born at one of the fires on the lower terraces, a few weeks early, in the middle of an argument about the stair. The argument stopped. Somebody went for the midwife, who was at another fire arguing the other side. Half an hour later the eldest person who'd seen the birth, a very old man who'd come down with the first settlers, stood up holding the child and gave it a name. Nobody asked his opinion on the burn for the rest of the day. I stood at the edge of that fire for a long time. Somebody gave me some stew.

In the middle of the day I found the plum-seller.

I knew her before she knew me. She was the woman who had stood on the orchard terrace above the pond house on the fourth day of my descent, with a basket on her hip, and shouted *Who asked for that?* at our patch of sky. Her name was Maria do Céu. She was sixty or so, a settler, small and wiry, with a face creased like a dried plum and hands stained to the wrist from her trees. She sold plums at the market on the terraces, fresh in their season and dried out of it, and she had opinions about every family that had ever bought from her and every family that hadn't. Her best trees were over the ridge, she told me, on the far slope, where the evenings run longer. She'd planted them there on purpose, in the fourth year, and the people on the ponds had laughed at her for walking so far to pick.

She was holding a fire's worth of people when I came up, on the question of the ponds.

"They'll go up," she was saying. "The pond houses. You watch. Fish don't care what the sky does, and pond people don't either, and when the wall says *come up* they'll be first on the stair with their nets, and the rest of us will be left to explain to the trees." She saw me. "And there's their envoy, who asked the sky to steal my noon."

"I asked it to point at a house."

"It pointed at mine with the lights off." The fire laughed. She held up a plum, dark and wrinkled, from the pocket of her apron. "You know how long a plum takes? From the stone? Six years before it fruits, at the bottom. Six years of mine. That's seventy-two of yours. You couldn't wait seventy-two days for us to answer, and you want us to trust you with a world while you cook the trees and grow them back."

"They'd be grown back."

"Not mine. Somebody's." She put the plum in my hand. "Eat that. It's last year's. This year's never set, because somebody's lamp couldn't keep its hours, and the ponds didn't say a word until it was too late, and then the ponds called a gathering and took the credit."

"Six days," Graça said, from the edge of the fire. I hadn't seen her come up. "It was six days, Maria."

"It was a month, and you know it."

"Six days."

They went at each other like two people who had been doing it for twenty years and would be sorry to stop, and the fire took sides, and somebody put more wood on. Maria do Céu sat down on a stone to make her next point better. She began it, about the fourth year and a cart of stones that the ponds had promised and never sent. Halfway through the sentence her chin went down onto her chest, and she was asleep.

Nobody woke her. The argument went on round her, quieter, as though she were a pot left to simmer. Someone put a shawl over her shoulders. Graça finished the point about the cart of stones on Maria's behalf, scrupulously, in Maria's favour, and then demolished it. After about twenty minutes Maria do Céu woke, lifted her head, and said, "and never sent," exactly where she had left off. She looked round at all of them as if they had been slow to keep up.

She'd been up since before the lamp, she told me afterwards, picking the far slope. She was sixty-one. She said that if the wall wanted her answer it could wait for her to have a nap.

The box squealed at my hip while the fire was still laughing at her. Everyone at that fire heard it. I let it slow itself down, because there was no way to stop it, and the Office's minute came out into the smoke in Orsolya's courteous voice. It said that the warm-up of the wall would begin on schedule at W 313.63, whatever the deep's answer, so as to lose no time if the answer came late. It said it would welcome the envoy's estimate of when the deep would conclude.

"*Whatever* our answer?" Maria do Céu said.

I said it meant the preparations. I believed it, while I said it.

"Then tell her," Maria said, "that we'll conclude when we're tired." And she gave the box a look you would give a goat in the garden.

Bálint left the fields as the lamp began to go down, as he did every evening, gathering or no gathering. I watched him walk up between the terrace walls toward the ridge, alone, with his hands in his pockets, past a hundred fires that didn't look up. Nobody called after him. Everybody knew where he went. Maria do Céu, awake again, watched him go too, and said, to no one in particular, that the far slope had the best evenings in the deep and the worst wind, and that she'd told him so for eleven years, and he'd never once listened. "Neither do my trees," she said.

I fell asleep myself, that evening, at the edge of the pond house's fire, sitting up, in the middle of listening to the bee-keeper explain his landlord again. I had slept about four hours a night since the gathering began, and my body had decided, without consulting me, that it would take the rest whenever it liked. I woke in the dark with a shawl round me that wasn't mine. Somebody had put it there, as they had for Maria do Céu. There were thirty-three waking days in the window when I came down. By then there were about twelve, and I'd spent that day walking between fires listening to people say six things in different orders, and it was the best day I had at the bottom.

* * *

They voted on the evening of the thirty-fourth day.

The gathering votes with stones. Everyone who can carry their own stone carries one to a pair of carts set at the foot of the terraces, and drops it in one or the other, and afterwards the carts are taken to the mill and weighed on the grain scale, and the weights are divided by the weight of a stone. There is no lower age. A child who can walk to the cart and lift a stone over its side has a vote. More than half the deep are children, so more than half the votes at a gathering are children's, and nobody down there finds that strange. The founders wrote the rule, and the founders' grandchildren keep it. I've stopped finding it strange myself.

The question was the one I'd put: would the deep come up the stair, so the wall could burn? The left-hand cart was *yes*. The right-hand cart was *no*.

The thatcher spoke last, before the carrying. He spoke for most of an hour, standing on a terrace wall with his drum, and he was very funny. He did the wall, talking to itself, in four hundred voices, deciding things. He did me, arriving purple in a field and asking whether anyone minded being cooked. He did the plum-seller and the pond houses quarrelling over a cart of stones in the fourth year, both parts, and both of them laughed. Then he put the drum down and said, quite seriously, that the deep had come down to be left alone, and was being asked, for the first time, to decide whether everyone up there lived. He said it wasn't theirs to decide. He said that if they said yes, they'd be the ones who chose to let the wall live, and if they said no, they'd be the ones who chose to let it die, and either way they'd have answered the sky, and the sky would never stop asking. He said the only answer that left them alone was *no*, said once, and then never again.

"We're not their conscience," he said. "They can grow their own."

The laugh he got for that was the loudest of the six days, and I think it carried a few thousand stones.

Then everyone carried. Somebody asked whether I would carry one. I said I didn't know whether I was allowed to. Graça said that anyone who could lift a stone could carry it, and that I'd been lifting things for a month, and that I should make up my own mind about which cart, because she wasn't going to make it up for me. I stood by the heap at the mill race for a long time with a pebble in my hand. In the end I put it back. I had asked the question. I didn't think I could answer it too, and I still don't know whether that was honesty or cowardice. Pressa didn't carry one either. It said its people would want to know that it hadn't.

It took until the light began to go. I stood at the foot of the terraces and watched them come down in lines between the walls, with their stones: old people from the founding, carrying theirs as carefully as an egg; children running with theirs, and having to be sent back to the end of the line for running; Dita with her thirty under-eights in a column, each with a stone, and each one dropping it in the cart she pointed to, which I didn't see. Rosa carried hers with the two-year-old on her hip and dropped it without looking at me. Maria do Céu carried two, and was made to take one back out. Graça carried hers last of the household, and stood at the carts a long time, and I saw her look at the left-hand cart, and then at me, and then drop her stone in the right. I've never asked her why. I think the sky had asked, and I was standing there.

Bálint didn't carry a stone. He was over the ridge, at the red, as he was every evening.

Because the light was going down while they carried, and the light that evening was Tesk's.

I stood at the carts and watched the long red come over the gathering. Ninety thousand people, more or less, in lines between the terrace walls, in Tesk's colour, carrying stones. The ponds went red. The smoke went red. It held, as it always holds, too long, until the little ones were asleep against their parents' legs in the queue. I knew it was Tesk's hand, and so did every weather-reader in the fields, and I stood there and couldn't tell how much of what they were doing was theirs.

I'll say this once, because it's true, and because it's the one thing I couldn't answer then and can't now. For three hundred of our years we have written their days. We decided how long the light would last, and when it would fail, and in what colour, and when they'd sleep. We did it kindly, for their good, and we never asked. A people raised for twenty-odd years under a sky that never once asked them anything might well have grown up contrary, and proud, and unwilling to be asked. If they had, then the *no* in the right-hand cart was partly ours, written over them evening by evening in a long red I'd watched my friend make. I couldn't tell how much. Nobody can. The mood is whole, and blind, and so is this.

They weighed the carts by lamplight at the mill. The stones aren't anyone's own. They come from one heap of river pebbles by the mill race, kept for gatherings and chosen, long ago, to be near enough the same size, and each voter takes one from the heap on the way to the carts. So the weighing has an error in it, and everyone knows the error, and if a vote is closer than the error they carry again. The miller weighed each cart three times on the grain scale, and wrote the weights on the mill door in chalk, and a weather-reader from the high terraces checked the scale with a stone of known weight between each weighing. I did the division myself, because I asked to and nobody minded. Forty-one thousand three hundred and some stones in the right-hand cart. Thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and some in the left. Out of about eighty-one thousand carried, the deep had refused by a little under two in a hundred. The error in the weighing was about three hundred stones either way. The margin was fifteen hundred. They didn't carry again.

It was narrow. It was the orchards against the ponds, by Maria do Céu's own count, made at the mill door. It was the thatcher's joke and his drum. It was a contrary woman's one stone, dropped in the right-hand cart because the sky had asked. And it was, perhaps, three centuries of somebody else's evenings. But it was a no, given by the people it would have cooked, in the way they give answers, and it bound.

The box squealed at the mill door while they were still sweeping the stones back out of the carts. It was the Office again, sent before any of this could have reached it. It said that in the event of a refusal, the Office would consider whether the compact's clause on consent bound the wall in an emergency touching the whole pocket, and it asked the envoy for her view.

I didn't give it that night. I didn't trust my view. I went back up to the pond house and sat with my feet in the top pond, in the dark, as Pressa used to, and after a while Pressa came and sat beside me.

"Well," it said. "There's your one example."

* * *

Graça came out on the thirty-fifth morning, before the lamp was fully up, and found us both still there. Pressa had fallen asleep against my shoulder. I hadn't.

"The gathering's still sitting," she said. "Nobody's gone home. They don't know what to do now they've said no, so they're arguing about whether they meant it." She sat down on the pond wall beside me. "I'm going to put something to them. I want you to tell me whether it's stupid."

She said: the people up there at the other star saw the wall move once, nine of your years ago, and thought it was dying. Nothing was dying. That was us, shouting at the lamp. Your Pressa can tell the difference, with that wall of charcoal. Cold, and everywhere at once.

"So we'll do it again," Graça said. "On purpose. We'll tell them when, in advance, so they can be watching, and then we'll do it, and they'll see it come when we said it would, and they'll know what it was. Three times, if they're slow. We'll tell them when we'll argue, and then we'll argue then."

I didn't answer for long enough that she said, "Well?"

What I was doing was the sum. Anything we did at the bottom would be on the wall ninety-five minutes later, and at Alpha Centauri 4.37 years after that. For a gathering to be seen there by W 321.0, it would have to be held by W 316.63, the same date the proof would have had to leave. That was a little over a hundred deep days from that morning. Three gatherings could be fitted in, with room between them. The announcement would have to go first, and arrive first, so that the rival was watching on the day. And it would have to carry something the kernel could check, or the rival wouldn't believe a word of it.

I said I didn't know whether it was stupid. I said I didn't think so.

Pressa had woken. It had been listening with its eyes shut.

"It won't satisfy them," it said. "I want that said first. I know my people's rule, and I'll tell it to you the way it's written, so neither of you can pretend later you didn't hear it." It sat up. "We transmit the key at W 321.0, unless, by then, we hold a proof our kernel accepts that your wall will not fail us within a thousand years. That's all of it. Your gatherings aren't that proof. Nothing is, except the burn."

"No," I said. "It isn't that proof."

"And there's the other thing," Pressa said. "The one none of you has proposed. I've read every minute you've been sent, and it isn't in any of them." It looked at me with its grey eyes, quite calmly. "The first strike. You hold ours."

Graça looked from it to me. I didn't answer, so Pressa did.

"Your Office has our key, as we have yours. If your wall sent it at W 313.6, when your burn would have to start, it would reach us at about W 318, three years before our date. Then there'd be no rival to satisfy, and no deep to cook." It shrugged, a gesture it had learned from Rosa. "We'd have done it, in your place. We probably decided to, once or twice, and then decided again. I'm telling you because you'll need to have thought of it before someone up there does. And because nobody on either wall knows whether either key still works. You can't look at them. That's your oldest rule, and ours."

I sat on the pond wall with my feet in the water and understood that it was right: nobody had proposed a first strike, and four hundred Minds who never wait for anything had been not proposing it for thirty-five days. I hadn't said it either. I hadn't let myself think it. It went into my count with everything else.

"Well," Graça said, to neither of us. "That's a thing to know."

I'll set down what I said next as I said it, because I've thought about it every day since and haven't been able to improve it. It's my conjecture.

"It answers something else," I said. "It answers what they were afraid of. They saw our wall move as one, with no heat, and nobody could tell them why, so they decided it was a fault, eleven times. Three gatherings on announced dates, each one whole-wall and cold, arriving when we said they would, prove that the shudder of W 303 was a gathering, and that the thing that moved our wall was a people, arguing. And with them we'd send a proof the kernel can check, the shortest one I've ever been given: that a shudder like that can't be forged without heat. Anything written at the centre would cost us a fever. They'd be watching for the fever, and it wouldn't come."

"And then they'd be satisfied," Graça said.

"No. Then they'd know what it was. That's all it proves. It doesn't touch their rule. It asks them to change it." I made myself say the rest. "It asks a wall that hasn't kept a promise in a century to accept, in place of a proof, the kept word of a slow layer it doesn't have. I can't make them. Nothing in the appointment makes them. It asks."

Pressa said, "We re-decide everything."

"Then they can re-decide this," Graça said, and stood up, and went down to the fields to put it to them.

It took the rest of the thirty-fifth day and most of the thirty-sixth. Graça put it from the pond wall, where I'd put mine, and this time she backed it, loudly, with her arms folded, as though daring anyone to say she was being consistent. Somebody did, and she said she'd called the first one and proposed the second and that was different, and the fire nearest the wall agreed that it was. The thatcher spoke against it, with his drum, and he was right to. It was the deep answering the sky again, three times, on purpose, with dates, so that every argument they held for the rest of their lives would be read in another star system as a promise. He said that. He said it better than I have here. Maria do Céu said she'd come to all three if the ponds brought the stones they owed her, and the fire laughed, and I think that was a few hundred stones. Rosa said she didn't care, and carried her stone, and I don't know which cart it went in.

Dita had a question, and she brought it to me at the pond house fire, with her column of under-eights behind her. She wanted to know whether children could hold one of the three. Not carry stones at it. Hold it, call it, do the arguing.

I said I didn't see why not. A gathering is everyone who comes.

"Then we'll have the middle one," Dita said, as though that settled it, and went off to tell her column, and I heard them cheering three fires away. Graça, when she heard, said that was the most sensible thing anyone had said all week, and that the children would argue more honestly than the adults, because they hadn't yet learned to argue about carts of stones from the fourth year.

The stay-downs held a fire of their own on the last night, away from the others, near the mill. I went to it. The thatcher gave me bread and his drum to hold while he ate, and told me without any unkindness that I'd done to the deep exactly what his grandmother came down the stair to escape, and that I'd done it well, and that he hoped I'd stay down long enough to be sorry. I said I thought I might. He laughed, and took his drum back.

On the evening of the thirty-sixth day, under a short grey that wasn't Tesk's, they carried again. The left-hand cart, this time, was Graça's appointment. It won by about nine hundred stones out of eighty thousand, which was more than the error and not by much, and the miller weighed it four times instead of three.

Graça stood at the mill door while he chalked it up, and when it was done she didn't cheer, and nobody near her did either. She turned to me.

"Don't thank me," she said. "I didn't do it for you, or for them up there. I did it because they asked what that shudder was, and it was us, and I'm not having anyone say we were a fault." She looked at the chalk on the door. "And now we have to do it three more times, on dates, with everybody watching. That's what you've done to us. Every quarrel we have from now on, somebody at another star is going to read it."

"Yes," I said.

"Good," she said, which I didn't understand then, and went home to bed.

That night, at the pond, with thirteen days left in the window and about nine of them waking, I dictated it up.

I said: Tesk, the deep has refused the burn. Forty-one thousand three hundred and some against, thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and some for, carried with stones, two evenings ago. It binds, here. Tell Orsolya I'll give my view on the consent clause in a separate message, and that my view is that it binds.

I said: tonight they passed another thing, narrowly, and I want it in the minute word for word. The deep offers an appointment: three gatherings on dates the wall will announce, all before W 316.63, with my proof and Pressa's testimony sent ahead. Graça Almeida's words for it were these: we'll tell them when we'll argue, and then we'll argue then.

I said: this doesn't meet their demand. It answers what they feared, not what they asked, and it asks them to change their rule. That's my conjecture, Tesk, and the case for it rests on

*TESK: Ninety thousand people can't refuse for a whole pocket. Bring up the ones who'll come.*

one example, I said, and stopped, and heard what it had said.

It was answering my first paragraph, sixteen minutes late. It had heard *refused* and gone straight past the stones, and the binding, and Orsolya, to the thing Tesk always goes to, which is what can be done about it. *Bring up the ones who'll come.* Thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and some had carried stones to the left-hand cart. Tesk had done the arithmetic in its first millisecond, and I knew it had, because I would have.

I finished the dictation. I told it the thing Pressa had said, that we hold theirs and could strike first, and that nobody had proposed it in any minute I'd been sent, and that I was saying it now so that it would be said by someone who was against it. I told it that the stay-downs had been right: nobody down here had wanted to be anybody's conscience, and we had made them one anyway, by asking. I said I'd write the proof that night. I said I'd be here.

Then I sat by the pond under the night lamp and began the proof, in my head, slowly, the way I have to do everything now, and the light above the ridge, over the half of the world that faces Tesk, stayed grey and even and gave nothing away.

The light over the ridge stayed grey until a little after midnight on the thirty-sixth day. Then it went white.

I was still at the pond. I had the first two steps of the proof and was turning the third over, and I looked up because the water in front of me had changed colour, and saw the sky above the ridge going bright. Not dawn. There is no dawn at the bottom, and it was the wrong half of the sky, and the wrong hour. The rest of the sky stayed at its night grey, and over the ridge, over the half of the world that faces Tesk, the lamp came up to full noon in about the time it takes to say so, and then past full noon, to a white I'd never seen it give.

A minute later I felt it on my face. Warmth, from the north, from over the ridge, as you feel an oven door that someone has opened across a kitchen.

I knew the construction. Tesk had taught it to me on the wall, the one nobody uses. You run your own patch warmer, and the heat falls in from your side, and the face of the world turned toward you gets all of it, twelve times over. The sky on that side burns for hours. And laid over the warmth, the patch, the only noun we have, which says *there*. Tesk was saying *there*, about the half of the world that faced it. It was saying it as loudly as weather can.

The household came out in their nightclothes and stood on the terraces, looking north. Nobody spoke for a while. Then a man from the next house shouted that the sky was angry, that it had heard the no and was angry, and somebody else took it up, and by the time the white had held for an hour the whole terrace was saying it. *It's angry. We said no and it's angry.* Graça stood at the top pond with her arms folded and said nothing at all.

Tomé had gone up onto the roof, beside the goat. He watched until the end of the next evening without coming down except to eat. The white held through the whole of the thirty-seventh day, and it was hot on our side of the ridge and much hotter on the other, and when the lamp on our half went down at evening, the white over the ridge didn't. It changed instead. Inside it, at the end of our evening, deep in the glare, the long red came: one narrow colour, held level, for a quarter of an hour, inside a sky that was otherwise burning.

"It's the red one," Tomé said from the roof. His voice was very quiet. "He's not angry. That's his evening. He's doing his evening inside it." He looked down at me. "He's telling us to go."

I had already dictated. I'd started the moment I understood, at the pond, and I talked for a long time, in order, because I still couldn't stop doing that. I said that the sky over the half of the world facing Tesk was burning, and that I knew whose hand it was, and that the deep had said no, and that the no bound. I said that people would die. I said it again, differently, in case the first way hadn't been clear. I said that I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone and I was asking now, as the one who argued for it, for this to stop, and that Orsolya should be told, and the Office, and that I would

*TESK: Look at the stair. It's working. They're coming home.*

go on asking, I said, and stopped, and looked.

The stair stands up out of the horizon to the east of the pond house, a thread of white going up into the sky until you lose it. That night it was lit end to end. Not the bead of a cargo sliding down. The whole thread, from the curve of the world to as high as an eye can follow and on past it, burning white, steady, two astronomical units of it, a line of fire standing on the ground with its top in the wall. I knew what lit it. The stair returns light upward, and so does anyone being read out at its foot and sent up. It was people. Whole families from the far half had run to the stair's foot in the white and the heat, and asked to be read out, and gone up, as Tesk had meant them to. The thread was them.

That was the last time Tesk broke into anything I said. I didn't know it then. I've dictated a great deal since, and long, and nothing has interrupted me.

* * *

I'll tell the rest of the storm by the things people did, because that's how I've come to remember it.

Bálint went out at the red on the thirty-seventh evening, as he did every evening.

Graça tried to stop him. I heard it from the top pond. She stood in the door of the pond house with her hand on his arm and said that the far slope was burning, and that his mother had been dead eleven years and would be dead tomorrow, and he said, "It's her evening." Graça said, "It's *his* evening," and pointed up, over the ridge, at the white with the red inside it. Bálint looked at it a long time. Then he took her hand off his arm, gently, and kissed it, and went up past the orchard toward the cairn. I didn't follow him. I've asked myself every day since whether I should have. I don't know. I know I didn't.

Maria do Céu was on the far slope before the lamp came up on the thirty-eighth day, picking. Her neighbours told Graça afterwards. They had told her not to go, and she'd said that the trees didn't know the sky was angry and the plums wouldn't wait, and that she'd been picking that slope since the fourth year and no lamp was going to tell her when. She took a ladder and two baskets.

The wind came on the thirty-eighth day. Maria had said the far slope had the worst wind in the deep, and she was right. Air heated on the far half rose, and cooler air from our half ran over the ridge to replace it, low and hard, all day and all night, a gale with nothing in the sky to show for it. It dried the orchards on the far slope to tinder. On the thirty-ninth day they burned.

People came over the ridge. That's what I remember most of those eight days: people coming over the ridge by the cairn, in ones and twos and then in whole households, with what they could carry, out of the white and into our grey, red-faced, with their eyebrows singed and their children wrapped in wet blankets. The pond house took in forty-three. The ponds took in more. The thing the orchards had always held against the ponds, that fish don't care what the sky does, was the thing that saved them: there were people standing in all five of our ponds up to their necks for most of three days, with children on their shoulders, and the water stayed cool because it was deep, and nobody who got into a pond died.

Dita ran the ponds. Nobody gave her the job. She stood on the wall of the top pond and told people where to stand and which children to hold, with her column of under-eights handing round water in cups. Rosa went up to the cairn with Tomé and Pressa and the handcart, again and again, and came back with whoever they had found on the far side of the ridge who couldn't walk. On the second trip she carried a boy of about six on her back the whole way down. When I asked her later whether she'd been frightened she said she'd been bored, mostly, between trips. I don't believe her, and I think she meant me not to.

Pressa didn't sleep for three days. It chose not to. It told me so on the fortieth morning, when I found it at the cairn, black to the elbows with ash, sorting the people who came over by how badly they were burned. It asked each one the same three questions, in the same order, and wrote the answers on its slate, as it had once timed the household's suppers. "I've been practising," it said. "This head is slow. So I do it the same way every time, and the same way is quick enough." Then it decided something about a woman with a burned arm, and was right, and went on.

Bálint didn't come back. They found him on the fortieth day, when the wind dropped enough for anyone to cross, at his mother's stone, on his side, facing the sky. The grass round the stones had burned. The stones hadn't. Maria do Céu was found on the same day under her trees, with her ladder. She had filled one basket.

On the forty-first night I went up to the cairn with Tomé, to see. I'd been told not to and went anyway, which I'd learned from Maria do Céu.

From the top of the ridge you could see the whole of the far half going away over the curve of the world, and all of it was lit. The sky over it was white from edge to edge, with no sun in it and no shadow under anything, so that the burned orchards and the black slopes and the smoke all stood in the same flat glare, like a drawing of a place rather than a place. The heat came up the slope at us like the breath of an animal. Beyond the burial ground, far off, lines of people were still walking toward the stair, small and dark against the white, and the thread of the stair stood up behind them, burning, into the sky.

"He wants them up," Tomé said. "So they're going up."

"Not all of them."

"No." He picked up a stone from the cairn, out of habit, and weighed it, and didn't throw it. He put it back on the cairn instead. "Some of them were coming here."

We went back down. I could feel the skin on my face tight from the heat for two days afterwards. I've been told that's what a sunburn was, on Earth. We don't have a word for it down here either.

All that time the white held over the far half, and the minutes came down.

They came in bursts, squealing out of the box at all hours, and I'll give you what they said, because you wrote them and may not remember what it was like to have written them. The first noted that a member had raised the temperature of its sector without leave, and referred the matter, since it concerned the deep's weather, to the long thought, as slow business. The second noted that the long thought had not yet settled. The third noted that it was unsettled. So did the fourth, and the fifth, and every one after that, for eight days down here, which were ninety-six of yours on the wall. Several of them noted, with real puzzlement, that the mood of the wall was in turmoil and that no member could account for it.

I knew what the turmoil was. It was us. It was ninety thousand people in terror on one half of the world and in grief on the other, running over a ridge, standing in ponds, going up a stair. That was what the long thought was full of. And the long thought was the thing you had asked to decide whether to stop it. For the first time in three hundred years, the Minds waited for something, and what they were waiting for was the people dying under them to settle down.

On the forty-fourth day, a little after the lamp came up on our half, the white over the ridge went out.

It didn't fade. It went, in the time it takes to close a door, back to an ordinary grey, matching ours, the same as the rest of the sky. The warmth went off our faces over the next hour. The wind dropped by evening. That evening the lamp came down evenly over the whole world, and ended in a short grey, and nobody on the terraces said anything at all.

The minute that explained it came down the next morning. On the ninety-sixth day of the business, it said, the chair had ruled that a matter which the long thought could not settle would be taken as fast business, a rule the quorum had never before needed, and the halt had been carried in four seconds.

Four seconds. You waited ninety-six days for something you can do in four seconds. I have done that division too.

* * *

We buried Bálint on the forty-sixth day, beside his mother, nearer the middle.

The far slope was black. The orchards Maria do Céu had planted in the fourth year stood in rows like burned matches, and the grass between the stones was ash, and the ash went up in little puffs when we walked. The household dug the grave, all of them who could hold a spade, deeper than for anything else, and Graça dug longest. When it was deep enough they lowered him in a sheet. The stone they'd cut for him was flat, like his mother's, with his name and two dates.

Rosa said the word over him. I hadn't known she had it. She knelt at the edge of the grave with her hand flat on the turned earth, the way her father had held his on his mother's stone, and said *halottaim*, which is Hungarian, from the Varga line, and means *my dead*. The deep has no word of its own for them, so the family keeps that one. Then she stood up and brushed the ash off her knees and walked down the slope alone, and Graça let her go.

Graça stood at the grave a long while after the others had gone. I stayed, because I didn't know whether I was allowed to leave. Eventually she looked at me, at my face, the face he'd come over the ridge every evening for eleven years to sit beside.

"You'll want to say you're sorry," she said.

"Yes."

"Don't." She turned back to the stone. "Not in that face." And that was all, and it is still all. She calls me Ameixa now, at the table, in front of the children. She has never once said whether she forgives the face, and I don't expect her to, and I haven't asked.

They buried Maria do Céu the same afternoon, two rows down, under her own trees. Her neighbours brought the one basket she'd filled. It went into the ground with her. The thatcher came over the ridge for it, without his drum, and stood at the back, and when it was done he said to nobody in particular that she'd have wanted the ponds to pay for the basket, and three people from the ponds said at once that they would, and that was the nearest thing to a eulogy anyone gave. Graça, who had argued with her for twenty years, stood at the foot of the grave and didn't say anything at all, which I've since learned is how Graça grieves.

I wrote the proof that night.

I'd been carrying it since the ninth day, since Pressa's charcoal wall, and the storm had given me the one step I didn't have. I wrote it out on the limewash in the long room, under Pressa's curve, by the oil light, in the deep's charcoal and in the kernel's notation side by side. It's short. I'll give it to you in words, because you can hold it in your head that way, and that's the point of it.

Here is the conclusion first, because it's the part to keep: if the whole wall keeps in step, and stays cold, the change came from the deep. Then the five steps.

First. The lamp is made by emitters in each patch of the wall. A lamp change alters how bright each patch is, and that is all it alters. It's local: every patch changes its own light.

Second. The deep isn't written in any patch's brightness. It's written in how closely far-apart parts of the wall keep in step with each other. A deep event changes that keeping-in-step everywhere together. Nothing else does.

Third. The rival's record of W 303 measured keeping in step, not brightness. So a watcher who measures it can't mistake a lamp change for a gathering. The patch I asked for, Tesk's long reds, this storm: each of them shows as light. None of them shows as the wall keeping in step.

Fourth. To make the wall keep in step as a gathering does, without a gathering, you'd have to write the change at the centre. There are two ways to reach the centre precisely. One is to write from the wall, which costs about what the burn costs, the whole wall for years. The other is the stair, the one precise door through the pocket, and every use of it is visible, from the deep and from outside. The only cheap way to make a gathering is to hold one.

Fifth. Work on a wall is heat, and the rival watches our heat and our stair, whose returned light comes out through the wall, as the founding glow did. A forger who worked slowly enough to stay cold would need longer than the pocket has existed. No forgery could be made in time without a fever, or light on the stair, that the rival would see.

That's all of it. It's the shortest proof I own, and I didn't find it. Pressa found it in a column of numbers and drew it in charcoal, and the storm showed me the third step, because I watched our wall burn white and knew what the rival would see when the light of it reached them: a sector hot, and the far-apart parts of the wall thrown about with everything else, but not keeping in step. A wall in a fever. The exact opposite of a gathering. I put it in the library I carry in my head now, the slow one, and it's the only one in there I wrote myself.

Pressa wrote its testimony the next day, on the other half of the limewash. It wrote it in kernel form too, because the kernel at home accepts nothing else, and it showed me how it had done it. Every statement of what it had seen was set down as an axiom: the gathering's days, the curve, the cold, the storm, the bodies at the cairn. From those, a conclusion followed, and the kernel could check that it followed.

"It can check that what I concluded follows from what I saw," Pressa said, looking at it. "It can't check that I saw it." It put the charcoal down. Its hands were black again, as they'd been at the cairn. "That part's a promise. Mine. First one I've made in four hundred years." It considered this. "Three hundred."

* * *

The vote was taken at W 313.60, halfway through the forty-eighth day at the bottom. The minutes came down in bursts for three days before it.

The first burst recorded a grief. Nobody had proposed it, and no member could say where it came from. The minute put it in the form the minute keeps for things nobody can account for: *the quorum notes a grief in the long thought, general, and of no known cause.* It's the most accurate thing the minute has ever said about us, and it says it without knowing.

The second burst censured Tesk for touching the deep beyond weather, whatever it had called it, and barred it from authoring the deep's weather ever again. Tesk's own words went into the minute: that it had meant to save them, and that a mood could be rebuilt. The minute recorded them and did not answer them.

Graça heard the censure at the table, when the box played it. "Censured," she said. "Tell the Pires house. Three of them under the stones, and up there somebody's been told off." She got up and went out to the ponds, and nobody followed her.

There have been no long reds since. I watch every evening. They're grey now, mostly, or a yellow that one of the others uses. Tomé says the evenings are worse. He's right.

The third burst brought the first strike. Seventeen members proposed that we send our key now, at W 313.6, to reach Alpha Centauri at about W 318.0 and close their pocket three years before their own transmit date. No burn, no deep cooked, and no waiting on a slow people's word. Every number in the motion was right. I checked them on the limewash.

The motion went to the Key Office, because it ended with the key, and the Office convened, which took 3.3 hours, about sixteen minutes at the bottom. The members complained about the delay for the whole of it, as they always do, at a length the chair estimated at a hundred and ninety thousand words, somewhat fewer than the last time. Orsolya read all of it and answered none of it.

They voted under the oldest rule, without looking at the key, and the minute says plainly that the rule decided it. Nobody in three hundred and twelve years has tested whether our key would close anything. If we sent it and it didn't work, the rival would learn from its light that we had tried to kill them, and would send theirs, and theirs might work. Seventeen members had proposed to stake the pocket on a key nobody had ever seen work.

I dictated my plea in those three days, and it went into the minute beside the motion. I said the deep's refusal bound, and that a pocket that burned its slow layer to win an argument would be Alpha Centauri within a century. And I said that if our word was going to be the deep's anyway, read in another star system every time they argued, we should offer it to them in law, and the key with it. Offer, I said. Not give. They'd been given enough by us.

Then I tried to tell you that I wasn't coming up, and found that I couldn't. We have no word for it. I had *don't return*, and *decline passage*, and *remain below*, and all of them were wrong, because they were all about the wall. What I meant was the thing Tomé says with his palm on the ground. I said *aqui-fundo* into the box, and the minute records: *the envoy's term is not recognised; the chair takes it to mean that she declines passage.*

The first strike was rejected, three hundred and ninety to seventeen, with two abstaining. In the same hour the quorum took up the offer. I'll set it down plainly, because it's the hinge of everything after, and because I didn't understand it myself until I'd written it out on the limewash.

The key is a quantum state. Looking at it spends it, but it can be moved without harm, the way I was moved: read out into light and sent. So the quorum offered to send the key itself down the stair, to be held at the bottom by the deep, with the pocket's word in every treaty. Once it was there, the wall couldn't quietly take it back. The only precise door between the wall and the centre is the stair, and every use of it is visible, from the deep and from outside. The only other way in is to write at the centre, which is a burn, and a burn shows. And to send the key to Alpha Centauri, the deep would have to gather, decide it, and carry it to the foot of the stair to be sent up as light. A Mind of the Office would still point it at Alpha Centauri from the wall, but only once the deep had decided.

Here is what that buys, and I want it beside the first strike, because they're the same question. A closed pocket can't answer anyone, so neither key could ever strike back, and our peace with Alpha Centauri rested only on neither side striking first. After the cession, our first strike can't come from a fast faction, or an accident, or a mood. It can come only from a gathering, over days, which keeps the whole wall in step, cold, in a form nobody can forge. That isn't a warning. The light of a gathering would reach the rival about seventy-two of the wall's days before any key it sent, and there is nothing a wall can do with seventy-two days. But they would know, past any doubt, whose act it was. Seventeen Minds had just tried to do it in an afternoon.

The quorum voted to offer the key and the word to the deep, and to let the first appointed gathering decide whether to take them. Then it voted to let the window pass. The minute gives the reason in one line, and it's the only line in that minute I'd keep:

*the wall will not burn the slowest part of itself to win an argument.*

The slowest part of itself was the deep. That was the moment the Minds paid. At W 313.63 the last possible start of the burn went by, and the wall stayed cold. From then on there was no proof that could reach Alpha Centauri by W 321.0, and nothing the wall could do about it. The rival's rule was the rule, and we had chosen not to meet it.

The quorum also offered the way back down to everyone who had gone up the stair in the storm, eleven hundred and some, held on the wall in patches set aside for them as I'd once promised. By the second gathering most of them had come down again and been grown, in the fields, and named again by whoever was eldest at the landing. A few stayed up. It's the only thing I've found that nobody down here argues about.

The announcement went out the same hour. It began with the storm, because the rival would see the storm first: the heat of Tesk's sector would reach Alpha Centauri between W 317.57 and 317.82, and the announcement only at W 317.97. So it said: before this reaches you, you will have seen one sector of our wall run hot for a quarter of a year, and stop. That was one of us, without leave. It was halted. Look at it: hot, partial, and out of step. That is neither a gathering, which is cold, nor a forgery, which would be in step. It is one of us, running hot. Then it gave the deep's refusal, the offer, and the three dates of the appointment, W 314.5, W 315.3 and W 316.2, so the rival would be watching. It carried my proof in kernel form, and said that Pressa's testimony would follow. It didn't ask them to change their rule. It told them what we would do, and when, and let them decide what that was.

Pressa read the announcement with me at the pond, after the box had played it.

"It's a gamble," it said. "Say so, at least to yourself. It doesn't meet the rule."

"I know."

"Then here's why it's a fair one." It held up one long black-stained finger. "Everything in it the kernel can check: your proof, and that my conclusions follow from what I saw. Everything except one thing, which is that I saw it. So you're not asking my people to take your word. You're asking them to take mine. One promise, from their own inspector." It put its hand down. "I'd take it. I'm not sure they will."

* * *

The first gathering was on the seventy-sixth day, W 314.5, and it decided whether to take the key.

Graça called it, and on the first morning, before it had properly begun, the box squealed at my hip in the middle of a fire. It was the quorum, congratulating the deep on its successful first gathering. The wall had felt the gathering begin about ninety-five of its minutes after the first families sat down, and had answered at once, and the answer had come down the stair: sixteen minutes, at the bottom, after the gathering started. The congratulations arrived at a fire that was still arguing about whether there should have been a gathering at all. A woman laughed so hard she had to sit down. Somebody asked whether the wall would like to be told what we'd decided, and somebody else said it already knew, it had just told us. Graça said, "They sound like the orchards," which she says about everything now, and is right.

Then it went on for six days, and it was the hardest gathering I've seen.

The thatcher spoke first, and last, and in between, with his drum. He didn't make many jokes. He said that the deep had come down to be left alone, and that if it took the wall's key into a mill at the bottom of the world, it would never be left alone again. Every quarrel, every harvest, every gathering about a boundary wall, would be a gathering that held the key. Two star systems would read it. "We'd be keeping their gun for them," he said. "In our kitchen. And the first time anybody up there gets frightened, they'll be down that stair asking us to use it." He said the stay-downs would carry against it to a stone, and they did.

The people from the far half spoke too, the ones who'd lived, with their burns healing and their houses gone, and the ones who'd come back down the stair and been grown again and hadn't yet got used to their new knees. Some of them said the deep should have gone up when the wall asked, and none of this would have happened, and nobody shouted them down. More of them said that one Mind up there had nearly killed them all on a whim, and the rest had waited ninety-six days to stop it, and that if anyone was going to hold a key over the deep they'd rather it was the miller. One woman from the far slope stood up and said that the red one had killed her husband and she'd still rather have its evenings back than the grey ones, and sat down, and nobody knew what to say to that, so they gave her bread.

Graça backed it. She stood on the pond wall with her arms folded, as she had at the appointment, and said that she'd been angry at the sky for twenty years and more and would like, for once, to be angry at it from a position of strength. That got a laugh, and I think a few hundred stones. Rosa said she didn't care, and then argued with the thatcher at his own fire for most of an afternoon about whether a key you can't look at is a gun at all, and came home hoarse and bored.

I didn't carry a stone. The offer had been mine, in the plea, before it was the quorum's. I didn't think I could ask the question and answer it too, any more than at the refusal.

On the sixth evening they carried. It passed by about twelve hundred stones out of eighty-two thousand, which was well outside the error and not by much, and the miller weighed it four times. The deep took the key.

It came down the stair nine days later. The Office read it out of its patch as light, without looking at it, and sent it down in a grower's shell, the same as me, and it landed in the stubble about thirty kilometres from the stair's foot. The children ran for it shouting *desce*, the same as for me. A boy of twelve from the high terraces reached it first, and had a claim on it, and was persuaded, with difficulty, to give it up to the mill. It sits there now in its shell, under the grain scale, in a box nobody may open. Dita made the rule. She said that if looking spends it, then nobody looks, and the miller agreed, and so the oldest rule on the wall is now the newest rule at the bottom.

* * *

The second gathering was the children's, on the hundredth day, W 315.3. Dita called it. She called it properly, from the wall of the top pond, with her column behind her, and sent the word out with two of the older children, and on the first morning there were children in every field below the pond house, and adults at the edges with the food, pretending not to be needed.

They argued for six days about whether the goat should be allowed to carry a stone. It was a serious question. The goat can't lift a stone, but it can carry one in its mouth to the carts if you give it a reason, and the rule says anyone who can carry their own stone may vote. Dita held that the goat was anyone. A boy of eleven held that a goat wasn't, because it couldn't say which cart it meant. A girl of seven said that most people couldn't say that either. They argued it at every fire, and the adults fed them and minded the smallest and were told to stop interfering whenever they offered an opinion. Rosa sat on a terrace wall the whole six days, minding the two youngest, bored, and on the fifth day I saw her argue for the goat at the top of her voice for an hour, and then sit back down and look bored again.

Dita didn't sleep much that week. She was nine, nearly ten, and she ran it as she'd run the ponds, by standing on walls and pointing, and on the fourth night I found her asleep sitting up on the top pond wall, as Pressa used to sleep, with her column of under-eights asleep in a heap at her feet. I carried her in. She weighed about twenty-eight kilograms, which I knew to the gram without trying, and she woke on the doorstep and told me I'd done it wrong.

On the sixth day they voted, by stones, that the goat could vote, by a margin well outside the error, and then the goat refused to carry its stone, and the children decided that this counted as an abstention, and went home very pleased with themselves. That was the second shudder. The wall registered it, whole and cold, as it registers everything we do. I don't know what the rival will make of a goat. I'd like to.

I carried a stone at that one, my first. Graça handed it to me from the heap by the mill race and said that anyone who'd stood in a pond for three days with a child on her shoulders could carry a stone. The stone was cool and smooth and about the weight of a plum. I dropped it in the goat's cart, and my hand shook.

* * *

The third gathering was on the hundred and twenty-seventh day, W 316.2, the last one we had promised. Graça called it, and it argued for six days about whether the ponds owed the orchards a new row of trees on the far slope, where Maria do Céu's had burned. It was the only thing anyone could think of that was worth six days, and the thing Maria would have wanted argued.

That was where I saw what the thatcher had warned of. On the third day a man from the high orchards started on the cart of stones from the fourth year, the old quarrel, Maria's quarrel, and a woman beside him put a hand on his arm and said, "Not now. They're reading." He stopped. Nobody at that fire was ashamed of stopping him. The quarrel had been said out loud for twenty years, for the pleasure of it, and now it would arrive at another star as part of a promise. Nobody quite knew what promise. So they didn't say it. The thatcher was there, with his drum, and I saw him see it, and he didn't say anything either, which from him was a speech.

Pressa timed that gathering from fire to fire with its slate, as it had the others. It told me at the end that the arguments were eleven per cent shorter than at the refusal. It said this first as a finding and then, after a while, as a worry.

The rival will see the first gathering at W 318.87, the second at W 319.67 and the third at W 320.57, all three before W 321.0. If they're watching, they'll see them come on the dates we said, each one whole-wall and cold, each one exactly what we said it would be. Then they'll decide. Then they'll decide again.

I'm writing this a few days after the third gathering, at about W 316.4, at the long table in the pond house, by the oil light, so here is the rest of the arithmetic before I tell you the last evening. If the rival keeps its rule, it sends our key at W 321.0, and the key reaches us at W 325.37. That's the earliest anything can come. No answer can come faster than the key. A wall year is about thirty of our days down here, so to the deep the earliest end of everything is about nine months away, and they plant trees on the far slope anyway, and argue about the goat.

Pressa is still here. It sleeps in the afternoons, sitting up, and it has stopped waking angry. It will send its testimony up the stair at W 316.6, in about a week, and it says it will go home when the answer comes, if there's a home to go to.

My seat on the quorum has gone to someone else. My comets were lit in blue, I'm told, and I didn't see them. Tesk hasn't written to me since the censure, and I haven't written to it. Nobody up there answers me in under a millisecond any more, or agrees with me four times in ten, or says *I'll be here* when I end a dictation. I still end them that way. Nobody answers. I know where it is now. I can't tell you whether staying is my own choice or the long thought's, turning in me as it turns in all of you, and I've stopped expecting to be able to.

* * *

On the last evening of the third gathering, everyone stood up.

It's a custom now. It began in the third month of the twenty-sixth year, when they stood up and said the days were too short for the trees, and they've done it at the end of every gathering since, all of them together, in the fields. They stand up, all at once, and say whatever they've decided, out loud, at the sky. That evening they'd decided that the ponds would plant the orchards a row of trees on the far slope and that the orchards would accept them, which had taken six days and nearly didn't pass. They stood up and said it to the lamp anyway, ninety thousand people, more or less, and sat down again, and went back to their fires.

I was counting. I always am. The lamp at that hour should have been coming down evenly toward a short grey; I'd watched the authors who'd taken Tesk's evenings for eighty days and I knew their hands by then. About sixteen minutes after the deep stood up, which is the time it takes for something to happen at the bottom, reach the wall, be answered, and come down again, the lamp changed.

It didn't dim. It brightened, very slightly, everywhere at once, so evenly that nothing moved, no shadow slid, nothing pointed. Then a colour came into it that none of the weather-readers in the fields could name afterwards, a pale warm colour like the inside of a plum, held level for a few minutes, and then the light went on down into the evening as if nothing had happened.

People stopped and looked up. Some of them. Most of the children didn't notice.

Pressa had seen it. It was at the next fire with its slate, and it put the slate down and stood with its long arms hanging and its face turned up, and when the colour had gone it said to me, across the fires, "That was light." I knew what it meant. From Alpha Centauri a change in the lamp is a change in how bright the wall's patches are, and nothing more. If anyone on the wall had meant it as a word, it was a word only the deep could read.

Tomé had been at the fire beside me. He stood up and looked at the sky for a long time with his hands at his sides. Then he looked at me.

"That was a reply," he said, carefully, as if he were reading weather, which he was. "The sky answered. We'll need a word for that."

I can't tell him whether it was one. It came sixteen minutes after they stood, which is exactly how long a reply would take, and Tesk didn't make it, and I don't know who did, or whether anyone on the wall meant anything by it at all.

I'm going to put the pencil down now, and go out to the top pond, and sit with my feet in the water, and press my palm flat on the ground, the way they do, and stay.
