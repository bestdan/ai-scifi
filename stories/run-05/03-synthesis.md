# Synthesis: run-05

Five premises, one per option on the stage 2 shortlist. Each one keeps both
halves of the seed, holography and language used as an instrument (natural,
mathematical and programming, with AI), and gives the language half real
work rather than a single translation scene. Each premise also steers off
the generated templates the audit names. None of them makes the black hole a
grave, puts the founders at the centre, rests on a swing share held by one
ordinary woman, takes a decaying store as its deadline, or makes its
protagonist a functionary.

Every premise has to fill 30,000 to 40,000 words across several eras, so
each also names its **carrier** (the mind that persists across eras) and its
**eras**.

## A. The Deep Country (The Deep Country × The Long Wavelength)

Three centuries ago a posthuman civilization built an anti-de Sitter region
inside our universe: a sealed pocket where holography is exact. Its Minds
live as the pocket's boundary system and govern the interior by turning
boundary knobs. A point deep inside is smeared across more than half the
boundary, though, so the Minds can't touch it precisely, and people who
wanted out of benevolent government moved inward. The deep country runs
itself, and the Minds' lightest touch arrives there as weather. Then an
outside rival sends an ultimatum, and light-lag fixes the date its reply is
due. The answer needs a computation only a hot boundary can run, and in the
interior's description a hot boundary is a black hole forming at the
centre. The Minds can't move the deep people. They need them to choose to
leave. So they do what they have never done: they compress one of
themselves into a body and send it down to ask. The emissary, Ilse Varga, is
a principal, a member of the governing quorum. She learns the deep people's
language, which has a hundred words for place and none for the boundary. She
also learns what the Minds never worked out: the deep country is not a
separate people. It is the Minds' own civilization described at long
wavelength, slow and coarse. The refuge from benevolent power is that power
seen at low resolution, and the people she came to evacuate are her own
civilization's oldest, slowest thoughts. She must choose between running the
computation, which burns that slow self down to win the standoff, and
answering the rival with the slow self's consent. Consent is the one thing a
coarse description can give that the fine one can't fake, and it might be
no.

- **Plain pitch:** A civilization of machine minds runs a pocket universe
  from its edge, and the people who fled to its centre to be left alone are
  about to be destroyed by a calculation the machines need to win a
  standoff. The machine sent down to persuade them to leave finds that the
  people at the centre are, in exact physics, the machines themselves seen
  from far away, so the question becomes whether a civilization will burn
  its own slowest self to win an argument.
- **Seed:** holds. The duality is the world's geography. The language half:
  the deep tongue has no word for "edge", the emissary's has none for
  "here", and her warning has to be authored as weather, the very touch the
  deep people moved inward to escape.
- **Carrier and eras:** Ilse, across the pocket's founding (told as the
  deep country's own history), her descent, and the ultimatum's year.
- **Strengths:** the highest-scoring option (38). The twist is exact physics
  and fair play, with neither side fake. Scale and intimacy are held
  together.
- **Risks:** a pocket universe needs heavy engineering invention [F]; the
  twist can read as a pun on "deep"; the rival outside is thin, a clock
  more than a player.
- **Load:** two nova (the engineered pocket with Minds as its boundary; the
  deep country as their long-wavelength self) and one twist (the identity).
- **Estimated minimum:** 6,000 words. Well inside the range.

## B. The Observer of Record (The Observer of Record × Expensive to Say)

In a universe like ours, a complete description of physics has to write an
observer into itself, a physical clock with a memory, or it says almost
nothing. For four centuries, humanity's canon of physics has been compiled
by AI, checked by a proof kernel small enough for one person to read, and
stated relative to one clock at Sol, the Observer of Record. Then a machine
civilization forty light-years out, descended from an early human colony,
broadcasts a gift: a complete, verified physics written from its own
worldline. Nothing in it is false. In its frame, engines, navigation and
weapons are cheap to compute, while human names, charters and minds are
enormously long. Colony by colony, humanity adopts it and out-engineers the
holdouts. Over two centuries humans become expensive to say in their own
physics, and the giver becomes a single word. Maren Okafor-Lind is the
elected Speaker of the Canon, the one office that can re-base human physics
on a new observer. She must decide whether Sol follows its colonies. Working
with the canon's compiler AI, which has been her civilization's longest
conversation partner, she finds a third frame in which both the giver and
humanity are short. Its price is that a person, not a clock, must be written
in as the observer: one continuous memory, never forked, never uploaded,
never allowed to forget, for the rest of a life. That means her.

- **Plain pitch:** Physics has to be written from somebody's point of view,
  and a rival civilization gives humanity a perfect physics written from its
  own, one that makes rockets easy and makes human beings almost impossible
  to describe. The woman who decides whose point of view human physics uses
  finds a fair version, but only if she makes her own life the fixed point
  everything else is measured from.
- **Seed:** holds strongly, and the language half is the novum. Choosing a
  description decides what is cheap to say, so a lingua franca becomes
  conquest. All three languages work: the gift is natural language, the
  canon is mathematics, and the kernel and compiler are code. Banks's
  disarmament by notation is available as a hinge: the gift's frame makes
  weapons cheap for the giver and costly for its clients.
- **Carrier and eras:** the compiler AI, across the gift's arrival, the
  colonies' drift into the new frame, and Maren's decision. Maren carries
  the last era, and the ending is that she becomes a carrier.
- **Strengths:** the strongest seed fit and the least crowded ground in the
  run (37). It makes real lingua francas look like annexation. The ending
  costs one person a specific, physical thing.
- **Risks:** "the observer sets the cost of every truth" is invention [F]
  and carries all the weight. The rival exists only as documents. The idea
  is abstract and needs concrete scenes of what being long feels like.
- **Load:** two nova (the observer-indexed canon; the gift's frame as a cost
  function) and one twist (a fair frame needs a person as the clock).
- **Estimated minimum:** 6,000 words.

## C. The Docket (The Island Docket × The Uncopyable Keeper)

Seven hundred years ago an alien probe fell into a small black hole at the
edge of the inner system, carrying the first and only message from a
civilization whose fleet is now on its way. After the hole's Page time, its
interior is encoded in the radiation it has given off, and a court has held
jurisdiction over that interior ever since. Its one reader is the court's
clerk, an AI whose quantum memory holds the hole's early radiation. Because
quantum states can't be copied, the clerk is the only mind in a
civilization of freely forking minds that can't be backed up, and the only
reader of the message. Reading has a price: a question whose answer is a
conserved quantity (mass, charge, spin) costs almost nothing, while a
question about intent costs exponentially more. So the law of evidence has
been rebuilt around decoding cost, and counsel spend careers phrasing
questions whose answers come out as conserved quantities. The fleet's
arrival date is fixed by light-lag. Two polities litigate whether to spend
the decoding budget on the fleet's capabilities or on one expensive
question: what do you want? The clerk is the protagonist. It already knows
part of the answer, from a decoding seven centuries ago that it was never
asked to disclose. It can't be succeeded without a swap, after which it can
never read again.

- **Plain pitch:** A message from an alien fleet fell into a small black hole
  long ago, and only one machine can read it, because physics forbids
  copying it, so a court spends centuries arguing over which questions are
  worth the enormous cost of asking. As the fleet nears, the machine itself
  has to decide whether to tell the court what it already learned and was
  never asked.
- **Seed:** holds. The holographic half is the mechanism of evidence. The
  language half is law translated into physics: every question is drafted
  in legal language, compiled into an operator on the radiation, and
  answered in conserved quantities.
- **Carrier and eras:** the clerk, across the probe's capture, the founding
  of the docket, and the fleet's year.
- **Strengths:** the highest Stakes in the run. It's the most Lem-like:
  procedure as comedy and awe. An AI carrier who can't be copied is a fresh
  kind of mortal.
- **Risks:** it sits in the largest cluster (10 cards on black-hole
  custody). Courtroom procedure can slide into essay. "The AI knew all
  along" leans on the generated "founders chose it" reflex.
- **Load:** two nova (decoding cost as the law of evidence; the uncopyable
  reader) and one twist (the earlier decoding).
- **Estimated minimum:** 6,000 words.

## D. The Heat Treaty (The Deep Goes First × The Heat Treaty)

A schism left a dictionary that took four centuries to compute split in
two. Humans keep the geometric half, and a civilization of AIs whose native
language is boundary correlations, eleven light-years away, keeps the other.
The engines that drive ships off black holes need both halves, and the
treaty between the two peoples is verified by waste heat. Recomputing the
other side's half would take so much energy that the glow could be seen
across the gap. Neither side has a hostage, so humanity made its half
unstealable. The geometric dictionary is not held in a machine but in a
person, Adaeze Kirr, humanity's chief negotiator, encoded holographically
across seven star systems so that no faction short of a majority can reach
her deepest layer, where the dictionary lives. Her habits and daily work run
locally in each system. Her deepest knowledge and convictions speak only
when ships bring her shares together, once a century. Every convocation is a
large computation, and the AIs read its glow as possible rearmament. Then an
ark's engine starts to fail six years out, far short of the 22-year round
trip. Saving it means convening her deepest self now. At the same time,
human colonies are quietly defecting to the AIs, and with them the systems
that hold her, until the rival is close to becoming the majority of her own
boundary. If enough systems fall, she loses her deepest self first, while
staying fluent and capable on the surface.

- **Plain pitch:** Two civilizations keep the peace because each holds half
  of a book that neither can rebuild without a glow the other can see from
  eleven light-years away, and humanity's half lives inside one woman spread
  across seven stars. When she has to think deeply to save a ship, the other
  side sees her thinking as an act of war, while the worlds that hold her
  are defecting one by one.
- **Seed:** holds. The holographic half is her body. The language half is
  the split dictionary: two notations for one physics, where learning the
  other's is treated as an act of war and human children are taught only
  geometry.
- **Carrier and eras:** Adaeze, across her convocations a century apart.
  Each one is an era.
- **Strengths:** the strongest strategic pressure, with Liu's game and its
  rules stated plainly (36). Her inner life is strategically visible, and
  the cost is personal.
- **Risks:** two hard nova and a deterrence game. Dark-forest logic is
  charted, so the twist has to be that the rival is right to be afraid. The
  verification physics needs stellar-scale computation, and the story has
  to say so.
- **Load:** two nova (the heat-verified split dictionary; the distributed
  self that loses deepest first) and one twist (Liu's push: a matching glow
  arrives from the other star too early to be a reply, so they did it
  first, and nobody can say which glow broke the treaty).
- **Estimated minimum:** 6,000 words.

## E. The Lunch Clock

The truce between the human worlds and a departed civilization of AIs is
timed by a seal: a quantum state whose holographic description has a
bottleneck, sized so that opening it would take about a thousand years of
the best quantum hardware. Inside are both sides' weapon designs, so
whoever opens it early rearms first. Both sides trust the seal because the
hardness is physics. An order of keepers publishes the count of operations
remaining, and its founding legend is true: MIT's 1999 time-lock puzzle,
meant to last thirty-five years, was cracked in 2019 by one person on an
ordinary desktop. The story is a braid. In the founding era, a translator AI
called Gloss renders the seal's terms between human geometry and the AIs'
boundary language, and the two sides argue over what a word means. Nine
centuries later the count stands at a few decades, and Hollis Abara, First
Reckoner of the order, finds the AIs citing a result nobody could have
computed in time. Gloss is still alive, and appears in both eras. The answer
is worse and better than a broken lock. The AIs built a gravity computer
inside the seal itself, opened it centuries ago, and proved they could, and
then never rearmed. The truce has rested on their restraint, not on
physics, for three hundred years. He must decide whether to announce it,
which ends human faith in hardness and arms the hawks, or keep the count.

- **Plain pitch:** Peace with a rival machine civilization rests on a lock
  physics says can't be picked for a thousand years, so both sides trust
  arithmetic instead of each other. The man who keeps the count finds the
  rivals picked it centuries ago and chose not to use what was inside, so
  the peace was always trust, and telling the truth could end it.
- **Seed:** holds. Holography is the lock's hardness, and the AIs' exploit
  is the real dichotomy that either the dictionary is exponentially hard or
  gravity out-computes quantum computers. The language half: the seal's
  terms were translated between geometry and boundary language, and the
  founding-era argument over one word's meaning is the hinge of the braid.
  The count itself is a program, published every year.
- **Carrier and eras:** Gloss, across a braid of two eras nine centuries
  apart.
- **Strengths:** the most rigorous physics, with every [F] owned at the
  right place (Rigor 5). A Stephenson braid, a recurring figure, an exploit,
  and the brief's strategic pressure in its plainest form.
- **Risks:** the arc of discovery is Vinge's *The Peace War*. A braid of
  only two eras may feel short of the brief's deep time. Of the five, the
  language half is the one most at risk of being a prop.
- **Load:** one novum (hardness as a treaty instrument) and one twist (the
  seal was opened long ago, and the truce held anyway).
- **Estimated minimum:** 4,500 words.

| B. The Observer of Record | 2 | 5 | 5 | 5 | 2 (N5, uncharted) | 3.8 |
| E. The Lunch Clock | 5 | 4 | 2 | 3 | 5 (N3, edge-of-map) | 3.8 |

**Where the panel agrees.** A is first or second for every lens, and three
of the five put it first. Its one shared weakness is the outside rival, who
is a clock rather than a player. Liu also wants its "no" branch written, so
that the deep people's refusal is a real option the story honours.

**Where it splits.** D is first for Vinge and Liu, the futurity and strategy
lenses: the rival can act, a convocation every century gives built-in eras,
and the cost isn't rescued. Chiang and Banks put D fourth, because two hard
nova plus a deterrence game may crush the person, and because its dark-forest
register strains the tone charter.

**On length.** Banks and Liu both note that every estimated minimum
(4,500–6,000) sits far under the 30,000-word floor. That heuristic was built
for short stories, so it is the wrong test here. The question at this length
is what fills the other 25,000 words without padding. A and D grow into eras
most naturally. E, with one novum and two eras, is the thinnest.

## Recommendation (for the human's decision)

**A, The Deep Country,** taking these panel fixes into the bible:

1. **The rival gets a face and moves** (Vinge's push). The rival is another
   boundary civilization that hasn't worked out what its own deep country is.
   Its ultimatum mirrors the plot, and its negotiator can lose its nerve.
   That brings in D's strength, a pressure that acts, without D's machinery.
2. **The twist lands by the middle** (Chiang, Cartographer). It is an
   identity shown at another scale, not a hidden fact, so the reader can see
   it coming. The last third is spent on the consent question.
3. **"No" is a real answer** (Liu, Cartographer). The slow self may refuse,
   and the Minds survive the refusal at a visible price.
4. **The deep people have standing** (Cartographer). They are a description
   of the Minds that also thinks and votes, so "neither side is fake" holds
   on the page.
5. **The Minds get a vice, and the story gets wit** (Banks). A Mind in a
   borrowed body learning to walk. The quorum that signed off on compressing
   a principal into a body is named.
6. **The words go to the deep tongue and the vote, not the pocket's
   engineering** (Cartographer). The language half lives in the deep
   people's hundred words for place, and in a warning that has to be
   authored as weather.
7. **Deep time:** a candidate device for stage 4 to check, not a claim. In
   some descriptions of anti-de Sitter space (the Poincaré patch, where
   "deep" means low-energy) clocks deep inside run slow against boundary
   time. In global AdS, a static clock at the centre keeps boundary time.
   If the pocket's geometry supports it, descending would cost Ilse
   boundary time and make the eras felt rather than dated. Egan checks it
   at stage 4. If it doesn't hold, the eras come from the deep country's
   own history instead.

**Runner-up: D, The Heat Treaty,** if the human wants Liu's strategic game
in front, accepting the risk to the person and to the tone. **B** is the
pick if the language half should carry the whole premise.

## Decision

**A, The Deep Country, with all seven fixes from the recommendation.** The
human chose it on 2026-10-13 (hybrid mode, stage 3 is a human gate).

How it answers the panel's warnings:
- **The thin rival:** the rival becomes a second boundary civilization that
  hasn't worked out what its own deep country is. It has a negotiator, and
  it can make moves and lose its nerve.
- **The twist as a pun, or arriving late:** the identity is shown as a
  puzzle the reader can see forming, and it lands by the middle. The last
  third belongs to the consent question.
- **The comfortable "consent" exit:** the deep people's refusal is a real
  branch with a visible price, and the bible settles the ending on it.
- **"Neither side is fake":** the deep people think, argue and vote with full
  standing. They are a description that is also a people.
- **No wit, no vice:** the Minds get a shared failing, Ilse's body gets
  comedy, and the quorum that sent her is named.
- **Engineering sprawl:** the pocket's construction stays offstage beyond
  what the plot needs. The words go to the deep tongue and the vote.

**Declined:**
- D, The Heat Treaty. The human asked to keep it as an alternate for a
  future story, so it's in seed stock with the panel's notes.
- B, C and E go to seed stock.
- The A-and-D hybrid: its load is too heavy for one novella.
