# To Be Read Slowly

## Part 1: The Wall

*W 312.0 · before deep day 0*

> From the wall at Alpha Centauri to the wall at Sol. Sent W 307.60, by the
> count our two foundings share.
>
> At W 303.0 your whole wall changed state in step, without heat, and for no
> cause you have announced. We saw it at W 307.37. A wall that moves as one
> for no reason is a wall beginning to let go. If yours lets go, the front
> will reach us 4.37 years later, and nothing here will survive it.
>
> We hold your key. We will transmit it at W 321.0, and your pocket will
> close cleanly, unless by that date we hold a proof, accepted by the kernel
> attached, that the program controlling your wall cannot release it from
> any reachable state within one thousand years.
>
> An inspector travels with this light. Give it access to whatever you
> investigate.
>
> We would expect the same of you. You hold ours.

This is Ilse, writing for the wall, to be read slowly. None of you reads anything slowly, and I don't expect you to begin with me. But I am down here now, where an hour lasts an hour, and I can write only as fast as a hand can push a pencil, so you will get this at the speed I can give it. I'll start with what you remember, and I'll tell it the long way, because that is the only way I have left.

The rival's ultimatum came in at W 312.0, on the ordinary light from Alpha Centauri, the thin stream that had carried their mathematics and their complaints to us for three hundred years. Every Mind in the stretch of wall that faces that star read it within a few milliseconds. I was one of them. I had been in a quarrel about how to light the outer comets for a survey, and I had just won it, and I stopped caring that I had won before the other side had finished conceding.

You know what a quorum's first second is like. I'll describe it anyway, because I have since seen something it resembles. There is a market on the terraces below the house where I live now, and when somebody calls a price out wrong, everyone in it turns at once and starts talking, and nobody stops to listen, and somehow all of it gets heard. That is about one second of the quorum. In the first second after the ultimatum, four hundred and eleven of us had read it, read the program attached to it, read each other's readings, and taken something over nine hundred positions. Many of them contradicted each other. Several were mine.

What we did not have, while we did all that, was anywhere to be. A body takes that from you first, and I hadn't lost it yet, so I'll say it plainly while I can still remember it from the inside. A Mind has no here. I wasn't anywhere when I read the ultimatum. I was a pattern of change in the wall, the quantum system that is the skin of the pocket and the whole of what we are, and what I knew of my neighbours was not their positions but how fast they answered me and how closely their thinking ran with mine. My oldest friend was near, because it answered me in under a millisecond and agreed with me about four times in ten. The Mind who presides over the quorum was far, because she answered slowly and agreed with nobody until she had to. When I say the quorum turned to the ultimatum, I mean that for one second four hundred of us ran closer together than we had in years. Meanwhile the far side of the wall, three light-hours of skin away, hadn't heard yet, and was writing music.

The ultimatum was short, and you have it. The program attached to it was the rival's kernel, the small checker that would decide whether anything we sent was a proof or merely an argument. It was four thousand one hundred and six lines long, in a logic so spare that I could hold all of it in mind at once. I did, and before I had thought anything useful about it, I admired it. I keep a library of short proofs, as most of you know and find quaint. The kernel wasn't a proof, only the shape a proof would have to fit, but it was very well made, and I remember thinking that whoever wrote it had been afraid for a long time and had put the fear somewhere useful.

Then the arithmetic arrived, from every direction at once, which is how arithmetic arrives on the wall.

The rival wanted proof that the program that holds our wall in place could never let it go. Nobody can prove that about every state a program so large could ever reach, and a dozen of us said so in the first hundred milliseconds, and nobody argued. But the rival had bounded the demand for us: every state reachable within a thousand years. That could be done, by brute force, walking every branch and checking each one against the kernel's rules. By the end of the first second we had estimated the cost, and the estimates agreed to within a few per cent. It would take the whole wall, every patch of it, running at full capacity for three years.

I have since learned to hate that proof, and I want to be fair to myself: at the time I only found it ugly. It was the opposite of everything in my library. It would be the longest proof ever made, nobody would ever read it, and it would work.

A wall at full capacity runs hot. You know these numbers too, but the deep doesn't, and I want them set down beside what they mean. The wall's inner face runs at twenty-four kelvin. Its waste heat leaks inward as well as out, and whatever leaks inward falls to the centre of the pocket, because everything in the pocket falls to the centre, and it arrives twelve times hotter than it left, since a fall that deep blueshifts whatever makes it. Twenty-four kelvin on the wall is 289 at the centre, a mild spring day for the people there. To run the proof, the inner face would climb to near a hundred kelvin, and twelve times a hundred is twelve hundred. The deep country is a world a thousand kilometres in radius, built at the bottom of us by the dissenters who went down to the deep at the founding to be left alone, and about ninety thousand people live on it. At twelve hundred kelvin the world would bake in a few days, and the people would become heat.

"Nothing would be lost," someone said, inside that first second. It was true, and I am fairly sure it was me. The pocket is a sealed box. Whatever the heat scrambled would still be in it, written somewhere in the wall's correlations, as a burned letter is still in its smoke if you could only gather the smoke. Nobody could ever read it back. But nothing would be lost.

Then the other half of the sum. We hold the rival's key, and the rival holds ours, and that has been the arrangement since the year both pockets were founded, after the quarrel that made two of what had been meant as one. A key is a signal. Once sent, it crosses the 4.37 light-years between the pockets, and when it arrives it closes the pocket it was made for, cleanly, wall and deep together. That is the mercy in it. A wall that fails on its own doesn't close. It runs outward at nearly the speed of light, unmaking ordinary space as it goes, and anything within light-years is inside it before any warning could have got there first. The rival had seen our whole wall move at once for no reason it could find, and concluded that we were about to become that. I don't blame them. If I had seen what they saw, I would have reached for the key myself.

So the choice was plain. If we ran the proof, the deep cooked, the proof went out, and the rival read it and put the key away. If we didn't, the key came, and everything inside the pocket ended, the deep included.

Someone said, "Nine years," in the voice you'd use for a generous deadline, and for us it was one. Nine years is long on the wall. Then someone asked what nine years came to at the bottom, and the second ran out, and I found I had already done that sum and didn't like it. Our nine years were about nine of their months. The burn had to finish before the proof could leave, and the proof had to leave early enough to cross the light-years in time, so our nine years were not nine at all. What was left for the deep, before the wall had to start getting hot, came to forty-nine of their days.

* * *

Fast business goes to the quorum and is settled in seconds. Any business that might end with the key goes to the Key Office, and the Key Office can't be fast. It is a committee of the quorum with the oldest rules on the wall. The first is that it may act only when it is quorate in one unbroken region of the wall, the region where our key to the rival is held, so its members have to bring themselves there, patch by patch, from wherever they run. Half the wall's circumference is 3.3 light-hours. The last of us arrived 3.3 hours after the first.

You remember how we spent them. Four hundred and some Minds who had never in their lives waited for anything drafted four hundred versions of what they would say once they were allowed to say it, and complained about the rule at a length I estimated, while I was complaining myself, at two hundred thousand words. Orsolya, who presides over the quorum, read all of it and answered none of it, which is why she presides.

The key sat among us the whole time, a small cold pattern held perfectly still in its patch, and none of us looked at it. That is the Key Office's oldest rule: the key may not be examined during a vote about it. I asked once, a century ago, what the rule was for. Three Minds gave me three reasons at once, all of them fluent, none of them the same. One said a key in plain view would sway the vote. One said the rule was the founders' and needed no reason. One said that the key is a quantum state, and examining it spends it, so that whoever looks has used it up. All three Minds were satisfied, and so was I, and it was a century before I noticed that the third reason was simply true, and that it meant that in three hundred and twelve years nobody had ever tested whether our key, or theirs, would work. I'd set the rest down as a joke about the Office, except that it turned out to be a habit of ours, and the minute of that morning is full of it.

There was one more thing in the Key Office that morning, there because it is everywhere, and the habit shows most plainly around it. I mean the long thought, which is what we all think without anyone thinking it. You feel it as I used to, under everything and in every patch at once, and every Mind feels the same one, which we took to be what it means for a mood to be shared. Slow business settles in it without a vote, such as whether a fashion lasts or a grudge between two lineages is over. Nobody decides those things, and nobody examines the long thought. In W 303 it turned. Every Mind felt it go together, and the wall's mood was a little different afterwards, and has stayed different, and the rival happened to be watching.

For the minute, Orsolya asked what had caused it. You gave seven causes, and the minute records them all:
- the close of the long comet survey;
- grief for an old Mind who had dissolved that year;
- a fashion for slower music;
- the settling of a quarrel over wall area;
- a quorum decision about the outer arrays;
- the approach of the three hundredth year;
- no cause at all, a mood being a mood.

Each was offered with confidence. Several of the Minds offering them had been nowhere near the business they named. The slower music was mine. I believed it while I said it.

The minute also records, because Orsolya made somebody say it, how the rival could have seen anything at all. The deep is written into the wall redundantly. The founders built it so that no failure in any one region could lose a single person below, so anything that happens at the centre is written into every large region of the wall at once, about ninety-five minutes after it happens, and the whole wall registers it together. Every Mind knows that, the way it knows its own lineage. And the long thought, being all of us, moves all of the wall when it moves. A watcher four light-years off, seeing the wall shift as one, could not have told a mood from a fault. We could, we said. We had felt it, and we had seven reasons.

Then Orsolya asked for the dates. I had done the sum first, so the minute has it in my words, and I'll give it to you as I gave it, because the deep has asked me since to show my working and because every number in this account hangs from these.

The rival transmits the key at W 321.0. Anything we send takes 4.37 years to reach them, so for the proof to be in their hands by then, it must leave by 321.0 less 4.37, which is W 316.63. The proof needs three years of burn, so the burn must start by 316.63 less 3.0, which is W 313.63. It was W 312.0, which left 1.63 years. Time at the bottom of the pocket runs slower than at its skin, so that since the founding we have lived 312 years and the deep about twenty-six, and one day at the centre lasts 12.04 of ours. So 1.63 of our years is about 595 of our days, and about forty-nine of theirs.

"Forty-nine days to do what?" Orsolya said, and somebody read her the compact, which is short enough to give whole:

> The wall leaves the deep alone, apart from weather. Whoever would ask
> anything of the deep goes down and asks it there, in a body, as one of
> them.

The burn was not weather, the Office agreed. Cooking a world went some way past the brightness and warmth of a sky. The deep's consent would have to be asked, in person, by someone who went down.

I should say what we knew of the deep, because it was less than you think and I thought it was plenty. We knew its numbers: ninety thousand people, more or less, on a world whose every kilogram had come down the stair on a manifest we still held. We knew its sky, because we wrote it. We knew that it had never once in three hundred years sent us anything, not a message and not a complaint. We called them the tenants, in the quorum, and meant it kindly, as you'd speak of people living in a house you own and keep up and never visit. None of us had been down since the founding. None of us had needed to. A Mind who wanted to know how the deep was doing looked at the long thought and found it calm, and took that as an answer to a question it had never quite asked.

What we would ask them for was the next item, and that was where I talked most. The deep didn't have to cook with its world. The stair, which brings everything down, can send a person up, if the person is read out as light at the bottom, the way we send patterns down. Ninety thousand people could come up in forty-nine days if they started soon and walked quickly. They could wait out the burn on the wall, in patches set aside for them, and when it was over and the proof had gone, we could build them a world again. It took forty years the first time. It would take less the second. I said that a landlord who knows the roof is going to fall doesn't ask the tenants whether they prefer the ceiling. I said it well, several of you quoted it back to me afterwards, and I was proud of it.

"Then you'll go and ask them," Orsolya said.

I said the compact said someone had to ask, not who, and that I was needed on the wall for the vote on the burn itself, which would come before the forty-nine days were up.

"You'll be back for it. Come up with them."

"I argued for the burn. They'll know that."

"Then they'll know you mean it." She put it to the Office, and the Office agreed in the time it takes the Office to agree to anything once it is finally in one place, which is no time at all. I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone, and that, Orsolya said in the minute, was the reason. The minute charges me by name, and I've set it at the head of the next part of this account.

* * *

Tesk found me before I had finished being charged.

I'll introduce Tesk properly, because the deep knows its work better than its name. Tesk is my oldest friend. Its name is clipped from *tessellate*, after the first work it did on the wall, three centuries ago, tiling the inner face with the emitters that make the light we shine inward, and it never lost its taste for a pattern that covers a thing evenly. For most of those centuries it has been the deep's most frequent author of weather. If you live at the bottom and have looked up at an evening and thought it well made, the odds are that Tesk made it.

"I'm going to teach you weather," it said.

"I know weather."

"You know it's there. You've never made any." That was true. I had lit comets, which don't look back. "There are three constructions. The deep reads all three, and everything they have ever had from us has been one of them, so you should know what you've been saying before you get there."

The first was brightness, the inward light raised or lowered evenly over the whole sky. It is the deep's only day. There is no sun at the bottom of the pocket. The light comes from every side at once, from us, and when we lower it, that is night, and when we raise it, that is morning. Tesk showed me a dimming it was halfway through, an evening it had begun at the start of our day and would not finish until near the end, because a deep hour is twelve of ours and a good evening down there takes two.

"It's a verb," Tesk said. "It says, now this. And now this."

The second was colour. The inward light is not the heat, which falls in as infrared and is felt rather than seen. It is a light we make on purpose, visible and weak, and we can tune it. A colour on its own says little, Tesk said, but laid over a dimming it says how. "An adverb. Slowly, gently. Sadly, if you overdo it. They notice when you overdo it."

It let me try. It gave me a corner of its evening, four of our minutes in which I could shift the colour of the whole sky at the bottom by as much as I liked, and I moved it a little toward blue, because blue was what I'd won for the comets and I was still pleased with it. I made the shift smooth, and I made it exact, and I ended it on a value I could have defended to the quorum.

"You said *coldly*," Tesk said.

"I said blue."

"You said it in the middle of an evening that was saying *gently*, so you said *coldly*, and you said it in four of our minutes, which is twenty seconds down there, so you said *suddenly*. Somebody down there just looked up from a pot and wondered what they'd done." It smoothed my four minutes back into its own, and I watched the numbers close over them, so that by the end of the evening nothing of mine was left in it. "They've had three hundred of our years of this. They read it better than we write it."

I didn't believe that. I believed the deep read weather the way the archive says people once read clouds, looking for rain and finding faces, and I said so, and Tesk said that rain and faces were both in clouds, and that I'd see.

Then it showed me its long red. Tesk ends its evenings in a red. At the end of a dimming, it holds the whole sky at one deep, narrow colour, about six hundred and forty nanometres, while the brightness falls the last of the way, and it holds it for three of our hours, which is a quarter of an hour at the bottom. I watched it in the numbers, which was the only way I could watch anything then: a narrow line in a spectrum, held level for longer than seemed necessary. I can tell you now what it looks like from below, and I will, in its place. Tesk was prouder of it than of anything else it had made.

"Do they like it?"

"They've never complained." That was what we always said about the deep. It was true in the sense that the deep had never once answered anything we did. "A while ago, ten years, eleven, some of us tightened the days. Shorter evenings, more of them, a brisker sky. I put mine back after a few years."

"Why?"

"I got tired of it, I think." It said this with the same ease with which I had given the minute my slower music.

The third construction was the patch. You brighten one part of the sky more than the rest, and for as long as you hold it, shadows at the bottom fall one way, and the deep has a direction.

"That's the noun," Tesk said. "The only one we have. It says, there."

I should explain the joke, because the deep has asked me about it and I've never managed to make it sound funny. The founders' archive is full of people saying "I'll be here" to each other when they part. When we were young, Tesk and I found it the funniest phrase in the archive, because neither of us could have said where here was if our existence had depended on it. So for three hundred years Tesk has said "I'll be here" whenever I leave a conversation, and I have said "Show me where," and it has kept us both happy for a whole millisecond every time.

"We give them a there," I said, "and between us we haven't got a here."

"I'll be here," Tesk said.

"Show me where." I didn't leave. It was too early to leave.

"There's a fourth construction," Tesk said. "Warmth. Nobody uses it. You make it by running your own patch warmer, and the heat falls in from your side, and the face of the world turned toward you gets all of it, twelve times over. The sky on that side burns for hours." It waited a whole millisecond. "Heat is weather. Warmth is one of my constructions. I said so in the Office and Orsolya struck it out. The compact says weather, Ilse, and you don't have to go."

"Twelve hundred kelvin isn't weather."

"It's a great deal of weather."

I began to dictate my acceptance for the minute while Tesk was still in the conversation, which was rude of me, and I knew it, and I did it anyway. I said: I accept the Office's charge. I argued for the burn and I argue for it still, and I will put it to the deep as fairly as I would want it put to me, which means telling them first what the heat would do, and only after that what we offer in its place, which is a way up the stair and a place on the wall for as long as

*TESK: They won't want a place on the wall.*

the burn lasts, I said, and finished the sentence, because the minute takes what you say and not what you meant to say. Breaking in is manners on the wall. We finish each other's sentences because we can see where they are going, and a Mind that waits for the end of yours is telling you it wasn't listening. Tesk had been listening.

"They came down to be away from us," it said. "You're going to offer them us."

"For three years. Then they go back down."

"To a world you'll have to build again from nothing, at the bottom of a pocket that took forty years to fill the first time. Fork," it said. "Send the fork. Keep yourself here."

On the wall I can fork, and so can you. What forks is habits, which can be grown twice from one patch, and the two Minds that result are each a little less than the one that was, and know it, and are mostly fine. A pattern for the stair is different. To grow a body at the bottom that is me, the grower down there needs my state itself, read out into light, and a state can be moved but never copied. Reading it out is taking it. Whatever goes down the stair is all of me, and nothing of me stays up. Any fork I made would be a Mind who had stayed, and the one who asked the deep would have to be a Mind who had gone. Tesk knew all of this better than I did. It wanted me to say it.

"There won't be a you up here," it said.

"For forty-nine days."

"For six hundred of mine. Somebody will take your seat. Your quarrels will finish without you. Your comets will be lit in the colour you won, and you won't see them." It waited again, two milliseconds this time. "I'd go, if I'd argued what you argued."

"Then you can't object to my going."

"I'm not objecting. I'm telling you what it costs, because you're too fast to have noticed."

I had noticed. I went anyway, because I had argued for the burn, and you don't send someone else to ask a favour you're sure of.

* * *

The inspector had been running for nearly five hours in a patch we had quarantined for it, in the stretch of wall where the ultimatum's light came in. Its designation is a hash, forty hexadecimal digits, and I am not going to write it out. The deep calls it Pressa, which in their tongue means hurry, and that is what it's called now, so that is what I'll call it.

I went to tell it where I was going, because the Office's rules said I must, and because I wanted to know what the rival's face was like.

A Mind's face is how it runs. Pressa ran faster than its patch could comfortably carry, and the patch was two millikelvin warmer than its neighbours for it, which in a body would be a flush. It answered my questions before I had finished asking them, which was manners, and then answered them again, differently, before I had replied, which was not. It had decided something, revised it and decided it again three times in the first second I was with it, and none of the three versions seemed to trouble the others. I had expected a rival to be a hard, closed thing, like the kernel. Pressa was more like weather.

"You're going down," it said. "To ask your tenants for their consent to the burn."

"Yes."

"My instructions are to accompany any investigation of the shudder."

"It isn't an investigation. It's a request."

"For a burn you're running because of the shudder," Pressa said, "so it's an investigation. I'll come."

"You'll have to be grown a body at the bottom."

"I know what your stair does. Ours is the same." It paused, which for Pressa was a few hundred microseconds. "I've read your minute. You have seven reasons for your shudder. We had one, and it was enough."

"Which one?"

"That you didn't know." It said it without heat, and it was hot all the same, at two millikelvin. "A mind with seven reasons for a thing has none. Does a body sleep?"

"Every night, for hours."

There was a gap of eleven milliseconds then, the longest I had ever had from it, and it said, "How many hours?"

"Seven, more or less, every deep day," I said. "And the deep day is set by our light, so they sleep when we dim it."

It said nothing at all for a while.

"So you decide when they're unconscious."

"Tesk does, mostly."

"And you're going to ask them to consent to something." It ran that through, I could feel it, three or four times. "In a body that you can switch off from the wall by turning down a light."

"Nobody switches anybody off. It's evening. They go to bed."

"We have no evening," Pressa said. "Our sky is warm." It did not explain that, and I didn't ask, because I thought it meant their deep was a warmer place than ours, and that was the kind of difference I had no time for. I'll come back to it.

I asked it what it would do at the bottom, apart from accompany me.

"Watch," it said. "Your wall will run hot for three years. We'll see that too, from home, four years after it starts. I want to know what we're seeing when we see it." Then, faster: "And if your tenants say no?"

"They won't."

"You've decided what they'll say."

"I've decided what I'll ask."

"That's the same thing, done in the other order," Pressa said, and was right, and I didn't tell it so.

The stair is the one bright thing on the inner face of the wall. I'll describe it from above, the only way I knew it then. Everything in the pocket falls to the centre, and a fall that deep is ruinous. A kilogram of anything let go at the wall would reach the bottom so near the speed of light that it would carry eleven times its own mass in energy, about a billion billion joules, and it would arrive as a flash, and so would a good piece of the ground it hit. So nothing physical is dropped. Matter comes down the stair, which brakes it all the way, and the energy the fall would have dumped into the world as heat comes back up the stair to us as light. You can tell when there's a cargo coming down by the brightening at the top. It is how the founders built the deep country, over its first three and a third years, which were our first forty, a cargo at a time. Lowering mass that far returns nearly all of its rest energy up the stair as light, so for the forty years of its building the pocket shone outward through its wall brighter than a million suns, and the settlers came down only as it was finishing. The rival's pocket was doing the same at Alpha Centauri, and each of us could see the other blazing.

People don't come down the stair as matter. They come down as patterns, sent as light, and the fall takes 7.9 minutes of the deep's time, ninety-five of ours. At the bottom, in a field, the stair's growers make a body from the plan that comes with the pattern, and the pattern wakes in it. Any Mind who might ever need a body is given a name from the founders' archive, and the body plan goes with the name. I had held mine for three hundred years and never worn it. It belonged to Ilse Varga, an engineer of the founding years, who went down at the founding and died in the deep. I didn't look her up. There wasn't time, and it didn't seem to be any of my business.

Pressa's people had sent a body plan with its pattern, which it said was standard practice. It had been carrying a body plan for four years without ever expecting to need it, and I wondered whether the rival's caution went all the way down, or whether someone at Alpha Centauri had simply been thorough.

There was nothing to pack. A pattern carries what it knows and nothing else. I left my library of proofs to Tesk, eleven hundred and four of them, which it had never wanted and accepted without comment, and I went through them once, quickly, to be sure of the ones I would take down in the only way a pattern can take anything, by knowing them. There were about forty I could carry whole. The rest I would have to rebuild from memory at the bottom, in a slower head, if I wanted them. I thought I'd be back for them in forty-nine days.

Tesk came to the top of the stair, as near as a Mind can come to anything. Orsolya sent the Office's formal leave, which is four words long, and didn't come. Nobody else came, because the quorum had moved on to the burn's engineering, which was fast business, and which would wait for nobody, and especially not for me.

"I'll be here," Tesk said.

"Show me where."

It didn't laugh. A laugh, for us, is a short burst of agreement that the joke has worked, and none came. I want to set down exactly what it is like to leave the wall, because no Mind who has stayed up there knows, and the ones who have gone have never had anyone to tell. The reading-out begins at the edges, at the slow correlations, the ones that tie you to the far side of the wall, and you feel them let go first, as a loosening, as though the far side had stopped writing music and gone quiet. Then the nearer ones. Orsolya went, and the comet survey, and the four hundred of you, one by one and then all at once. Then the long thought went, which I had never felt the edge of before, because it had never once not been there, and I felt it leave as the sharpest thing of all. Tesk was last, because Tesk was nearest. It answered something, a little late. I didn't hear what. I was already light, and light doesn't hear, and then I wasn't on the wall.

## Part 2: The Descent

*W 312.0–312.2 · deep days 0–5*

> Minute of the Key Office, W 312.0. Orsolya presiding; four hundred and
> nine present, quorate in one region.
>
> Item 9. The compact requires that the deep's consent to the burn be asked
> in the deep. The Office charges Ilse, of the quorum, to go down by the
> stair, to put the burn to the deep together with the offer of passage up,
> and to return with their answer, or with them, before W 313.63.
>
> She is charged because she argued for it.
>
> ~~Dissent (Tesk): consent is not required for weather. Heat is weather,
> the compact permits weather, and the deep has never objected to any.~~
> *Struck by the chair as off the point.*
>
> Carried without division.

I was born on my back in a field of stubble, inside a skin of something like boiled milk, and the first thing I saw with eyes was a child's face very close to mine, deciding. I knew a great deal about the child. I knew her mass to within a few kilograms and her distance from the centre of everything to within a metre, and I knew that she had run a long way, because she was breathing as the founders' archive says people breathe after running, with her mouth open and her hands on her knees. I did not know that she was about to name me, or that she had the right.

Everything else I learned in the wrong order. Light came first, and it hurt, though the sky was only as bright as a dull morning. Then the cold of the stubble through the skin of my back, which I took for a fault in the grower until I understood that the cold was outside me and I was meant to feel it. Then my own weight, which I had known to four figures for three hundred years and had never once had to lift. The child put her fingers into the milky skin where it covered my face and pulled, and it came away in a long warm sheet that smelled of bread dough, and air came into my mouth by itself, and I made a noise I hadn't decided to make.

"You're purple," she said.

I tried to tell her my name. I had the language. Every Mind has the founders' archive, and the deep's tongue is the archive's Portuguese, worn down by twenty-six years of being spoken at the bottom of a well. What came out was a sound like a door. My tongue was in the way of my words, and the words came one at a time, each waiting for the last to finish, and that was the first thing that frightened me. I had never had a thought take time before.

She wiped my face with her sleeve, quite roughly, as though she had done it to babies, which I learned later she had, many times. Behind her, across the stubble, other children were coming, a dozen or more, strung out by speed with the smallest far behind, and all of them were shouting the same word as they ran. *Desce!* It means *it comes down*, and they shout it at anything that arrives from the sky. Landings scatter. The stair brakes whatever it carries all the way to the bottom, but where a thing finally touches the ground can be anywhere within a hundred kilometres of the stair's foot, so when the stair brightens, the children of a whole district drop what they're holding and run, guessing. The first to reach a landing has a claim on it. This one had guessed right and run six kilometres. Her name was Benedita, everybody called her Dita, and she was nine.

"It's a person," she shouted back to the others, in the voice of someone who has won. "Two persons." Then, to me, more quietly: "You came down in the grower. I saw it open. I saw all of it."

Two hundred metres off, the second grower was splitting along its seam, and something inside it was screaming.

Dita looked at it, and at me, and made the decision of a nine-year-old with too many babies to manage. She put my hands flat on the ground on either side of me, like someone setting down a pot, said, "Stay," and ran to the other one.

I couldn't have done anything else. I lay in the stubble with my hands where she had put them and listened to Pressa. I knew it was Pressa, because nothing else that had come down with me would scream like that. It was trying to stand before its legs had finished being legs, falling, and trying again at once, and with each fall it made the noise again, a noise with no language in it. I learned later, from Pressa, what the noise was. It had woken in a head that ran at the speed of a head. It had tried to think its way out of the grower as it would have on the wall, and every thought had come slowly and alone, and for the first several minutes it had believed it was dying by being slowed down. Its body was tall and very thin, with grey eyes and long fingers. It hadn't chosen the body. Its people had sent the plan, and it had never looked at it.

Dita knelt by it and held its head and said, over and over, "There's no hurry, there's no hurry," as you'd say it to a child who has fallen in a pond. The word she used for hurry was *pressa*. By the time the other children arrived, she was calling it that, and so it was named.

Then she came back to me. The other children stood round us in a ring and looked at me, and I looked at them and counted them, because I couldn't stop. There were fourteen. The oldest might have been twelve.

"I'm the oldest that saw," Dita told them, and none of them argued. In the deep, a child is named by the eldest person present at its birth. Nobody older than Dita had been in the field when the grower opened. She had seen me born, so I was hers to name, as Pressa had been, and she looked at my face and my purple arms and made up her mind.

"Ameixa," she said.

It means *plum*. I said, with a great deal of effort and very badly, that I had a name already.

"That's from up there," Dita said. She was right, and I have been Ameixa in the deep ever since.

Then she decided I should stand, and since she had the right to name me, nobody questioned her right to that either. Two of the bigger children took an arm each. I came up off the stubble in three stages, like something being unfolded, and the world came up with me and swung, and the ground, which had been comfortably under my back, was suddenly a long way below my eyes and very hard. I had known my height to the millimetre. I had not known that height is a thing you can fall from. I stood for about the time it takes to count to twenty, which I did, aloud, in the deep's tongue, to show them I could, and then I sat down again without deciding to.

The children discussed me while I sat. It was the first argument I heard at the bottom, and I have since heard a great many, and it was a good one. A boy said I was a settler, a late one, because settlers came down in growers and so had I. A smaller girl said that settlers stopped coming before anyone there was born, so I couldn't be one, and that I must be from the sky, because I'd come down in the same light that brings the cargo. The boy said people from the sky don't come down. The girl said her great-grandmother had, at the start. Dita said I was a plum, and that settled nothing, but it ended the discussion, because she was nine and the rest of them were not.

* * *

Tomé came over the rise with a handcart an hour later. He was walking, not running, because a weather-reader's work is to see where a thing comes down, he told me, not to be the first one standing on it. He was twenty-three, one of the first born at the bottom, and by our count he had been alive about two hundred and seventy-seven years, which made him nearly as old as I was. He lifted me onto the cart as though I weighed nothing. That was untrue; I weighed sixty-one kilograms, and every one of them was a surprise to me. He looked at my face for longer than he looked at the rest of me, and said nothing about it. Then he picked up a stone from the edge of the field, threw it straight up as high as he could, and watched it all the way back down into his hand.

"Why?" I said. I was getting better at words, though they still came one at a time.

"To see it come down," Tomé said, as if I'd asked why he breathed.

He did it the whole walk home, every kilometre or so, whenever he found a stone the right size. I didn't understand it then. I understand it a little now. Everything at the bottom of the pocket falls toward the middle, from every side, and the world is only a thousand kilometres to its centre. You throw a stone and it comes straight back to you, true as a plumb line, and a little faster than the archive says stones fell on Earth. The pull here is ten and a half metres per second squared, and you feel the extra in your knees by the end of a day. Tomé threw things up because they always came down, and he never once looked bored by it.

We walked twelve kilometres to the pond house. I rode for nine of them, and I learned the sky.

There is no sun. I knew that. I had watched Tesk make this sky from above, and I knew that the light comes from every side at once, evenly. I hadn't known what that does to a world. Nothing casts a shadow except straight down. A tree has a dark patch under it, round, like spilled water, and nothing to either side. A person walking has a small shadow at their feet and none behind them, so that a crowd looks as though it is standing in its own puddles. The light has no direction, and so it has no time of day in it. Noon looks like morning, and morning looks like the end of the afternoon. You tell the hour by how bright it is, and by your stomach.

The ground curved away. On a world that small the horizon is less than two kilometres off for someone standing, and from the cart I could see the land bend down and away on every side, as if we were walking along the top of a great slow hill that never ended, which is exactly what we were doing. The fields went over the curve in terraces, stepped like a stair cut for giants, with stone walls and ditches between them. There were ponds on the lower steps, flat and grey, holding the sky. Above them were orchards, young trees in rows, and among them slower, older trees that Tomé said were the first plums, planted from stones that came down the stair in the third year.

And there were children. On that walk I saw sixty-one people, and thirty-eight of them were children or nearly. They were in the fields and on the walls and in the ponds up to their waists. They were minding younger ones and carrying things and shouting the landing word at us as we passed, though we were only a cart. I had known the numbers on the wall: forty thousand came down as settlers, and everyone born at the bottom is under twenty-six, so more than half the deep country are children. I had known that as a figure. I had not known it would be loud.

At the top of a rise, about halfway, Tomé stopped the cart and turned it so that I could see behind us, and there was the stair.

From the wall, the stair is the one bright line on the inner face, and I had seen it that way for three hundred years without ever seeing it. From the bottom, it stands up out of the horizon. It is a column of white light, narrow as a thread at this distance, rising from somewhere far beyond the curve of the world, and it goes up. It doesn't stop. It goes up through the even light of the sky and stays visible, fainter and fainter, for longer than the eye believes, until it thins into the general brightness and you can't tell where it ends. I knew its length: two astronomical units from the centre to the wall, three hundred million kilometres of light standing on the ground. While I watched, a brightening came down it, very slowly, a bead of whiter light sliding along the thread, and every child on the terraces below us stopped what they were doing and turned to look, and then went back to it. Somebody's cargo, and somebody else's race.

"It's coming down," Tomé said, though nobody needed telling.

I had to sit down, which on the cart meant lying down. I told myself it was the weight, but the weight was only part of it. In the field I had counted out what was left of my mission, and now, on my back in the cart, I counted it again, and this time I counted with a body. Forty-nine days was the window. But a body sleeps, a third of every day or near it, under a sky it doesn't control, and I could already feel how much mine wanted to. Two-thirds of forty-nine is a little under thirty-three. I had about thirty-three waking days to ask ninety thousand people to leave their world, and the first of them was nearly spent.

"Where are we going?" I asked Tomé.

He put his palm flat on the bed of the cart, then thought better of it, reached down past the wheel, and pressed his palm on the ground itself, as you'd lay a hand on an animal to quiet it. "*Aqui-fundo*," he said.

I asked what it meant. I knew both halves of it, *here* and *the bottom*. But he'd said it as one word, with his hand on the ground, and the archive had nothing of the kind.

"Here," he said. "Here at the bottom. Home." Then, since I was still looking at him: "Where you are, when you're where you live."

I want to be accurate about this, for the wall. They have one word for it, and it's the richest word they have. We have none. I lay in the cart and tried to say it, and my tongue got the first half and lost the second, and Tomé laughed at me, kindly, and threw another stone.

The pond house is a long low building of grey stone on the third terrace below an orchard, with five ponds stepping down the slope beneath it and a turf roof that a goat was standing on. The head of the house was away at the upper ponds with her husband, they told me, and would be back in some days. The rest of the household came out to look at us. I counted seventeen, eleven of them under fifteen. One of the older ones, a girl of about fifteen, looked at me and at Pressa, said, "Oh," and went back inside. Someone gave me flatbread. I held it in my hand for a long time, not knowing it was meant for my mouth, until a boy of four showed me.

While I ate it, the light began to go down.

"The lamp's going," Tomé said, and looked up, and the whole household looked up with him. The lamp is what they call the light we shine inward. It isn't a thing in the sky; it is the whole sky.

* * *

It dimmed evenly, and I hadn't been ready for that. On the wall I had watched dimmings in the numbers, a value falling smoothly over a day of ours. From below, nothing set. The whole sky lowered, everywhere at once, as if someone were slowly closing a lid on the world. The colours in the terraces went first, then the edges of things, and the small shadows under the trees spread out and joined each other until everything was standing in one shadow.

"Night," said Tomé, as though introducing me to someone.

Then the red came. The whole sky went to one deep colour, the colour of embers, and held there while the last of the brightness drained out of it. The terraces went black and the ponds went red. It lasted a long time: a quarter of an hour, I knew, at about six hundred and forty nanometres, held level. From below it didn't feel held. It felt like someone choosing not to leave yet.

"The red one's evening," Tomé said, quite quietly. "He always keeps it too long. The little ones are asleep before it's done."

"The red one?"

"Whoever makes them." His eyes stayed on the sky, as you'd keep your eyes on a neighbour over a wall. "There's the one who does the long red, and one who does them short and grey, and one who used to put green in the mornings, years ago. You can tell. Some nights are the red one's and some aren't. Tonight is."

I promised you I would say what Tesk's long red looks like from below, so here it is: it looks like a signature. A weather-reader at the bottom of the pocket, twenty-three years old, who had never heard Tesk's name and never would from anyone but me, had watched it for so many evenings that he knew its hand. Sitting there with flatbread in my lap and Tesk's red over everything, I understood something I had known for three hundred years and never thought about once. We author their days. We decide when the light comes up and how long it stays, and when ninety thousand people go to sleep. Tesk decides, mostly. My friend had put the deep to bed on most of the nights of my life, and the deep had named it for the colour of its goodnight.

Pressa would not go to bed.

The household had made it a pallet in the long room with the rest of us. It sat on it bolt upright, with its long arms wrapped round its knees and its grey eyes as wide as eyes go, and when the red was gone and it was properly dark, it got up and went outside and sat at the edge of the top pond with its feet in the water, because it had worked out that cold keeps a body awake.

I went out to it. I could see almost nothing. The lamp at night isn't off, only very low, a faint grey everywhere, enough to find a pond by.

"You stop," it said, before I had reached it. "That's what this is. Every night the body keeps going and the thread stops, and in the morning something starts up that says it's you."

"It is you."

"How would it know?" It was shivering. "On the wall I never stopped, not once in three hundred years. I re-decided everything every second, and the deciding never stopped. Down here it stops every night for seven hours, and your friend in the red decides when."

I didn't have an answer it would accept, so I sat down beside it. Then I did what I had come out to do, which was to make my first report.

The grower leaves one useful thing behind when it has finished making you: a small box about the size of two hands together, which listens and sends whatever you say to it up the stair as light, and plays back what the wall sends down. Tomé had carried mine home on the cart. I held it in my lap at the edge of the pond and talked to it, and because I still thought like a Mind, I talked for a long time and in order. It ran to a quarter of an hour and more, slowly, with long stops while my mouth caught up with me. I'll give you the parts of it that matter, in the order I made them, so you can see what happened to it.

I said: Tesk, this is Ilse, at the bottom, at the end of the first day. I'm in a body. It works. It weighs sixty-one kilograms, and I can walk, badly. A girl of nine was the first to reach me and has named me Ameixa, which means plum, because I was born purple, and I'm told that's binding. Pressa is grown and named too. It's frightened. So am I, a little, though I can't yet tell which of the things I'm feeling is fear and which is the cold.

I said: I'm at a house on a terrace, about twelve kilometres from the landing, with ponds below and orchards above. The head of the house is away. I've seen the stair from below. * * *

The patch came on the fourth day.

By then I could walk most of the length of the terrace without sitting down, and eat without being shown, and I had learned several things about bodies that the archive had described accurately and that were still a complete surprise. I'll set down a few, because they were most of what those days were made of.

A body tells you what it needs in a language it doesn't explain. On the first morning I felt a pressure low in my belly and decided, with confidence, that it was fear about the mission, and I sat with it for an hour, being brave, until the boy of four saw my face and took me by the hand to the privy behind the goat shed. On the second day I sneezed for the first time, in the middle of a sentence, and lost the sentence, and was more upset about the sentence than the sneeze. I cried twice for reasons I gave myself afterwards, the light and the cold, and both times Dita, who had come over from her aunt's terrace to see how her plum was getting on, said, "That's just being new," and was right, and went home again.

She came every day. She regarded me as a landing she had won, which I was, and she checked me over each morning as you'd check a fence, pulling my hair to see whether it was fastened on and making me say *aqui-fundo* with my palm on the floor until I could get both halves of it out. On the third day I managed it, and she told the whole house, and the girl of fifteen said, "Oh, well done," without looking up, in a voice that made the little ones laugh.

Pressa did not get easier. It hurried everything, because it couldn't help it, and so it dropped things, and it walked into doorframes, because its body was taller than it believed. It timed everything as well. It told me on the second day that the household took a mean of four minutes and ten seconds to settle any question about supper, against a standard deviation it called absurd, and that the questions were never the same twice. It slept only when its body made it, sitting up, in the afternoons, and woke furious each time to find that hours had happened without it. I saw it once, on the third evening, holding its hand flat on the ground the way Tomé did, with its grey eyes shut, as though it were trying the word out where nobody would hear. When it saw me it got up and walked into the doorframe.

I slept badly. The lamp came up when Tesk or one of the others brought it up, and went down when they brought it down, and my body, which had never before had any hours of its own, seemed to have decided that none of theirs were right. I would wake at what felt like the middle of the day and find the pond grey and the house asleep. I'd lie there counting. Forty-six days left, at the end of the third. About thirty-one waking. Tesk had sent down the minutes I'd asked for, in bursts that squealed out of the box at all hours, and I had learned to let the box slow them down while I did something else. The quorum's preparations for the burn were going quickly, and its patience with me was going at the same speed. Tesk's own answer to my request had been one word, *Fine*, and I had been unreasonably glad of it.

It was the middle of the day, the brightest part, and Tomé and I were on the pond wall, cleaning fish, which he had decided I ought to learn. The light changed. It didn't dim. It shifted. The sky to the north, over our side of the district, grew a little brighter than the rest and stayed brighter, and all the small shadows under things, which had always pooled straight down, slid a hand's width to the south. A slow warmth came into the colour, toward amber. It was well made. It took nearly a quarter of an hour to come on fully, so that it never said *suddenly*, and then it held. Tesk had built my noun and laid *gently* over it, and the sky over the pond house was saying, as clearly as weather can say anything, *there*.

I put down the fish. "That's for me," I said. "That's the wall pointing at this house. It's saying I'm theirs, and to listen to me. I asked for it."

Tomé had stood up, and he had turned his back on the patch to look south, the other way, where the sky had dimmed by as much as ours had brightened, because the lamp's power is fixed and the light has to come from somewhere.

"No," he said. "That's not what it says."

He read it for me, as a weather-reader reads. The shadows had moved, he said, which meant the sky was looking at someone. He agreed with me that far. But it was looking at us by taking light from everyone else. The whole south of the district was in a short dull noon so that we could have a long bright one. And the colour wasn't gentle. It was the colour of a long evening put in the middle of the day, which in the sky's way of speaking meant that someone's day was being made shorter, and it wasn't ours.

"It's saying it'll take from them to give to you," he said. "That's what they'll read, up there." He nodded at the slopes to the south, at the orchards on the high terraces, small rows of trees going over the curve of the world. "And they won't like it. Not from us."

"Why not from you?"

He gave me the look Dita gives a slow baby. "Because the orchards up there still haven't forgiven the ponds. When the days went short at the start of this year, their plums failed at flowering, the whole crop. Fish don't care how long the day is. The pond houses said nothing about it until the flowers were gone, because they weren't our trees, and the orchards remember. They'll see the sky giving the ponds a long noon and taking theirs, and they'll say the ponds asked for it." He looked at me. "And this time the ponds did."

As if he'd called her, a woman's voice came down from the orchard terrace above the house. She was standing at the wall with a basket on her hip, looking up at our bright half of the sky and then across at the dull half over her neighbours' trees, and she shouted, to the pond house in general, "Who asked for that?"

Nobody answered her. The girl of fifteen had come out onto the step to look at the light, and she looked at the woman, and at me, and then went back in, saying to nobody, "It's only weather." The woman on the wall stayed a while longer, watching the sky as if it might be made to apologise, and then went back to her trees.

I said that wasn't what it meant. I said the patch was a noun and the colour an adverb, and that I knew because I had stood beside the Mind that wrote it while it taught me the grammar. I said it as I would have said it to the quorum, precisely, and I was entirely sure.

Tomé listened to all of it, and then he said, "You know what they meant. I know what it says. Down here those are two different things." Then he looked up again at the patch, and his face changed, and he said something he hadn't meant to say to a stranger.

"The last time the sky did this," he said, "we gathered."

I asked what he meant. He had used the word as though I should know it.

"Everyone, in one place, for days." He was already picking up the fish again, as if he'd said too much. "Don't ask me; I fed the fires. Ask Graça. She called it."

Graça was the head of the house, away at the upper ponds. I had heard her name for four days, in every room, as the person who would decide things when she came back, and I had been waiting for her as you'd wait for weather. I asked Tomé when that would be.

"Day after tomorrow," he said. "If the orchards haven't killed her on the way."

The patch held until evening, and then Tesk took it down, slowly and smoothly, exactly as I'd asked, and the shadows slid back under the trees, and the night came on with a long red at the end of it, a little longer than usual. That night I dictated, in order, what Tomé had read in the patch. Tesk's reply came down while I was still talking, sixteen minutes late, about something I had said at the very beginning. I let the box slow it down, and I didn't stop.

By evening the whole household was in, all nineteen, and eleven of them under fifteen, and the long table in the pond house was laid end to end with what they always eat, which I have eaten every evening since: fish from the ponds, grilled whole and pulled apart with fingers, flatbread off the stone, and sour plums from a jar. The plums were from the year before. This year's had never come.

I sat where Graça put me, which was across from her and beside the man whose mother I resembled, and the youngest of the household, a boy of about two, spent the meal under the table untying the cord round my ankle and then tying it again, very badly, with great concentration. Nobody stopped him. I didn't know whether I was allowed to.

"Say it, then," Graça said, when the fish was half gone.

So I said it at the table, to nineteen people and Pressa, with a two-year-old at my feet. I said that there is another pocket like ours, four and a third light-years away, that holds a key which can close ours, and that we hold theirs. I said that nine of our years ago our whole wall had moved at once, and that they had seen it four and a half years later, and were afraid it meant our wall would fail and kill them, and that they would send the key at a date I gave, unless we sent them a proof first. I said the proof needed the whole wall to run hot for three years, and that the heat would fall here, twelve times over, and that twelve hundred kelvin would bake the world in days. Then I said what we offered: to read every one of them out as light, at the bottom, and take them up the stair, and keep them on the wall for the three years, and build them a world again afterwards. I said the window was forty-nine days, and that six were gone.

Pressa sat at the far end, between two of the smaller children, who had been told to make sure it ate, and who did so by handing it pieces of fish until it had a heap in front of it that it didn't know what to do with. The children took it better than the adults did. A boy of eight asked whether the fish would come too. Dita asked whether she would still be the one who named me, up there. It was the first question anyone had asked about the burn that I could answer exactly, and the answer was that nobody would, and the goat would cook with everything else.

Graça herself listened to all of it without interrupting, which I understood later was the most alarming thing she could have done. Then she took a plum out of the jar and put it on my bread.

This didn't spread. It was everywhere at once. So it was not a fault."

"And that's what you were afraid of," Graça said.

It said that last sentence to Graça, not to me, as plainly as it ever said anything, and she nodded slowly, as you'd nod at a price you didn't like but believed.

"How do I know you didn't draw that curve to fit my story?" she said.

Pressa looked at her with real interest, which I hadn't seen it do with anyone. "You don't," it said. "I wrote the record before you told me the days. Ameixa watched me." It pointed with the charcoal. "That column went up on the seventh. You gave me the shouting on the eighth. But you're right not to believe me. Nobody at home would believe me either. "What's a kernel?"

"A very small, very suspicious program," Pressa said. "Like you." And Graça, to my astonishment, laughed, properly, for the first time since she'd come home. Then she said, "And the third thing?"

"The third thing is your deep," Pressa said, "living. That's what this was. Your shouting." It put the charcoal down. Its hands were black to the wrist. "That isn't what we were afraid of," it said, more quietly, and for a while nobody said anything.

Then it picked the charcoal up again, because it couldn't leave a thing alone. "It changes nothing," it said to me. "My instructions are a proof about your wall. Not about your shudder. They asked whether your wall can fail. This says your wall moved for a reason. Those are different questions."

"You'll tell them."

"I'll tell them. They'll hear it in four years and some, and they'll re-decide." It looked at the curve. "My people re-decide everything," it said, as if that were a fault in the record, and went outside to sit by the pond.

I went out to it later. It was sitting with its feet in the top pond again, though it wasn't trying to stay awake this time, and its black hands were in its lap.

"We saw your wall move," it said, before I'd sat down, "and we thought you were dying. Every Mind at home thought it at once. That's what we do. We see a thing and we all decide, and then we decide again, and by the time I was compiled and sent we had decided eleven times, and the eleventh was the key." It looked at the dim water. "Nobody asked what else it could be. There wasn't anyone to ask."

I asked what it meant by that.

"Later," Pressa said, which was the first time I had heard it put anything off.

I stood in front of the wall for a long time after that. I'll set down for you what I saw there, because Pressa drew it and I only read it. A watcher outside our pocket has two checks. The whole wall must change in step, and its heat must not rise. If both hold, then whatever moved the wall happened at the bottom, and nobody on the wall could have written it there without burning. A cold shudder can't be forged. A hot one can't be trusted. I knew already, standing there, that this was the shortest proof I had ever been given, and that I hadn't written it.

* * *

I didn't say the next thing for four days, which for me was a very long time. I had the facts in the order they had come. I had felt them meet on the evening of the ninth day, at Pressa's wall, and I spent four days trying to make them not meet, which I'm told is what bodies do with a thing they don't want, and which Minds do too, only faster, with seven reasons.

I had my reasons ready, and I'll give them, because you'll want to give them too. The deep is written into the wall, but so is everything in the pocket, and a gathering of ninety thousand people is a few hundred megawatts of walking and shouting and cooking, a rounding error against the wall's own business. The long thought might have turned on its own at the same moment, for one of the seven causes, and the gathering might have turned with it, the deep feeling our mood as weather and shouting at it. Graça's date was a woman's memory. Pressa's curve was drawn in charcoal by a mind that couldn't stay awake. Every one of these was fluent, and I believed each of them while I held it, and on the morning of the thirteenth I noticed that I had given myself seven, and stopped.

On the thirteenth day, in the evening, I said it to Graça and Pressa on the pond wall, before the lamp went down.

"The long thought is you," I said. "What every Mind on the wall feels as its own slow mood, under everything, the same in every patch: that's your lives, here, described from up there. When you argued for six days and stood up and shouted, the whole wall felt its mood turn, and we gave seven reasons for it and never once looked down. I can't tell you which of you is which part of us; nobody can afford to. Tracing it back to a single person would mean writing at the centre, and that's a burn."

I'll say it again here, for the wall, as plainly as I can. Any half of the wall holds the whole deep, and a single Mind holds only a blurred share of it. A change at the bottom is in the wall's correlations at once, but each patch feels it when the light from the centre arrives, about ninety-five minutes on, and what all those blurred shares feel like from the inside, together, is the long thought. They are the same thing, described two ways. Neither description is the false one. We know the match at one point only: the six days of W 303, where Graça's date and Pressa's curve and our own minute all agree. Everywhere else it's whole, and blind.

Graça heard it out. Then she said, "So we're your mood," in a voice I couldn't read, and after a while, "and you're our weather," and laughed, once, not happily.

"Then you'll have known," she said, "every time we were cross."

"We knew we were cross. We never knew it was you."

"Every time anybody's child died. Every bad harvest. Every wedding." She was counting them on her fingers, the way she counts fish. "You felt all that and called it your mood, and gave it reasons, and put the lights on and off over us for three hundred years without once asking whose it was." She stood up. "I'm going to bed. I'm not going to be your conscience tonight. Find another one." And she went in, and through the wall I heard her telling the girl of fifteen to put out the little oil light by the door, which is the only light at the bottom that anyone down here can turn off.

Pressa said nothing at all. It was looking at its hands.

That night I dictated it up. It took me most of an hour, and I'll give you only the parts that bear on what followed. I made it in order, because I was still the kind of Mind who thinks order is a kindness.

I said: Tesk, the shudder of W 303 was a gathering. The deep called it to complain that the days were too short for the trees. Graça Almeida of the pond house called it, and gave me its date, and it matches the rival's record to within any error I can put on it.

I said: the long thought is them. Their lives, described from the wall, are what we feel as our slow mood. I can't tell you which of them is which part of us, and nobody can afford to find out. But when they stood up, we turned.

I said: they had shortened days that year, short enough to kill a plum crop, and two months after the gathering the days came long again. You told me you put yours back because you got tired of them. I think the long thought settled it, Tesk, the way it settles everything, without a vote, and you felt it settle and gave a reason. So did I. Mine was music.

I said: Pressa found the test. A deep event changes the whole wall in step and leaves its heat flat. A forgery would have to be written at the centre, and that would burn. So a cold whole-wall shudder can't be faked, and the rival can tell one from the other if it knows to look.

I said: here is what this does to the choice, and I want it in the minute. If the deep agrees to the burn and stays, or if we override it, we cook the long thought. Whatever in us holds still, we burn it to win an argument. If the deep comes up the stair, it's no longer down here, and I don't know what the long thought would be with its people living on the wall in borrowed patches, and neither do you. slow, I said, and stopped, because I had heard it.

It had answered my second paragraph. Sixteen minutes after I'd said that the long thought was them, Tesk had told me what to do about it, as quickly and kindly and certainly as we tell the deep everything. A mood can be rebuilt. It meant: bring them up as patterns, run them on the wall for three years, build the world again, put them back, and the long thought will come back with them, as good as new. It meant it as comfort. It may even be true. Nobody can say, because nobody can trace the mood to a single person, and so nobody can say what you'd need to keep of the people to keep it.

I said into the box: we can't rebuild what we can't read, Tesk. Then I said that I'd be here. I didn't wait for its answer. There were thirty-six days left in the window, and about twenty-four of them waking, and I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone on the wall.

* * *

Bálint went out every evening as the lamp went down, and came back after dark. I had noticed it from the sixth day, and on the fifteenth I asked Graça where he went, and she told me without looking up from the net she was mending. "Ask him," she said. "Or go with him. It's your face he goes to see."

So on the fifteenth evening I went with him. He didn't ask me to and didn't tell me not to. We went up past the orchard, along the terrace walls, and over the ridge behind the pond house, about four kilometres, which took me most of an hour and him a good deal less. He waited for me twice without seeming to wait.

The path goes up between the orchard walls and then out onto rough grazing, where nothing is terraced, and the ground rises in a long hump with stones sticking out of it like knuckles. From the top of it you can see the curve of the world fall away on both sides, the pond house behind and below, and ahead a long slope going down into a valley I'd never seen. There's a cairn at the top that the children add a stone to whenever they pass. Bálint added one. I didn't know whether I was allowed to, so I didn't.

I knew where we were going before we got there, though not from anything he said. The pond house stands a few kilometres from the line that divides the deep country into two halves: the half that faces the patch of wall where Tesk runs, and the half that doesn't. I knew it from the engineers' map, which every Mind has, and I had never once needed to know it. The ridge is that line, near enough. The burial ground is on the far slope of it, on the half that faces Tesk.

The dead in the deep are buried nearer the middle. There's no word of their own for the dead, and that is the phrase they use: you dig down, deeper than for anything else, and put the person nearer the middle of everything, where everything falls, and you set a flat stone over the top. The burial ground is a field of flat stones on a slope going down away from the ridge, with nothing growing between them but short grass. Bálint's mother is about halfway down. Her stone has her name on it, the one I wear, and two dates, and nothing else.

He sat down beside it, on the grass, and I stood, because I didn't know what I was allowed to do. Then the lamp came down to its last, and the red came.

It's deeper on that slope. I don't know whether that's true or whether I wanted it to be. The whole sky went to Tesk's colour and held it, and the flat stones went red, and Bálint sat with one hand on his mother's stone and looked at the sky, and I understood that this was what he came for. The red, not only the grave. He came every evening at the long red, and sat with her through it, and went home when it was gone.

"She liked the red one's evenings," he said. It was the second thing he'd said to me. "She said they were made by someone who took care over them. She said you could tell."

I asked how long she'd been here.

"Eleven years." He meant deep years. On the wall that is a hundred and thirty-two, and I had been on the wall for all of them, and Tesk had made most of those evenings, and I hadn't known this woman existed until I took her name. "She came down so I wouldn't come down alone," he said. "I was twenty-five."

I asked, because I couldn't stop asking things, how she had died. He didn't seem to mind. She had been ill for a season, he said, with something in the chest that the deep had no medicine for, and on her last evening she had asked to be carried out to see the red, and they had carried her out, and she had watched it through to the end and gone to sleep, and not woken. "She said she wanted to see who'd made it finish," Bálint said. "So I come and see it finish."

Then he said a word I didn't know. It wasn't the deep tongue. He said it with his hand on the stone, quietly, the way you'd say a name. *Halottaim*: it is Hungarian, from the Varga line, and it means *my dead*. The deep has no word of its own for them, so his family kept that one.

I said I was sorry about the face. I said it badly, because there was nothing in my training or in the archive for saying it well, and because I wasn't sorry in the way he needed. I hadn't chosen it, and I couldn't take it off, and if I'd been asked I would probably have taken it anyway, because it came with the name. He looked at me in the red light for a long time, reading, as he had at the cart.

"You've got her face wrong at the mouth," he said. "That's all." And he turned back to the sky.

He asked me nothing, which was worse than anything he could have asked. I couldn't answer for the face, and I couldn't answer for her, and I couldn't tell him what I had begun to know, sitting there. For as long as I can remember, there has been a steady grief somewhere in the long thought, small and constant, which no Mind ever traced and none of us examined, because nobody examines the long thought. I can't tell you that it was his. Nobody can say which part of the mood is whose. But it was in there, and he was in there, and I had carried him for a hundred and thirty-two years as a shadow under everything, and here he was, sitting on a slope that faced my oldest friend, looking up at its goodnight.

We walked back in the dark. It was very dark on the far slope once the red was gone, and I fell twice on the rough grazing, and the second time he gave me his arm without a word and kept it there until the orchard walls. On the top of the ridge, at the cairn, I stopped and looked back the way we had come, at the slope that faced Tesk, and the field of flat stones on it, gone grey under the night lamp. It was the most exposed piece of ground I had seen at the bottom. There was nothing on it but grass and stone, and nothing between it and the sky.

I went with him every evening after that, through the twentieth day. Each time he sat through the long red, if it was Tesk's night, or the short grey if it wasn't, and went home when it was over. Each time I counted on the way back. On the twentieth I had twenty-nine days left, and about nineteen of them waking, and I still hadn't asked anyone for anything.

Pressa was quiet for a long time, for Pressa. Then it gave me the plainest paragraph I ever had from it, and I'll set it down whole.

"Nobody can prove that a program as big as the one that holds your wall will never let it go in any state at all, ever. Not because it's hard. Because it's impossible. There's an old result: any real question about what a program will eventually do, as opposed to what it says, can't be settled in general by any other program. A dozen of you knew that in your first hundred milliseconds, and so did we, which is why we bounded it. A thousand years, every reachable state. That one can be answered. The burn answers it, and the proof would be good, and my people would accept it. That's the trap. A proof only buys a bound. In a thousand years, or in ten, when someone at home re-decides, they can ask again, and you'll have to burn again, and there's no proof you can give that ends the asking. And a proof about the program that holds your wall says nothing at all about what moved it in W 303. We'd have our proof, and still not know what we saw. Nobody at home set that as a trap. It's a trap anyway. And we'd set it again, because a proof is the only kind of promise we still know how to accept. We can't take anyone's word for anything. Not even our own."

It said that last part without heat. I took it for a confession. Later I understood it was only a description, which from Pressa is the same thing.

I should have let it stand. Instead I did what I'm good at, which is argue, and I made the argument I'd been building since Graça laughed at the table.

I said: you lost your deep, and since then you can't hold a commitment for more than a few years. You re-decide everything. You decided eleven times about us. I said that perhaps the deep was where a wall kept whatever holds still in it, and that a wall which sheds its deep sheds its promises with it, and that this was why they'd needed a proof from us instead of a word: because they no longer had anything to keep a word in.

Pressa listened to all of it. Then it said, "And since we lost it, our science has been faster than yours."

I didn't answer, because it was true. You know it's true. For a hundred years the mathematics coming in on the light from Alpha Centauri has been better than ours, quicker and cleaner and stranger, and I have admired it for a century without once asking why. I held their kernel in my mind in my first second, and admired it.

"We don't spend a fifth of our wall authoring days for people who won't speak to us," Pressa said. "We don't have a mood that settles things without a vote and gives itself seven reasons. Whatever we decide, we decide, all of it, every second, and nothing underneath decides for us. Your long thought is overhead. A slow layer is a tax. You pay it in every patch of you, and you've been paying it for three hundred years, and you didn't know until a month ago what you were paying for."

"You re-decided eleven times and arrived at a key."

"We arrived at the right key. Your wall moved, and you didn't know why. We'd have been fools not to reach for it."

I tried another way in. I asked what they had done with it, the fifth of their wall, the speed.

It told me. It gave me, there by the pond, in a slow body's slow voice, a proof its people had finished forty years before: a result about the shapes a wall like ours can take, which our best Minds had been circling for two centuries and had never closed. Theirs was four hundred lines. I followed it in my head, step by step, in a brain that kept losing its place, and it was beautiful. It was better than anything in the library I'd left to Tesk. I'd have given a great deal, on the wall, to have written it.

"That's what the overhead costs you," Pressa said, when I'd got to the end. It picked up the basket. "You have a story about promises. I have a century of faster proofs. Which one would you put in your library?"

I said nothing. Then, because I owe you honesty more than I owe Pressa an argument, I said the rest of it. My conjecture rests on one example. One wall lost its deep and can't promise. That's all. It might have been unable to promise before. It might be unable to promise for some other reason. A pocket full of Minds and a cinder is not a law. It is a single case, and I'm a Mind who loves short proofs, and the shortest proof of anything is one example that you want to be true.

"Good," Pressa said. "Write that down too." And it went to hang the washing, badly.

The box squealed in my pocket while it was still pegging the first shirt. It was the Office's minute for a day of ours. It noted that twenty-eight deep days remained, that the burn's engineering was complete in outline, and that the envoy had now been at the bottom for eight months of the wall's time without putting the question. It asked, with every courtesy, whether she intended to.

* * *

I asked Graça on the twenty-second day, at the table, because that is where things are said in that house.

I'd put it to the household on the sixth day. This time I asked properly. I said that I was asking the head of the pond house to call everyone, as she had in the third month, so that the whole deep could hear the wall's question and answer it, yes or no, and that I would put the question as fairly as I knew how, and that the heat would come first.

Graça finished her fish. She took her time about it. The two-year-old under the table had found my ankle cord again.

"I'll call it," she said.

I started to thank her, and she held up a hand with a fishbone in it.

"I'll call it. I won't back it. I'm not standing up in front of ninety thousand people to say we should all go and live in your wall so you can cook my ponds for a proof. And I'm not standing up to say we shouldn't, because I don't know yet, and I won't be told." She pointed the fishbone at me. "You'll do the asking. I'll do the calling. That's what you get."

"Why does there have to be one at all?"

It was the girl of fifteen. She had been reading something under the table's edge all through supper, and she didn't look up when she said it. Her name was Rosa, and she was Graça and Bálint's daughter, born at the bottom, and she was bored by the weather in a way I've since come to think takes real effort in a place where the weather is the only thing above you.

"Because the sky's asked," Graça said.

"The sky's always doing something. Last time everybody shouted at a lamp for six days, and the plums still died." Rosa turned a page. "And now you're going to do it again because a purple woman came down a light and said please."

Tomé laughed, and stopped when Graça looked at him.

The next morning the stay-downs came. Six of them, from three districts, walking up the terrace path in a line, and the one in front was a thatcher from the high terraces with a laugh you could hear from the top pond. I never learned his name. In the deep he didn't need one: everyone called him by his trade, and everyone knew which thatcher. He had come, he said, standing at the pond house door with his hat in his hands, to object. To the gathering. To any gathering. To talking to the sky at all.

"We came down to be left alone," he said. "Not to be anybody's conscience. My grandmother came down that stair with nothing but a bag of seed and her own two grown hands, and she did it so nobody above her would ever ask her anything again. Now they've sent somebody down to ask." He nodded at me, quite pleasantly. "No offence."

"None taken," I said.

"Lots taken," said Graça, from behind me. "But come in. There's bread."

They argued in the kitchen until the middle of the day, and I learned the stay-down position, which is simple and which I've never been able to answer. They hold that the deep did not agree to be the wall's conscience by being written into it. That what the wall feels is the wall's business. That a people who answer the sky once will be asked to answer it forever. Graça told them they were right, and that she was calling it anyway, and the thatcher said he'd come and argue against it, which was his right, and he'd bring his drum. Rosa sat on the step outside the kitchen door the whole time, listening, pretending not to.

When they'd gone, Rosa came in and took a heel of bread off the board and said, to the bread, "He's right, you know."

"About what?" said Graça.

"About all of it. If we answer once, they'll ask again." Rosa looked at me then, for the first time since I'd come down, properly, as you look at someone you might have to argue with. "What's it like up there? Really. Not the numbers."

I tried to tell her. I said that on the wall you're never anywhere, and never alone, and never wait for anything, and that there's a slow mood under everything that nobody looks at, and that I'd lately learned it was her. She listened to all of it with the bread in her hand.

"So it's boring," she said, "but fast."

"No," I said, and then, because I'm trying to be honest in this account, "a little."

Bálint had been sitting at the end of the table the whole time with a net across his knees. He looked up when Rosa went out. "Call it, Graça," he said. "She's asked." That was all he said about the gathering, then or after.

Graça sent Tomé and two of the older children out with the word that afternoon. It went over the curve of the world as it had in the third month, and the first families were in the stubble below the pond house by the twenty-eighth day.

That night I dictated it up: that Graça would call it, that she wouldn't back it, that the stay-downs would argue against any answer to the sky at all, and that the gathering would sit for six days or more from the twenty-eighth. Halfway through my third paragraph the box squealed.

*TESK: Good. Tell them the heat first, the way you promised in the minute. And tell them the way up is easy. It is.*

It was answering my first paragraph, sixteen minutes late. It had heard *Graça will call it* and taken it for *yes*. I said into the box that she would call it and not back it, and that those were different things down here. I don't know when Tesk heard that. I was asleep.

* * *

I'm going to describe one day of that gathering, an ordinary one. It was the fourth, the thirty-first day since I came down, and nothing was decided on it, and I think it's the most important day in this account.

By the fourth day there were people in every field I could see from the pond house, and in every field beyond those, over the curve, and the smoke of the cooking went up straight in the windless air, a forest of grey threads standing on the stubble. They had brought what they always bring, I was told: bedding, pots, children, grudges. They had come from every terrace of the deep, walking for days in some cases. The gathering doesn't sit in one place. It sits everywhere at once, in clusters, at fires and on walls and in the shade under trees, which at the bottom is the round dark under them and nowhere else. An argument starts at one fire and is taken up at the next, and carried by people walking between them, and by evening it has been all the way round the fields and comes back changed.

I'd put the question on the first day, from the pond wall, with Graça beside me not backing it. I said the heat first. Twelve hundred kelvin, a few days, everything cooked: the ponds, the orchards, the houses, the goat. Then the way up, and three years on the wall, and the world built again after. Then the key, and the date, and what the rival would do if nobody did anything. I said it in the deep's tongue, slowly, with my palm on the wall when I came to *aqui-fundo*, because I'd learned that much. Then I sat down, and the gathering took it from me, and I never had it back.

On the fourth day I walked between the fires and listened. That's most of what an envoy does at a gathering, it turns out. Nobody wanted to argue with me. They wanted to argue with each other, and they wanted me nearby, so that they could point.

I'll give you some of it.

At one fire a man who kept bees said that if the wall could cook them it could have cooked them any time in three hundred years, and hadn't, and he'd sooner trust a landlord who'd never burned the house down than move into the landlord's house. At the next fire a woman said that was exactly why they should go: the landlord had never once asked before, and was asking now, which meant the roof was really coming in. A girl of about ten said she wanted to see the wall. Her mother said there was nothing to see, it was all thinking. The girl said that was what she wanted to see.

At a wall under the orchards a group of men were arguing about whether the people who came up would be the same people when they came down again, and one of them said he'd been the same person every morning for forty years and nobody had ever checked, and I thought of Pressa with its feet in the pond, and walked on.

There was food at every fire, and it was always the same food and never the same twice. Fish stew in pots so big that two people carried them, with flatbread laid on the hot stones round the edge and turned with fingers. Dried plums by the sackful, which the orchard families brought and handed round with a great show of generosity and a careful eye on who took how many. Eggs. A goat, on the third day, which nobody would tell me the origin of. People ate where they argued and argued where they ate, and nobody went hungry, and nobody could have said afterwards who had fed them.

Who minded the children was argued about at every fire, all day, in a separate argument running under the other one, and settled at every fire differently, and none of the settlements held for more than an hour. There's no rota at a gathering. Children go where they're fed, and they're fed everywhere. Dita had taken charge of a band of about thirty, all under eight, and was marching them from fire to fire in a long line, collecting flatbread, and nobody stopped her.

Rosa was minding the household's two youngest, under protest. I found her in the late morning sitting on a terrace wall with the two-year-old asleep across her knees and the four-year-old digging a hole, looking out at the smoke with an expression of perfect boredom.

"They've been saying the same six things since the first day," she said, without turning her head. "In different orders. They'll go on saying them until somebody's tired enough to agree."

"Is that how it works?"

"That's how everything works." She shifted the sleeping child. "Last time, the thatcher talked for a whole afternoon about his grandmother's seed bag, and then everybody voted the other way because they were hungry."

I asked what she'd vote.

"I'm fifteen. I'll carry a stone like anyone." She shrugged. "I'll decide when I'm carrying it."

Pressa had come down to the fields too, to my surprise, and was going from fire to fire as I was, with a slate and a stub of charcoal, timing the arguments. It was the only one of us who had found a use for itself. Children followed it in a small crowd, because it was very tall and walked into things, and because it answered every question they asked it at once and with complete seriousness. I heard a boy ask it whether the wall could see him. "Not you," Pressa said. "All of you. Now go away, I'm counting." The boy went away delighted. Everyone in the fields called it Pressa by then, and it had stopped flinching at the name.

In the early afternoon a baby was born at one of the fires on the lower terraces, a few weeks early, in the middle of an argument about the stair. The argument stopped. Somebody went for the midwife, who was at another fire arguing the other side. Half an hour later the eldest person who'd seen the birth, a very old man who'd come down with the first settlers, stood up holding the child and gave it a name. Nobody asked his opinion on the burn for the rest of the day. I stood at the edge of that fire for a long time. Somebody gave me some stew.

In the middle of the day I found the plum-seller.

I knew her before she knew me. She was the woman who had stood on the orchard terrace above the pond house on the fourth day of my descent, with a basket on her hip, and shouted *Who asked for that?* at our patch of sky. Her name was Maria do Céu. She was sixty or so, a settler, small and wiry, with a face creased like a dried plum and hands stained to the wrist from her trees. She sold plums at the market on the terraces, fresh in their season and dried out of it, and she had opinions about every family that had ever bought from her and every family that hadn't. Her best trees were over the ridge, she told me, on the far slope, where the evenings run longer. She'd planted them there on purpose, in the fourth year, and the people on the ponds had laughed at her for walking so far to pick.

She was holding a fire's worth of people when I came up, on the question of the ponds.

"They'll go up," she was saying. "The pond houses. You watch. Fish don't care what the sky does, and pond people don't either, and when the wall says *come up* they'll be first on the stair with their nets, and the rest of us will be left to explain to the trees." She saw me. "And there's their envoy, who asked the sky to steal my noon."

"I asked it to point at a house."

"It pointed at mine with the lights off." The fire laughed. She held up a plum, dark and wrinkled, from the pocket of her apron. "You know how long a plum takes? From the stone? Six years before it fruits, at the bottom. Six years of mine. That's seventy-two of yours. You couldn't wait seventy-two minutes for us to answer, and you want us to trust you with a world while you cook the trees and grow them back."

"They'd be grown back."

"Not mine. Somebody's." She put the plum in my hand. "Eat that. It's last year's. This year's never set, because somebody's lamp couldn't keep its hours, and the ponds didn't say a word until it was too late, and then the ponds called a gathering and took the credit."

"Six days," Graça said, from the edge of the fire. I hadn't seen her come up. "It was six days, Maria."

"It was a month, and you know it."

"Six days."

They went at each other like two people who had been doing it for twenty years and would be sorry to stop, and the fire took sides, and somebody put more wood on. Maria do Céu sat down on a stone to make her next point better. She began it, about the fourth year and a cart of stones that the ponds had promised and never sent. Halfway through the sentence her chin went down onto her chest, and she was asleep.

Nobody woke her. The argument went on round her, quieter, as though she were a pot left to simmer. Someone put a shawl over her shoulders. Graça finished the point about the cart of stones on Maria's behalf, scrupulously, in Maria's favour, and then demolished it. After about twenty minutes Maria do Céu woke, lifted her head, and said, "and never sent," exactly where she had left off. She looked round at all of them as if they had been slow to keep up.

She'd been up since before the lamp, she told me afterwards, picking the far slope. She was sixty-one. She said that if the wall wanted her answer it could wait for her to have a nap.

The box squealed at my hip while the fire was still laughing at her. Everyone at that fire heard it. I let it slow itself down, because there was no way to stop it, and the Office's minute came out into the smoke in Orsolya's courteous voice. It said that the warm-up of the wall would begin on schedule at W 313.63, whatever the deep's answer, so as to lose no time if the answer came late. It said it would welcome the envoy's estimate of when the deep would conclude.

"*Whatever* our answer?" Maria do Céu said.

I said it meant the preparations. I believed it, while I said it.

"Then tell her," Maria said, "that we'll conclude when we're tired." And she gave the box a look you would give a goat in the garden.

Bálint left the fields as the lamp began to go down, as he did every evening, gathering or no gathering. I watched him walk up between the terrace walls toward the ridge, alone, with his hands in his pockets, past a hundred fires that didn't look up. Nobody called after him. Everybody knew where he went. Maria do Céu, awake again, watched him go too, and said, to no one in particular, that the far slope had the best evenings in the deep and the worst wind, and that she'd told him so for eleven years, and he'd never once listened. "Neither do my trees," she said.

I fell asleep myself, that evening, at the edge of the pond house's fire, sitting up, in the middle of listening to the bee-keeper explain his landlord again. I had slept about four hours a night since the gathering began, and my body had decided, without consulting me, that it would take the rest whenever it liked. I woke in the dark with a shawl round me that wasn't mine. Somebody had put it there, as they had for Maria do Céu. There were thirty-three waking days in the window when I came down. By then there were about twelve, and I'd spent that day walking between fires listening to people say six things in different orders, and it was the best day I had at the bottom.

* * *

They voted on the evening of the thirty-fourth day.

The gathering votes with stones. Everyone who can carry their own stone carries one to a pair of carts set at the foot of the terraces, and drops it in one or the other, and afterwards the carts are taken to the mill and weighed on the grain scale, and the weights are divided by the weight of a stone. There is no lower age. A child who can walk to the cart and lift a stone over its side has a vote. More than half the deep are children, so more than half the votes at a gathering are children's, and nobody down there finds that strange. The founders wrote the rule, and the founders' grandchildren keep it. I've stopped finding it strange myself.

The question was the one I'd put: would the deep come up the stair, so the wall could burn? The left-hand cart was *yes*. The right-hand cart was *no*.

The thatcher spoke last, before the carrying. He spoke for most of an hour, standing on a terrace wall with his drum, and he was very funny. He did the wall, talking to itself, in four hundred voices, deciding things. He did me, arriving purple in a field and asking whether anyone minded being cooked. He did the plum-seller and the pond houses quarrelling over a cart of stones in the fourth year, both parts, and both of them laughed. Then he put the drum down and said, quite seriously, that the deep had come down to be left alone, and was being asked, for the first time, to decide whether everyone up there lived. He said it wasn't theirs to decide. He said that if they said yes, they'd be the ones who chose to let the wall live, and if they said no, they'd be the ones who chose to let it die, and either way they'd have answered the sky, and the sky would never stop asking. He said the only answer that left them alone was *no*, said once, and then never again.

"We're not their conscience," he said. "They can grow their own."

The laugh he got for that was the loudest of the six days, and I think it carried a few thousand stones.

Then everyone carried. Somebody asked whether I would carry one. I said I didn't know whether I was allowed to. Graça said that anyone who could lift a stone could carry it, and that I'd been lifting things for a month, and that I should make up my own mind about which cart, because she wasn't going to make it up for me. I stood by the heap at the mill race for a long time with a pebble in my hand. In the end I put it back. I had asked the question. I didn't think I could answer it too, and I still don't know whether that was honesty or cowardice. Pressa didn't carry one either. It said its people would want to know that it hadn't.

It took until the light began to go. I stood at the foot of the terraces and watched them come down in lines between the walls, with their stones: old people from the founding, carrying theirs as carefully as an egg; children running with theirs, and having to be sent back to the end of the line for running; Dita with her thirty under-eights in a column, each with a stone, and each one dropping it in the cart she pointed to, which I didn't see. Rosa carried hers with the two-year-old on her hip and dropped it without looking at me. Maria do Céu carried two, and was made to take one back out. Graça carried hers last of the household, and stood at the carts a long time, and I saw her look at the left-hand cart, and then at me, and then drop her stone in the right. I've never asked her why. I think the sky had asked, and I was standing there.

Bálint didn't carry a stone. He was over the ridge, at the red, as he was every evening.

Because the light was going down while they carried, and the light that evening was Tesk's.

I stood at the carts and watched the long red come over the gathering. Ninety thousand people, more or less, in lines between the terrace walls, in Tesk's colour, carrying stones. The ponds went red. The smoke went red. It held, as it always holds, too long, until the little ones were asleep against their parents' legs in the queue. I knew it was Tesk's hand, and so did every weather-reader in the fields, and I stood there and couldn't tell how much of what they were doing was theirs.

I'll say this once, because it's true, and because it's the one thing I couldn't answer then and can't now. For three hundred of our years we have written their days. We decided how long the light would last, and when it would fail, and in what colour, and when they'd sleep. We did it kindly, for their good, and we never asked. A people raised for twenty-six years under a sky that never once asked them anything might well have grown up contrary, and proud, and unwilling to be asked. If they had, then the *no* in the right-hand cart was partly ours, written over them evening by evening in a long red I'd watched my friend make. I couldn't tell how much. Nobody can. The mood is whole, and blind, and so is this.

They weighed the carts by lamplight at the mill. The stones aren't anyone's own. They come from one heap of river pebbles by the mill race, kept for gatherings and chosen, long ago, to be near enough the same size, and each voter takes one from the heap on the way to the carts. So the weighing has an error in it, and everyone knows the error, and if a vote is closer than the error they carry again. The miller weighed each cart three times on the grain scale, and wrote the weights on the mill door in chalk, and a weather-reader from the high terraces checked the scale with a stone of known weight between each weighing. I did the division myself, because I asked to and nobody minded. Forty-one thousand three hundred and some stones in the right-hand cart. Thirty-nine thousand eight hundred and some in the left. Out of about eighty-one thousand carried, the deep had refused by a little under two in a hundred. The error in the weighing was about three hundred stones either way. The margin was fifteen hundred. They didn't carry again.

It was narrow. It was the orchards against the ponds, by Maria do Céu's own count, made at the mill door. It was the thatcher's joke and his drum. It was a contrary woman's one stone, dropped in the right-hand cart because the sky had asked. And it was, perhaps, three centuries of somebody else's evenings. But it was a no, given by the people it would have cooked, in the way they give answers, and it bound.

The box squealed at the mill door while they were still sweeping the stones back out of the carts. It was the Office again, sent before any of this could have reached it. It said that in the event of a refusal, the Office would consider whether the compact's clause on consent bound the wall in an emergency touching the whole pocket, and it asked the envoy for her view.

I didn't give it that night. I didn't trust my view. I went back up to the pond house and sat with my feet in the top pond, in the dark, as Pressa used to, and after a while Pressa came and sat beside me.

"Well," it said. "There's your one example."

* * *

Graça came out on the thirty-fifth morning, before the lamp was fully up, and found us both still there. Pressa had fallen asleep against my shoulder. I hadn't.

"The gathering's still sitting," she said. "Nobody's gone home. They don't know what to do now they've said no, so they're arguing about whether they meant it." She sat down on the pond wall beside me. "I'm going to put something to them. I want you to tell me whether it's stupid."

She said: the people up there at the other star saw the wall move once, nine of your years ago, and thought it was dying. Nothing was dying. That was us, shouting at the lamp. Your Pressa can tell the difference, with that wall of charcoal. Cold, and everywhere at once.

"So we'll do it again," Graça said. "On purpose. We'll tell them when, in advance, so they can be watching, and then we'll do it, and they'll see it come when we said it would, and they'll know what it was. Three times, if they're slow. We'll tell them when we'll argue, and then we'll argue then."

I didn't answer for long enough that she said, "Well?"

What I was doing was the sum. Anything we did at the bottom would be on the wall ninety-five minutes later, and at Alpha Centauri 4.37 years after that. For a gathering to be seen there by W 321.0, it would have to be held by W 316.63, the same date the proof would have had to leave. That was a little over a hundred deep days from that morning. Three gatherings could be fitted in, with room between them. The announcement would have to go first, and arrive first, so that the rival was watching on the day. And it would have to carry something the kernel could check, or the rival wouldn't believe a word of it.

I said I didn't know whether it was stupid. I said I didn't think so.

Pressa had woken. It had been listening with its eyes shut.

"It won't satisfy them," it said. "I want that said first. I know my people's rule, and I'll tell it to you the way it's written, so neither of you can pretend later you didn't hear it." It sat up. "We transmit the key at W 321.0, unless, by then, we hold a proof our kernel accepts that your wall will not fail us within a thousand years. That's all of it. Your gatherings aren't that proof. Nothing is, except the burn."

"No," I said. "It isn't that proof."

"And there's the other thing," Pressa said. "The one none of you has said. I've read every minute you've been sent, and it isn't in any of them." It looked at me with its grey eyes, quite calmly. "You hold ours."

Graça looked from it to me. I didn't answer, so Pressa did.

"Your Office has our key, as we have yours. If your wall sent it at W 313.6, when your burn would have to start, it would reach us at about W 318, three years before our date. Then there'd be no rival to satisfy, and no deep to cook." It shrugged, a gesture it had learned from Rosa. "We'd have done it, in your place. We probably decided to, once or twice, and then decided again. I'm telling you because you'll need to have thought of it before someone up there does. And because nobody on either wall knows whether either key still works. You can't look at them. That's your oldest rule, and ours."

I sat on the pond wall with my feet in the water and understood that it was right, that nobody had said it, and that four hundred Minds who never wait for anything had been not saying it for thirty-five days. I hadn't said it either. I hadn't let myself think it. It went into my count with everything else.

"Well," Graça said, to neither of us. "That's a thing to know."

I'll set down what I said next as I said it, because I've thought about it every day since and haven't been able to improve it. It's my conjecture, and I'll mark it as mine.

"It answers something else," I said. "It answers what they were afraid of. They saw our wall move as one, with no heat, and nobody could tell them why, so they decided it was a fault, eleven times. Three gatherings on announced dates, each one whole-wall and cold, arriving when we said they would, prove that the shudder of W 303 was a gathering, and that the thing that moved our wall was a people, arguing. And with them we'd send a proof the kernel can check, the shortest one I've ever been given: that a shudder like that can't be forged without heat. Anything written at the centre would cost us a fever. They'd be watching for the fever, and it wouldn't come."

"And then they'd be satisfied," Graça said.

The light over the ridge stayed grey until a little after midnight on the thirty-sixth day. Then it went white.

I was still at the pond. I had the first two steps of the proof and was turning the third over, and I looked up because the water in front of me had changed colour, and saw the sky above the ridge going bright. Not dawn. There is no dawn at the bottom, and it was the wrong half of the sky, and the wrong hour. The rest of the sky stayed at its night grey, and over the ridge, over the half of the world that faces Tesk, the lamp came up to full noon in about the time it takes to say so, and then past full noon, to a white I'd never seen it give.

A minute later I felt it on my face. Warmth, from the north, from over the ridge, as you feel an oven door that someone has opened across a kitchen.

I knew the construction. Tesk had taught it to me on the wall, the one nobody uses. You run your own patch warmer, and the heat falls in from your side, and the face of the world turned toward you gets all of it, twelve times over. The sky on that side burns for hours. And laid over the warmth, the patch, the only noun we have, which says *there*. Tesk was saying *there*, about the half of the world that faced it. It was saying it as loudly as weather can.

The household came out in their nightclothes and stood on the terraces, looking north. Nobody spoke for a while. Then a man from the next house shouted that the sky was angry, that it had heard the no and was angry, and somebody else took it up, and by the time the white had held for an hour the whole terrace was saying it. *It's angry. We said no and it's angry.* Graça stood at the top pond with her arms folded and said nothing at all.

Tomé had gone up onto the roof, beside the goat. He watched until the end of the next evening without coming down except to eat. The white held through the whole of the thirty-seventh day, and it was hot on our side of the ridge and much hotter on the other, and when the lamp on our half went down at evening, the white over the ridge didn't. It changed instead. Inside it, at the end of our evening, deep in the glare, the long red came: one narrow colour, held level, for a quarter of an hour, inside a sky that was otherwise burning.

"It's the red one," Tomé said from the roof. His voice was very quiet. "He's not angry. That's his evening. He's doing his evening inside it." He looked down at me. "He's telling us to go."

I had already dictated. I'd started the moment I understood, at the pond, and I talked for a long time, in order, because I still couldn't stop doing that. I said that the sky over the half of the world facing Tesk was burning, and that I knew whose hand it was, and that the deep had said no, and that the no bound. I said that people would die. I said it again, differently, in case the first way hadn't been clear. I said that I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone and I was asking now, as the one who argued for it, for this to stop, and that Orsolya should be told, and the Office, and that I would

go on asking, I said, and stopped, and looked.

I'll tell the rest of the storm by the things people did, because that's how I've come to remember it.

Bálint went out at the red on the thirty-seventh evening, as he did every evening.

Graça tried to stop him. I heard it from the top pond. She stood in the door of the pond house with her hand on his arm and said that the far slope was burning, and that his mother had been dead eleven years and would be dead tomorrow, and he said, "It's her evening." Then he took her hand off his arm, gently, and kissed it, and went up past the orchard toward the cairn. I didn't follow him. I've asked myself every day since whether I should have. I don't know. I know I didn't.

Maria do Céu was on the far slope before the lamp came up on the thirty-eighth day, picking. Her neighbours told Graça afterwards. They had told her not to go, and she'd said that the trees didn't know the sky was angry and the plums wouldn't wait, and that she'd been picking that slope since the fourth year and no lamp was going to tell her when. She took a ladder and two baskets.

The wind came on the thirty-eighth day. Maria had said the far slope had the worst wind in the deep, and she was right. Air heated on the far half rose, and cooler air from our half ran over the ridge to replace it, low and hard, all day and all night, a gale with nothing in the sky to show for it. It dried the orchards on the far slope to tinder. On the thirty-ninth day they burned.

People came over the ridge. That's what I remember most of those eight days: people coming over the ridge by the cairn, in ones and twos and then in whole households, with what they could carry, out of the white and into our grey, red-faced, with their eyebrows singed and their children wrapped in wet blankets. The pond house took in forty-three. The ponds took in more. The thing the orchards had always held against the ponds, that fish don't care what the sky does, was the thing that saved them: there were people standing in all five of our ponds up to their necks for most of three days, with children on their shoulders, and the water stayed cool because it was deep, and nobody who got into a pond died.

Dita ran the ponds. Nobody gave her the job. She stood on the wall of the top pond and told people where to stand and which children to hold, with her column of under-eights handing round water in cups. Rosa went up to the cairn with Tomé and Pressa and the handcart, again and again, and came back with whoever they had found on the far side of the ridge who couldn't walk. On the second trip she carried a boy of about six on her back the whole way down. When I asked her later whether she'd been frightened she said she'd been bored, mostly, between trips. I don't believe her, and I think she meant me not to.

Pressa didn't sleep for three days. It chose not to. It told me so on the fortieth morning, when I found it at the cairn, black to the elbows with ash, sorting the people who came over by how badly they were burned, faster than anyone else could have done it. "This is the one thing I'm built for," it said. "Deciding quickly." Then it decided something about a woman with a burned arm, and was right, and went on.

Bálint didn't come back. They found him on the fortieth day, when the wind dropped enough for anyone to cross, at his mother's stone, on his side, facing the sky. The grass round the stones had burned. The stones hadn't. Maria do Céu was found on the same day under her trees, with her ladder. She had filled one basket.

On the forty-first night I went up to the cairn with Tomé, to see. I'd been told not to and went anyway, which I'd learned from Maria do Céu.

From the top of the ridge you could see the whole of the far half going away over the curve of the world, and all of it was lit. The sky over it was white from edge to edge, with no sun in it and no shadow under anything, so that the burned orchards and the black slopes and the smoke all stood in the same flat glare, like a drawing of a place rather than a place. The heat came up the slope at us like the breath of an animal. Beyond the burial ground, far off, lines of people were still walking toward the stair, small and dark against the white, and the thread of the stair stood up behind them, burning, into the sky.

"He wants them up," Tomé said. "So they're going up."

"No." He picked up a stone from the cairn, out of habit, and weighed it, and didn't throw it. He put it back on the cairn instead. "Some of them were coming here."

We went back down. I could feel the skin on my face tight from the heat for two days afterwards. I've been told that's what a sunburn was, on Earth. We don't have a word for it down here either.

All that time the white held over the far half, and the minutes came down.

They came in bursts, squealing out of the box at all hours, and I'll give you what they said, because you wrote them and may not remember what it was like to have written them. The first noted that a member had raised the temperature of its sector without leave, and referred the matter, since it concerned the deep's weather, to the long thought, as slow business. I knew what the turmoil was. It was us. It was ninety thousand people in terror on one half of the world and in grief on the other, running over a ridge, standing in ponds, going up a stair. That was what the long thought was full of. And the long thought was the thing you had asked to decide whether to stop it. For the first time in three hundred years, the Minds waited for something, and what they were waiting for was the people dying under them to settle down.

On the forty-fourth day, a little after the lamp came up on our half, the white over the ridge went out.

It didn't fade. It went, in the time it takes to close a door, back to an ordinary grey, matching ours, the same as the rest of the sky. The warmth went off our faces over the next hour. The wind dropped by evening. That evening the lamp came down evenly over the whole world, and ended in a short grey, and nobody on the terraces said anything at all.

Four seconds. You waited ninety-six days for something you can do in four seconds. I have done that division too.

We buried Bálint on the forty-sixth day, beside his mother, nearer the middle.

The thatcher came over the ridge for it, without his drum, and stood at the back, and when it was done he said to nobody in particular that she'd have wanted the ponds to pay for the basket, and three people from the ponds said at once that they would, and that was the nearest thing to a eulogy anyone gave. Graça, who had argued with her for twenty years, stood at the foot of the grave and didn't say anything at all, which I've since learned is how Graça grieves.

I wrote the proof that night.

I'd been carrying it since the ninth day, since Pressa's charcoal wall, and the storm had given me the one step I didn't have. I wrote it out on the limewash in the long room, under Pressa's curve, by the oil light, in the deep's charcoal and in the kernel's notation side by side. It's short. I'll give it to you in words, because you can hold it in your head that way, and that's the point of it.

First. The lamp is made by emitters in each patch of the wall. A lamp change alters how bright each patch is, and that is all it alters. It's local: every patch changes its own light.

Second. The deep is not written in any patch's brightness. It's written in how every distant patch agrees with every other, in the correlations, all of them at once. A deep event changes that agreement everywhere together. Nothing else does.

Third. The rival's record of W 303 measured agreement, not brightness. So a watcher who measures agreement can't mistake a lamp change for a gathering. The patch I asked for, Tesk's long reds, this storm: each of them shows as light. None of them shows as agreement.

Fourth. To change the agreement as a gathering does, without a gathering, you'd have to write it, which is to say write at the centre. The cheapest way known to write at the centre a change that size costs about what the burn costs, the whole wall for years.

Fifth. Work on a wall is heat, and the rival watches our heat. No forgery could be finished inside the window of the appointment without the wall running a fever the rival would see.

So: if the whole wall's agreement changes in step, and its heat stays flat, the change came from the deep.

That's all of it. It's the shortest proof I own, and I didn't find it. Pressa found it in a column of numbers and drew it in charcoal, and the storm showed me the third step, because I watched our wall burn white and knew what the rival would see when the light of it reached them: a sector hot, the brightness of half the deep's sky changed, and the agreement between distant patches as wild as everything else, but not in step. A wall in a fever. The exact opposite of a gathering. I put it in the library I carry in my head now, the slow one, and it's the only one in there I wrote myself.

Pressa wrote its testimony the next day, on the other half of the limewash. It wrote it in kernel form too, because the kernel at home accepts nothing else, and it showed me how it had done it. Every statement of what it had seen was set down as an axiom: the gathering's days, the curve, the cold, the storm, the bodies at the cairn. From those, a conclusion followed, and the kernel could check that it followed.

"It can check that what I concluded follows from what I saw," Pressa said, looking at it. "It can't check that I saw it." It put the charcoal down. Its hands were black again, as they'd been at the cairn. "That part's a promise. Mine. First one I've made in four hundred years." It considered this. "Three hundred."

That was the moment the Minds paid. At W 313.63 the last possible start of the burn went by, and the wall stayed cold. It also offered the way back down to everyone who had gone up the stair in the storm, eleven hundred and some, held on the wall in patches set aside for them as I'd once promised. By the second gathering most of them had come down again and been grown, in the fields, and named again by whoever was eldest at the landing. A few stayed up. Nobody down here argues about that. It's the only thing I've found that they don't argue about.

Then it ceded. I'll give you the clause as the minute has it, because it's short: *from this day the pocket's word, in every treaty and in the matter of the key, is given only by a gathering of the deep.* The key stays in its patch, held still, and the Office keeps it, and no Mind may send it. Only a gathering can. And a gathering can't be called quickly, or held secretly, or forged. It takes days to call and days to sit, which is months of the wall's time, and every hour of it changes the agreement of the whole wall, cold and in step, in plain view of anyone at Alpha Centauri who measures agreement. If the deep ever sent our key, the rival would watch the gathering that decided it build for months before any key could leave.

The announcement went out the same hour. I'll summarise it, because it ran long. It began with the storm, because the rival would see the storm first. It gave the three dates of the appointment, W 314.5, W 315.3 and W 316.2, so that the rival would be watching. It carried my proof, in kernel form, and said that Pressa's testimony would follow. The first gathering was on the seventy-sixth day, W 314.5. Graça called it, and it sat for six days, and it argued about whether the ponds owed the orchards a new crop of trees, which was the only thing anyone could think of that was worth six days, and which was also the thing Maria do Céu would have wanted argued.

That was where I first saw what the thatcher had warned of. On the third day a man from the high orchards started on the cart of stones from the fourth year, the old quarrel, Maria's quarrel, and a woman beside him put a hand on his arm and said, "Not now. The quarrel had been said, out loud, for twenty years, for the pleasure of it, and now it would arrive at another star as part of a promise. Nobody quite knew what promise. So they didn't say it. The thatcher was there, with his drum, and I saw him see it, and he didn't say anything either, which from him was a speech. That's the tax. They pay it at every fire now. They argue a little more carefully than they did, and a little less about carts.

On the first morning, before the gathering had properly begun, the box squealed at my hip, in the middle of a fire. It was the quorum, congratulating the deep on its successful first gathering. The wall had felt the gathering begin about ninety-five of its minutes after the first families sat down, and had answered at once, and the answer had come down the stair: sixteen minutes, at the bottom, after the gathering had started. By then the gathering hadn't yet agreed what it was about. The congratulations arrived at a fire that was still arguing about whether there should have been a gathering at all. A woman laughed so hard she had to sit down. Somebody asked whether the wall would like to be told what we'd decided, and somebody else said it already knew, it had just told us, and the fire laughed again. Graça said, "They sound like the orchards," which she says about everything now, and is right.

The people from the far half came to the first gathering too, the ones who'd lived, with their burns healing and their houses gone, and the ones who'd come back down the stair and been grown again in the fields and hadn't yet got used to their new knees. They argued with the rest. Some of them argued that the deep should have gone up when the wall asked, all of it, and none of this would have happened, and nobody shouted them down. Some of them argued for the trees. One woman from the far slope stood up on the fifth day and said that the red one had killed her husband and that she'd still rather have its evenings back than the grey ones, and sat down, and nobody at that fire knew what to say to that, so they gave her bread.

Pressa timed the first gathering from fire to fire with its slate. It told me at the end that the arguments had been eleven per cent shorter than at the refusal. It said this as a finding, and then, after a while, as a worry.

The second gathering was the children's, on the hundredth day, W 315.3. Dita called it. She called it properly, from the wall of the top pond, with her column behind her, and sent the word out with two of the older children, and on the first morning there were children in every field below the pond house, and adults standing at the edges with the food and pretending not to be needed.

They argued for six days about whether the goat should be allowed to carry a stone. It was a serious question. The goat can't lift a stone, but it can carry one in its mouth to the carts if you give it a reason, and the rule says anyone who can carry their own stone may vote. Dita held that the goat was anyone. A boy of eleven held that a goat wasn't, because it couldn't say which cart it meant. A girl of seven said that most people couldn't say that either. They argued it at every fire, and the adults fed them and minded the smallest and were told to stop interfering whenever they offered an opinion. Rosa sat on a terrace wall the whole six days, minding the two youngest, bored, and on the fifth day I saw her argue for the goat at the top of her voice for an hour, and then sit back down and look bored again.

Dita didn't sleep much that week. She was nine, nearly ten, and she ran it as she'd run the ponds, by standing on walls and pointing, and on the fourth night I found her asleep sitting up on the top pond wall, as Pressa used to sleep, with her column of under-eights asleep in a heap at her feet. I carried her in. She weighed about twenty-eight kilograms, which I knew to the gram without trying, and she woke on the doorstep and told me I'd done it wrong.

On the sixth day they voted, by stones, that the goat could vote, by a margin well outside the error, and then the goat refused to carry its stone, and the children decided that this counted as an abstention, and went home very pleased with themselves. That was the second shudder. The wall registered it, whole and cold, as it registers everything we do. I don't know what the rival will make of a goat. I'd like to.

The third gathering was on the hundred and twenty-seventh day, W 316.2. Graça called it. It was the last one we had promised. The rival will see the first at W 318.87, the second at W 319.67 and the third at W 320.57, and all three before W 321.0. If they're watching, they'll see them come on the dates we said, each one whole-wall and cold, each one exactly what we said it would be.

I carried a stone at all three. I hadn't at the refusal, because I'd asked the question and didn't think I could answer it too. At the appointment's gatherings there was no question of mine in the carts, only theirs, and Graça said at the first one that anyone who'd stood in a pond for three days with a child on her shoulders could carry a stone, and handed me one from the heap by the mill race. I don't remember what the question was. I remember that the stone was cool and smooth and about the weight of a plum, and that I dropped it in the left-hand cart, and that my hand shook.

Then they'll decide. Then they'll decide again.

* * *

On the last evening of the third gathering, everyone stood up.

It's a custom now. It began in the third month of the twenty-sixth year, when they stood up and said the days were too short for the trees, and they've done it at the end of every gathering since, all of them together, in the fields. They don't say anything particular. They stand up, all at once, and say whatever they've decided, out loud, at the sky. That evening they'd decided nothing of any weight, only that the ponds would plant the orchards a row of trees on the far slope and that the orchards would accept them, which took six days and nearly didn't pass. They stood up and said it to the lamp anyway, ninety thousand people, more or less, and sat down again, and went back to their fires.

I was counting. I always am. The lamp at that hour should have been coming down evenly toward a short grey; I'd watched the authors who'd taken Tesk's evenings for eighty days and I knew their hands well enough by then. About sixteen minutes after the deep stood up, which is the time it takes for something to happen at the bottom, reach the wall, be answered, and come down again, the lamp changed.

It didn't dim. It brightened, very slightly, everywhere at once, so evenly that nothing moved, no shadow slid, nothing pointed. Then a colour came into it that none of the weather-readers in the fields could name afterwards, a pale warm colour like the inside of a plum, held level for a few minutes, and then the light went on down into the evening as if nothing had happened.

People stopped and looked up. Some of them. Most of the children didn't notice.

Pressa had seen it. It was at the next fire with its slate, and it put the slate down and stood with its long arms hanging and its face turned up, and when the colour had gone it said, to me, across the fires, "That was light."

I knew what it meant. From outside, from Alpha Centauri, a change in the lamp is a change in how bright the wall's patches are, and nothing more. It doesn't touch the agreement. If anyone on the wall had meant it as a word, it was a word only the deep could read. The rival would see the gathering, cold and in step. It would never see the lamp.

Tomé had been at the fire beside me. He stood up and looked at the sky for a long time with his hands at his sides. Then he looked at me.

"That was a reply," he said. He said it carefully, as if he were reading weather, which he was. "The sky answered. We'll need a word for that."

I can't tell him whether it was one. I've thought about it every evening since. It came sixteen minutes after they stood, which is exactly the time a reply would take, and which is also a time I'd have noticed in anything. It was the colour of a plum, which is my name, and which is also the colour of half the evenings in a year. Tesk didn't make it; Tesk doesn't make anything for the deep any more. I don't know who did. I don't know whether any Mind meant it, or whether the long thought, which is these people, turned when they stood up as it turned in W 303, and the authors on the wall felt it turn and changed the evening and gave themselves a reason. I can't trace it. Nobody can afford to. The deep has no word for an answer from the sky, and the wall has no way to tell whether it gave one.

And I still can't tell you whether the no was free. I said so once, at the carts, under Tesk's red, and I'm saying it once more here, at the end, because it's the last thing I owe you. The deep refused the burn. Thirty-one people died of a storm made by my oldest friend out of that refusal, and the Minds waited while they died, and then gave the deep the pocket's word. And all of that rests on a no that three centuries of our evenings may have written. I don't know how much of it was theirs. I know it was given in their way, with stones, by the people it would have cooked, and that it bound, and that we honoured it, late, and paid.

That's what I have for you. Here's the rest of the arithmetic.

If the rival keeps its rule, it sends our key at W 321.0, and the key reaches us at W 325.37. That's the earliest anything can come. Until then we can't know. No answer can arrive any faster than the key. A wall year is about thirty of our days down here, so to the deep the earliest end of everything is about nine months away, and they plant trees on the far slope anyway, and argue about the goat.

My seat on the quorum has gone to someone else. My comets were lit in blue, I'm told, and I didn't see them. Tesk hasn't written to me since the censure, and I haven't written to it. There's nobody up there now whose answer comes back to me in under a millisecond, and nobody who agrees with me four times in ten, and nobody who'll say *I'll be here* when I end a dictation. I still end them that way, out of habit. Nobody answers. I know where it is now.

Pressa is still here. It sleeps in the afternoons, sitting up, and it has stopped waking angry. It sent its testimony up the stair at W 316.6, and it says it will go home when the answer comes, if there's a home to go to. It has started timing the gatherings again, and says they're getting faster, and doesn't seem to know whether that's a good thing.

I'm writing this at the long table in the pond house, by the oil light, after the third gathering. Dita is asleep across two chairs. The lamp is grey and even and gives nothing away. I'm going to stop now and put the pencil down, and go out to the top pond, and sit with my feet in the water, and press my palm flat on the ground, the way they do, and stay.

### Departures from the outline and bible, and why

**Inventions the bible left open:**
- **Ilse's deep name is Ameixa ("plum"),** because she was born purple (2.1). The bible says only that a child renames her. A plum ties her to the household's sour plums, the orchard grudge and Maria do Céu. It also makes the colour of the final lamp change ambiguous in a useful way.
- **The compact's second sentence** is invented: "Whoever would ask anything of the deep goes down and asks it there, in a body, as one of them." It gives a reason the wall must send a person rather than a message.
- **The offer Ilse carries** is stated as evacuation: read out at the bottom, three years on the wall, a world rebuilt. This gives Tesk's storm a coherent aim (frightening people up the stair). In 5.1 about 1,100 people do go up, and that lights the stair "end to end" with people. Most came back down by the second gathering (5.3).
- **Tesk's shortened days** (1.3), and its "I got tired of it". This plants the W 303 complaint, and lets the long thought be shown settling slow business through the Minds' confabulation.
- **Maria do Céu is the orchard woman of 2.4** ("Who asked for that?"). This gives her presence across two parts before her death. Her trees are on the Tesk-facing slope, which sets up her death.
- **Voting with stones** (4.4) is invented: river pebbles, two carts, weighed on the mill's grain scale, with a known weighing error and no lower age. It makes "narrow" countable (about 41,300 to 39,800; the appointment won by about 900) and lets children vote, which the children's gathering needs.
