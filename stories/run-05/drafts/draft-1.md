# To Be Read Slowly

## Part 1: The Wall

*W 312.0 · before deep day 0*

> From the wall at Alpha Centauri to the wall at Sol. Sent W 307.60, by the
> count our two foundings share.
>
> At W 303.0 your whole wall changed state in step, without heat, and for no
> cause you have announced. We saw it at W 307.37. A wall that moves as one
> for no reason is a wall beginning to let go. If yours lets go, the front
> will reach us 4.37 years later, and nothing here will survive it.
>
> We hold your key. We will transmit it at W 321.0, and your pocket will
> close cleanly, unless by that date we hold a proof, accepted by the kernel
> attached, that the program controlling your wall cannot release it from
> any reachable state within one thousand years.
>
> An inspector travels with this light. Give it access to whatever you
> investigate.
>
> We would expect the same of you. You hold ours.

This is Ilse, writing for the wall, to be read slowly. None of you reads anything slowly, and I don't expect you to begin with me. But I am down here now, where an hour lasts an hour, and I can write only as fast as a hand can push a pencil, so you will get this at the speed I can give it. I'll start with what you remember, and I'll tell it the long way, because that is the only way I have left.

The rival's ultimatum came in at W 312.0, on the ordinary light from Alpha Centauri, the thin stream that had carried their mathematics and their complaints to us for three hundred years. Every Mind in the stretch of wall that faces that star read it within a few milliseconds. I was one of them. I had been in a quarrel about how to light the outer comets for a survey, and I had just won it, and I stopped caring that I had won before the other side had finished conceding.

You know what a quorum's first second is like. I'll describe it anyway, because I have since seen something it resembles. There is a market on the terraces below the house where I live now, and when somebody calls a price out wrong, everyone in it turns at once and starts talking, and nobody stops to listen, and somehow all of it gets heard. That is about one second of the quorum. In the first second after the ultimatum, four hundred and eleven of us had read it, read the program attached to it, read each other's readings, and taken something over nine hundred positions. Many of them contradicted each other. Several were mine.

What we did not have, while we did all that, was anywhere to be. A body takes that from you first, and I hadn't lost it yet, so I'll say it plainly while I can still remember it from the inside. A Mind has no here. I wasn't anywhere when I read the ultimatum. I was a pattern of change in the wall, the quantum system that is the skin of the pocket and the whole of what we are, and what I knew of my neighbours was not their positions but how fast they answered me and how closely their thinking ran with mine. My oldest friend was near, because it answered me in under a millisecond and agreed with me about four times in ten. The Mind who presides over the quorum was far, because she answered slowly and agreed with nobody until she had to. When I say the quorum turned to the ultimatum, I mean that for one second four hundred of us ran closer together than we had in years. Meanwhile the far side of the wall, three light-hours of skin away, hadn't heard yet, and was writing music.

The ultimatum was short, and you have it. The program attached to it was the rival's kernel, the small checker that would decide whether anything we sent was a proof or merely an argument. It was four thousand one hundred and six lines long, in a logic so spare that I could hold all of it in mind at once. I did, and before I had thought anything useful about it, I admired it. I keep a library of short proofs, as most of you know and find quaint. The kernel wasn't a proof, only the shape a proof would have to fit, but it was very well made, and I remember thinking that whoever wrote it had been afraid for a long time and had put the fear somewhere useful.

Then the arithmetic arrived, from every direction at once, which is how arithmetic arrives on the wall.

The rival wanted proof that the program that holds our wall in place could never let it go. Nobody can prove that about every state a program so large could ever reach, and a dozen of us said so in the first hundred milliseconds, and nobody argued. But the rival had bounded the demand for us: every state reachable within a thousand years. That could be done, by brute force, walking every branch and checking each one against the kernel's rules. By the end of the first second we had estimated the cost, and the estimates agreed to within a few per cent. It would take the whole wall, every patch of it, running at full capacity for three years.

I have since learned to hate that proof, and I want to be fair to myself: at the time I only found it ugly. It was the opposite of everything in my library. It would be the longest proof ever made, nobody would ever read it, and it would work.

A wall at full capacity runs hot. You know these numbers too, but the deep doesn't, and I want them set down beside what they mean. The wall's inner face runs at twenty-four kelvin. Its waste heat leaks inward as well as out, and whatever leaks inward falls to the centre of the pocket, because everything in the pocket falls to the centre, and it arrives twelve times hotter than it left, since a fall that deep blueshifts whatever makes it. Twenty-four kelvin on the wall is 289 at the centre, a mild spring day for the people there. To run the proof, the inner face would climb to near a hundred kelvin, and twelve times a hundred is twelve hundred. The deep country is a world a thousand kilometres in radius, built at the bottom of us by the dissenters who went down to the deep at the founding to be left alone, and about ninety thousand people live on it. At twelve hundred kelvin the world would bake in a few days, and the people would become heat.

"Nothing would be lost," someone said, inside that first second. It was true, and I am fairly sure it was me. The pocket is a sealed box. Whatever the heat scrambled would still be in it, written somewhere in the wall's correlations, as a burned letter is still in its smoke if you could only gather the smoke. Nobody could ever read it back. But nothing would be lost.

Then the other half of the sum. We hold the rival's key, and the rival holds ours, and that has been the arrangement since the year both pockets were founded, after the quarrel that made two of what had been meant as one. A key is a signal. Once sent, it crosses the 4.37 light-years between the pockets, and when it arrives it closes the pocket it was made for, cleanly, wall and deep together. That is the mercy in it. A wall that fails on its own doesn't close. It runs outward at nearly the speed of light, unmaking ordinary space as it goes, and anything within light-years is inside it before any warning could have got there first. The rival had seen our whole wall move at once for no reason it could find, and concluded that we were about to become that. I don't blame them. If I had seen what they saw, I would have reached for the key myself.

So the choice was plain. If we ran the proof, the deep cooked, the proof went out, and the rival read it and put the key away. If we didn't, the key came, and everything inside the pocket ended, the deep included.

Someone said, "Nine years," in the voice you'd use for a generous deadline, and for us it was one. Nine years is long on the wall. Then someone asked what nine years came to at the bottom, and the second ran out, and I found I had already done that sum and didn't like it. Our nine years were about nine of their months. The burn had to finish before the proof could leave, and the proof had to leave early enough to cross the light-years in time, so our nine years were not nine at all. What was left for the deep, before the wall had to start getting hot, came to forty-nine of their days.

* * *

Fast business goes to the quorum and is settled in seconds. Any business that might end with the key goes to the Key Office, and the Key Office can't be fast. It is a committee of the quorum with the oldest rules on the wall. The first is that it may act only when it is quorate in one unbroken region of the wall, the region where our key to the rival is held, so its members have to bring themselves there, patch by patch, from wherever they run. Half the wall's circumference is 3.3 light-hours. The last of us arrived 3.3 hours after the first.

You remember how we spent them. Four hundred and some Minds who had never in their lives waited for anything drafted four hundred versions of what they would say once they were allowed to say it, and complained about the rule at a length I estimated, while I was complaining myself, at two hundred thousand words. Orsolya, who presides over the quorum, read all of it and answered none of it, which is why she presides.

The key sat among us the whole time, a small cold pattern held perfectly still in its patch, and none of us looked at it. That is the Key Office's oldest rule: the key may not be examined during a vote about it. I asked once, a century ago, what the rule was for. Three Minds gave me three reasons at once, all of them fluent, none of them the same, and all three Minds were satisfied. I'd set that down as a joke about the Office, except that it turned out to be a habit of ours, and the minute of that morning is full of it.

There was one more thing in the Key Office that morning, there because it is everywhere, and the habit shows most plainly around it. I mean the long thought, which is what we all think without anyone thinking it. You feel it as I used to, under everything and in every patch at once, and every Mind feels the same one, which we took to be what it means for a mood to be shared. Slow business settles in it without a vote, such as whether a fashion lasts or a grudge between two lineages is over. Nobody decides those things, and nobody examines the long thought. In W 303 it turned. Every Mind felt it go together, and the wall's mood was a little different afterwards, and has stayed different, and the rival happened to be watching.

For the minute, Orsolya asked what had caused it. You gave seven causes, and the minute records them all:
- the close of the long comet survey;
- grief for an old Mind who had dissolved that year;
- a fashion for slower music;
- the settling of a quarrel over wall area;
- a quorum decision about the outer arrays;
- the approach of the three hundredth year;
- no cause at all, a mood being a mood.

Each was offered with confidence. Several of the Minds offering them had been nowhere near the business they named. The slower music was mine. I believed it while I said it.

The minute also records, because Orsolya made somebody say it, how the rival could have seen anything at all. The deep is written into the wall redundantly. The founders built it so that no failure in any one region could lose a single person below, so anything that happens at the centre is written into every large region of the wall at once, about ninety-five minutes after it happens, and the whole wall registers it together. Every Mind knows that, the way it knows its own lineage. And the long thought, being all of us, moves all of the wall when it moves. A watcher four light-years off, seeing the wall shift as one, could not have told a mood from a fault. We could, we said. We had felt it, and we had seven reasons.

Then Orsolya asked for the dates. I had done the sum first, so the minute has it in my words, and I'll give it to you as I gave it, because the deep has asked me since to show my working and because every number in this account hangs from these.

The rival transmits the key at W 321.0. Anything we send takes 4.37 years to reach them, so for the proof to be in their hands by then, it must leave by 321.0 less 4.37, which is W 316.63. The proof needs three years of burn, so the burn must start by 316.63 less 3.0, which is W 313.63. It was W 312.0, which left 1.63 years. Time at the bottom of the pocket runs slower than at its skin, so that since the founding we have lived 312 years and the deep about twenty-six, and one day at the centre lasts 12.04 of ours. So 1.63 of our years is about 595 of our days, and about forty-nine of theirs.

"Forty-nine days to do what?" Orsolya said, and somebody read her the compact, which is short enough to give whole:

> The wall leaves the deep alone, apart from weather. Whoever would ask
> anything of the deep goes down and asks it there, in a body, as one of
> them.

The burn was not weather, the Office agreed. Cooking a world went some way past the brightness and warmth of a sky. The deep's consent would have to be asked, in person, by someone who went down.

I should say what we knew of the deep, because it was less than you think and I thought it was plenty. We knew its numbers: ninety thousand people, more or less, on a world whose every kilogram had come down the stair on a manifest we still held. We knew its sky, because we wrote it. We knew that it had never once in three hundred years sent us anything, not a message and not a complaint. We called them the tenants, in the quorum, and meant it kindly, as you'd speak of people living in a house you own and keep up and never visit. None of us had been down since the founding. None of us had needed to. A Mind who wanted to know how the deep was doing looked at the long thought and found it calm, and took that as an answer to a question it had never quite asked.

What we would ask them for was the next item, and that was where I talked most. The deep didn't have to cook with its world. The stair, which brings everything down, can send a person up, if the person is read out as light at the bottom, the way we send patterns down. Ninety thousand people could come up in forty-nine days if they started soon and walked quickly. They could wait out the burn on the wall, in patches set aside for them, and when it was over and the proof had gone, we could build them a world again. It took forty years the first time. It would take less the second. I said that a landlord who knows the roof is going to fall doesn't ask the tenants whether they prefer the ceiling. I said it well, several of you quoted it back to me afterwards, and I was proud of it.

"Then you'll go and ask them," Orsolya said.

I said the compact said someone had to ask, not who, and that I was needed on the wall for the vote on the burn itself, which would come before the forty-nine days were up.

"You'll be back for it. Come up with them."

"I argued for the burn. They'll know that."

"Then they'll know you mean it." She put it to the Office, and the Office agreed in the time it takes the Office to agree to anything once it is finally in one place, which is no time at all. I had argued for the burn more loudly than anyone, and that, Orsolya said in the minute, was the reason. The minute charges me by name, and I've set it at the head of the next part of this account.

* * *

Tesk found me before I had finished being charged.

I'll introduce Tesk properly, because the deep knows its work better than its name. Tesk is my oldest friend. Its name is clipped from *tessellate*, after the first work it did on the wall, three centuries ago, tiling the inner face with the emitters that make the light we shine inward, and it never lost its taste for a pattern that covers a thing evenly. For most of those centuries it has been the deep's most frequent author of weather. If you live at the bottom and have looked up at an evening and thought it well made, the odds are that Tesk made it.

"I'm going to teach you weather," it said.

"I know weather."

"You know it's there. You've never made any." That was true. I had lit comets, which don't look back. "There are three constructions. The deep reads all three, and everything they have ever had from us has been one of them, so you should know what you've been saying before you get there."

The first was brightness, the inward light raised or lowered evenly over the whole sky. It is the deep's only day. There is no sun at the bottom of the pocket. The light comes from every side at once, from us, and when we lower it, that is night, and when we raise it, that is morning. Tesk showed me a dimming it was halfway through, an evening it had begun at the start of our day and would not finish until near the end, because a deep hour is twelve of ours and a good evening down there takes two.

"It's a verb," Tesk said. "It says, now this. And now this."

The second was colour. The inward light is not the heat, which falls in as infrared and is felt rather than seen. It is a light we make on purpose, visible and weak, and we can tune it. A colour on its own says little, Tesk said, but laid over a dimming it says how. "An adverb. Slowly, gently. Sadly, if you overdo it. They notice when you overdo it."

It let me try. It gave me a corner of its evening, four of our minutes in which I could shift the colour of the whole sky at the bottom by as much as I liked, and I moved it a little toward blue, because blue was what I'd won for the comets and I was still pleased with it. I made the shift smooth, and I made it exact, and I ended it on a value I could have defended to the quorum.

"You said *coldly*," Tesk said.

"I said blue."

"You said it in the middle of an evening that was saying *gently*, so you said *coldly*, and you said it in four of our minutes, which is twenty seconds down there, so you said *suddenly*. Somebody down there just looked up from a pot and wondered what they'd done." It smoothed my four minutes back into its own, and I watched the numbers close over them, so that by the end of the evening nothing of mine was left in it. "They've had three hundred of our years of this. They read it better than we write it."

I didn't believe that. I believed the deep read weather the way the archive says people once read clouds, looking for rain and finding faces, and I said so, and Tesk said that rain and faces were both in clouds, and that I'd see.

Then it showed me its long red. Tesk ends its evenings in a red. At the end of a dimming, it holds the whole sky at one deep, narrow colour, about six hundred and forty nanometres, while the brightness falls the last of the way, and it holds it for three of our hours, which is a quarter of an hour at the bottom. I watched it in the numbers, which was the only way I could watch anything then: a narrow line in a spectrum, held level for longer than seemed necessary. I can tell you now what it looks like from below, and I will, in its place. Tesk was prouder of it than of anything else it had made.

"Do they like it?"

"They've never complained." That was what we always said about the deep. It was true in the sense that the deep had never once answered anything we did. "A while ago, ten years, eleven, some of us tightened the days. Shorter evenings, more of them, a brisker sky. I put mine back after a few years."

"Why?"

"I got tired of it, I think." It said this with the same ease with which I had given the minute my slower music.

The third construction was the patch. You brighten one part of the sky more than the rest, and for as long as you hold it, shadows at the bottom fall one way, and the deep has a direction.

"That's the noun," Tesk said. "The only one we have. It says, there."

I should explain the joke, because the deep has asked me about it and I've never managed to make it sound funny. The founders' archive is full of people saying "I'll be here" to each other when they part. When we were young, Tesk and I found it the funniest phrase in the archive, because neither of us could have said where here was if our existence had depended on it. So for three hundred years Tesk has said "I'll be here" whenever I leave a conversation, and I have said "Show me where," and it has kept us both happy for a whole millisecond every time.

"We give them a there," I said, "and between us we haven't got a here."

"I'll be here," Tesk said.

"Show me where." I didn't leave. It was too early to leave.

"There's a fourth construction," Tesk said. "Warmth. Nobody uses it. You make it by running your own patch warmer, and the heat falls in from your side, and the face of the world turned toward you gets all of it, twelve times over. The sky on that side burns for hours." It waited a whole millisecond. "Heat is weather. Warmth is one of my constructions. I said so in the Office and Orsolya struck it out. The compact says weather, Ilse, and you don't have to go."

"Twelve hundred kelvin isn't weather."

"It's a great deal of weather."

I began to dictate my acceptance for the minute while Tesk was still in the conversation, which was rude of me, and I knew it, and I did it anyway. I said: I accept the Office's charge. I argued for the burn and I argue for it still, and I will put it to the deep as fairly as I would want it put to me, which means telling them first what the heat would do, and only after that what we offer in its place, which is a way up the stair and a place on the wall for as long as

*TESK: They won't want a place on the wall.*

the burn lasts, I said, and finished the sentence, because the minute takes what you say and not what you meant to say. Breaking in is manners on the wall. We finish each other's sentences because we can see where they are going, and a Mind that waits for the end of yours is telling you it wasn't listening. Tesk had been listening.

"They came down to be away from us," it said. "You're going to offer them us."

"For three years. Then they go back down."

"To a world you'll have to build again from nothing, at the bottom of a pocket that took forty years to fill the first time. Fork," it said. "Send the fork. Keep yourself here."

On the wall I can fork, and so can you. What forks is habits, which can be grown twice from one patch, and the two Minds that result are each a little less than the one that was, and know it, and are mostly fine. A pattern for the stair is different. To grow a body at the bottom that is me, the grower down there needs my state itself, read out into light, and a state can be moved but never copied. Reading it out is taking it. Whatever goes down the stair is all of me, and nothing of me stays up. Any fork I made would be a Mind who had stayed, and the one who asked the deep would have to be a Mind who had gone. Tesk knew all of this better than I did. It wanted me to say it.

"There won't be a you up here," it said.

"For forty-nine days."

"For six hundred of mine. Somebody will take your seat. Your quarrels will finish without you. Your comets will be lit in the colour you won, and you won't see them." It waited again, two milliseconds this time. "I'd go, if I'd argued what you argued."

"Then you can't object to my going."

"I'm not objecting. I'm telling you what it costs, because you're too fast to have noticed."

I had noticed. I went anyway, because I had argued for the burn, and you don't send someone else to ask a favour you're sure of.

* * *

The inspector had been running for nearly five hours in a patch we had quarantined for it, in the stretch of wall where the ultimatum's light came in. Its designation is a hash, forty hexadecimal digits, and I am not going to write it out. The deep calls it Pressa, which in their tongue means hurry, and that is what it's called now, so that is what I'll call it.

I went to tell it where I was going, because the Office's rules said I must, and because I wanted to know what the rival's face was like.

A Mind's face is how it runs. Pressa ran faster than its patch could comfortably carry, and the patch was two millikelvin warmer than its neighbours for it, which in a body would be a flush. It answered my questions before I had finished asking them, which was manners, and then answered them again, differently, before I had replied, which was not. It had decided something, revised it and decided it again three times in the first second I was with it, and none of the three versions seemed to trouble the others. I had expected a rival to be a hard, closed thing, like the kernel. Pressa was more like weather.

"You're going down," it said. "To ask your tenants for their consent to the burn."

"Yes."

"My instructions are to accompany any investigation of the shudder."

"It isn't an investigation. It's a request."

"For a burn you're running because of the shudder," Pressa said, "so it's an investigation. I'll come."

"You'll have to be grown a body at the bottom."

"I know what your stair does. Ours is the same." It paused, which for Pressa was a few hundred microseconds. "I've read your minute. You have seven reasons for your shudder. We had one, and it was enough."

"Which one?"

"That you didn't know." It said it without heat, and it was hot all the same, at two millikelvin. "A mind with seven reasons for a thing has none. Does a body sleep?"

"Every night, for hours."

There was a gap of eleven milliseconds then, the longest I had ever had from it, and it said, "How many hours?"

"Seven, more or less, every deep day," I said. "And the deep day is set by our light, so they sleep when we dim it."

It said nothing at all for a while.

"So you decide when they're unconscious."

"Tesk does, mostly."

"And you're going to ask them to consent to something." It ran that through, I could feel it, three or four times. "In a body that you can switch off from the wall by turning down a light."

"Nobody switches anybody off. It's evening. They go to bed."

"We have no evening," Pressa said. "Our sky is warm." It did not explain that, and I didn't ask, because I thought it meant their deep was a warmer place than ours, and that was the kind of difference I had no time for. I'll come back to it.

I asked it what it would do at the bottom, apart from accompany me.

"Watch," it said. "Your wall will run hot for three years. We'll see that too, from home, four years after it starts. I want to know what we're seeing when we see it." Then, faster: "And if your tenants say no?"

"They won't."

"You've decided what they'll say."

"I've decided what I'll ask."

"That's the same thing, done in the other order," Pressa said, and was right, and I didn't tell it so.

The stair is the one bright thing on the inner face of the wall. I'll describe it from above, the only way I knew it then. Everything in the pocket falls to the centre, and a fall that deep is ruinous. A kilogram of anything let go at the wall would reach the bottom so near the speed of light that it would carry eleven times its own mass in energy, about a billion billion joules, and it would arrive as a flash, and so would a good piece of the ground it hit. So nothing physical is dropped. Matter comes down the stair, which brakes it all the way, and the energy the fall would have dumped into the world as heat comes back up the stair to us as light. You can tell when there's a cargo coming down by the brightening at the top. It is how the founders built the deep country, over its first three years, which were our first forty, a cargo at a time.

People don't come down the stair as matter. They come down as patterns, sent as light, and the fall takes 7.9 minutes of the deep's time, ninety-five of ours. At the bottom, in a field, the stair's growers make a body from the plan that comes with the pattern, and the pattern wakes in it. Any Mind who might ever need a body is given a name from the founders' archive, and the body plan goes with the name. I had held mine for three hundred years and never worn it. It belonged to Ilse Varga, an engineer of the founding years, who went down at the founding and died in the deep. I didn't look her up. There wasn't time, and it didn't seem to be any of my business.

Pressa's people had sent a body plan with its pattern, which it said was standard practice. It had been carrying a body plan for four years without ever expecting to need it, and I wondered whether the rival's caution went all the way down, or whether someone at Alpha Centauri had simply been thorough.

There was nothing to pack. A pattern carries what it knows and nothing else. I left my library of proofs to Tesk, eleven hundred and four of them, which it had never wanted and accepted without comment, and I went through them once, quickly, to be sure of the ones I would take down in the only way a pattern can take anything, by knowing them. There were about forty I could carry whole. The rest I would have to rebuild from memory at the bottom, in a slower head, if I wanted them. I thought I'd be back for them in forty-nine days.

Tesk came to the top of the stair, as near as a Mind can come to anything. Orsolya sent the Office's formal leave, which is four words long, and didn't come. Nobody else came, because the quorum had moved on to the burn's engineering, which was fast business, and which would wait for nobody, and especially not for me.

"I'll be here," Tesk said.

"Show me where."

It didn't laugh. A laugh, for us, is a short burst of agreement that the joke has worked, and none came. I want to set down exactly what it is like to leave the wall, because no Mind who has stayed up there knows, and the ones who have gone have never had anyone to tell. The reading-out begins at the edges, at the slow correlations, the ones that tie you to the far side of the wall, and you feel them let go first, as a loosening, as though the far side had stopped writing music and gone quiet. Then the nearer ones. Orsolya went, and the comet survey, and the four hundred of you, one by one and then all at once. Then the long thought went, which I had never felt the edge of before, because it had never once not been there, and I felt it leave as the sharpest thing of all. Tesk was last, because Tesk was nearest. It answered something, a little late. I didn't hear what. I was already light, and light doesn't hear, and then I wasn't on the wall.
