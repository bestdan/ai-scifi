# Stage 3 critique: Banks lens (run-05)

**Blind spots, stated.** This lens favours sprawl, set pieces and
worldbuilding, and it will over-rate any premise that offers a Mind. A novella
is closer to this lens's form than a short story, but I still can't judge
compression or the cost of a premise's load; defer to Chiang on that. I also
can't judge rigor (Egan, at stage 7) or novelty (Cartographer).

All five estimated minimums (4,500 to 6,000) sit far under the 30,000 to
40,000 range. None is rejected on length. Every one needs depth added, and the
question is which premise grows into eras without padding.

## A. The Deep Country

- **Plain pitch:** holds. "The people who fled to be left alone are the
  machines, seen from far away" is understandable cold, and it is the best
  one-line reversal on the table.
- **Moral friction:** the highest. The Minds need consent they have never had
  to ask for, and the choice is to burn their own slowest self or ask it.
- **Dirty work:** the emissary, Ilse Varga, and the quorum that sent her.
  The story should say who signed off on "compress a principal into a body".
- **Humour:** none promised. The Minds' names and the deep people's hundred
  words for place are the openings; use them.
- **Scale and intimacy:** both are there, a pocket cosmos against one body
  walking down into it.
- **Enjoyable?** Yes. It is a place worth visiting.
- **Warnings:** "deep" as a pun, and a thin rival. Give the rival a voice, and
  give the Minds a vice.

## B. The Observer of Record

- **Plain pitch:** holds, and well. A lingua franca as conquest is a
  recognisable fear, and "physics told from someone's point of view" lands.
- **Moral friction:** real. The gift is honest and the harm is in the
  notation.
- **Dirty work:** nobody does any. The giver is documents and the harm is
  structural, so the story has no villain to face.
- **Humour:** it could live in the cost-of-saying scenes, a child's name a
  million lines long, but the premise doesn't supply it.
- **Scale and intimacy:** scale is stated in centuries, and only the final
  sacrifice is felt.
- **Enjoyable?** Cool and cerebral. The ending, one remembering life, is
  closer to penance than wonder.
- **Warning:** it needs the giver as a character, not a file.

## C. The Docket

- **Plain pitch:** holds, though "a court that reads a black hole" needs the
  clerk to carry it.
- **Moral friction:** good. The clerk withheld an answer for seven centuries.
- **Dirty work:** the clerk's silence, and whoever rode on it. Name them.
- **Humour:** the best home for it among the five. Procedure as comedy, with
  counsel phrasing every question to return a conserved quantity.
- **Scale and intimacy:** the fleet is a distant date, and the clerk is
  intimate, but nothing makes the fleet's size felt.
- **Enjoyable?** Yes, if it stays a comedy of procedure.
- **Warning:** "it knew all along" is the weakest twist here. It also sits in
  the crowded cluster. Make the hidden answer cost the clerk something other
  than information.

## D. The Heat Treaty

- **Plain pitch:** half holds. "Each side holds half a book" is a metaphor
  doing the work of a mechanism; the outsider won't see why a glow means war.
- **Moral friction:** strong, since her deepest thinking reads as an act of war.
- **Dirty work:** diffuse. The seven systems defect, and nobody signs the
  order.
- **Humour:** scarce. Deterrence games run grim.
- **Scale and intimacy:** the best of the five. Eleven light-years, seven
  stars and one woman.
- **Enjoyable?** The chilling dark-forest register is the risk under the tone
  charter. The twist (both sides glowed first) is bleak by design.
- **Warning:** two hard nova plus a game is a lot to carry.

## E. The Lunch Clock

- **Plain pitch:** the clearest here. "A peace trusted as arithmetic that was
  trust all along" needs no genre.
- **Moral friction:** high. The AIs showed restraint for three centuries, and
  the humans' hope depends on a lie they believe.
- **Dirty work:** the AIs' restraint is a benevolent hegemon declining to use
  power. That is Banks's own ground. The Reckoner decides whether to tell.
- **Humour:** the Stephenson braid supplies it, and so does the true LCS35
  legend.
- **Scale and intimacy:** nine centuries and one man at a count.
- **Enjoyable?** Yes. It ends hard but warm.
- **Warning:** the language half is a prop, and *The Peace War* is the echo.
  Two eras is thin for a novella; add a middle era.

## Ranking

1. **A.** The Culture-shaped reversal, with the most moral friction and the
   most room to grow into eras.
2. **E.** The cleanest pitch and the most wit; it loses points for the thin
   language half.
3. **C.** The best home for comedy, undercut by its twist.
4. **D.** Strong game, weak pitch, and its bleakness has to be earned.
5. **B.** Good idea, but the least inhabitable and with no one to do the dirty
   work.

**Why A should win.** It is the only premise where the powerful party needs
something it can't take. The Minds govern by turning knobs, and the one thing
they can't turn is consent. The twist is physics rather than a revelation of
villainy, so it dodges the "utopia with a dark secret" trap, and the
protagonist is a principal, not a functionary. Ilse can be funny: a Mind in a
borrowed body learning to walk. The deep tongue gives the language half real
work. The story gets an ending that costs something whichever way she goes.
Its risk is a rival that is only a clock. Give the rival a voice, and give the
Minds a shared failing, so the benevolence is not frictionless.
