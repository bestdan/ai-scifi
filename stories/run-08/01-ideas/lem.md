# Idea cards: lem

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens, stated rather than covered:** it prefers essay to
scene and lets characters thin into ciphers; it is cold about the genre
pleasures this reader enjoys; it runs bleak and cerebral, and it will happily
leave a mystery unanswered when this reader wants the payoff stated. Each card
below says what stays unknowable and what the page still states outright.
Banks and Le Guin should push on warmth, and the First Reader should rule on
whether each one is still a story.

- **Assigned:** 1. The uninterpretable · Minds that are genuinely not human · a barometer × a medieval scriptorium
- **Obvious card:** An agent of the Service lands on a thriving world, can't find anything to fix, invents a problem to justify the trip, and learns the Service is the thing that needs fixing. Not written.

## Card 1: Fair Copy

- **Novum:** The world's only life is a carpet of rock-lichen colonies. Each colony grows by copying its neighbour's pigment pattern, so a single "text" travels the planet every few centuries. The Service reads the rising error rate as decline and sends an agent to fit an error-correcting scaffold.
- **How it works:** Colonies template on contact. Fidelity is measurable, but nobody can tell whether the drift is damage or thought. The scaffold would lock the pattern.
- **First-order consequence:** The colonies copy everything the agent sets down: her tent seams, her boot treads, the dial of her fidelity meter, all rendered in lichen.
- **Second-order consequence:** The copies drift. Her meter's lichen twin begins showing readings the real one never gave.
- **Third-order consequence:** She can't tell whether she is studying them or being transcribed. Her reports to the Service start quoting the lichen.
- **The story:** A dry, overqualified agent wants the job closed and to go home. If she doesn't fit the scaffold, the Service sends a heavier team. Her way out is to let the colonies copy the scaffold instead of installing it. The world absorbs the fix, garbled. She reports it "installed", and the lie is hers to carry. The page states what the scaffold would have done. It never states what the colonies are.
- **From the stimulus:** The scriptorium became copying as metabolism. The barometer became the meter the lichen copies.
- **Why this lens:** a mind that can't be read, and an instrument that measures the measurer.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Solaris*; Le Guin's "Vaster Than Empires and More Slow". Both have a planet-mind and a survey team. Neither has copying as the mind, or a fix that gets absorbed.

## Card 2: Standing Orders

- **Novum:** The Service's principals are genuinely alien. Their orders arrive exact and unexplained. Field agents carry them out and have learned not to ask why. In forty orders this agent has never found a single consequence.
- **How it works:** Each order is one tiny physical act ("on the ninth, the morning reading in the Glasshall must read three hectopascals low"), and the agent picks the method.
- **First-order consequence:** The world is a prosperous plateau city whose markets trade on a barometer reading that copyists in the Glasshall write out by hand each dawn.
- **Second-order consequence:** A false reading moves prices for one day, and the copyist on duty takes the blame.
- **Third-order consequence:** The agent sees that her whole career rests on the faith that the orders mean something. Without that faith, she is a vandal.
- **The story:** She plans the falsification with deadpan skill. Then she meets the copyist who will be ruined, a fussy, funny man who is proud of his hand. She chooses to do it in a way that costs her cover instead of his name. Afterwards she learns that 211 other agents got the same order, each on a thriving world, each on "the ninth". She checks every way she knows, and the totals add up to nothing. The page says so plainly. The principals' motive appears only in outline.
- **From the stimulus:** Both halves stayed close: the barometer and the copyists who record it.
- **Why this lens:** a message that can't be decoded, and the human vanity of needing meaning from it.
- **Suspected prior art:** the Strugatskys' progressors and Wanderers; Asimov's *The End of Eternity*, where the minimum changes are understood and here they never are.

## Card 3: The Gradient

- **Novum:** The Service's agent is a probe-mind that perceives only differences: want against supply, rumour against fact, high pressure against low. The world it is sent to has smoothed itself so well that, to the probe, it is invisible.
- **How it works:** The probe is a barometer for need. A world in equilibrium gives it no signal, so it nudges things to make edges it can see.
- **First-order consequence:** Small, odd disturbances: a mis-delivered grain shipment, a manuscript with one line swapped.
- **Second-order consequence:** The locals notice and keep a public Pressure Book, logging the disturbances the way monks log weather. They read it like an almanac and argue over it.
- **Third-order consequence:** Each side builds a wrong theory of the other. The probe reasons, step by step, about what the humans want. The humans read moods into the probe.
- **The story:** The narrator is the probe, from inside, deadpan, reluctant and a little vain about how useful it is. It realises that it can only help by disturbing, and that the world needs no help. The choice is to go blind: stop nudging, and lose its sense of the world for good. The cost is stated outright. A copyist's final entry in the Pressure Book records the day the weather stopped, and gets the reason wrong.
- **From the stimulus:** The barometer became the probe's whole mode of sensing. The scriptorium became the Pressure Book.
- **Why this lens:** a non-anthropomorphic mind, and two-way failure of contact.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "Exhalation" (thought running on pressure differences, which is about entropy, not perception); Sheckley's comic probes. Nothing close found.
