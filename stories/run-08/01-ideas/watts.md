# Idea cards: watts

**Blind spots of this lens:** it drifts toward relentless bleakness, turns
characters into case studies, and packs density that wears readers out. It
underrates meaning, ritual and joy as real forces, which matters here
because the brief wants funny and wry. Each card below puts the comedy in
the narrator's deadpan collision with a doctrine, not in anyone's misery.
Le Guin and Banks should push back on whether the locals get to be more
than evidence.

- **Assigned:** 2. The finding believed · Reasoning as the way through · spectroscopy × a lunar far-side base
- **Obvious card:** a meddling agent lands on a thriving world, finds
  nothing wrong, and has to invent a crisis to justify the trip. Not written.

## Card 1: Presenting as Fine

- **Novum:** The service takes the confabulation research (Nisbett & Wilson 1977; Gazzaniga's split-brain interpreter) as settled: what a mind says about itself is evidence of nothing. So it never asks a world how it is. It reads the world instead.
- **How it works:** Survey posts sit on the far sides of target moons, shielded from the world's broadcasts on purpose, and judge it by atmospheric spectra. In the field, agents read people by breath spectroscopy (real diagnostics from volatile compounds in breath).
- **First-order consequence:** A world that says it is fine is, by doctrine, showing anosognosia, unawareness of one's own deficit (Babinski 1914). The louder it protests, the sicker it reads.
- **Second-order consequence:** Agents are trained not to listen, and that includes listening to themselves.
- **Third-order consequence:** At home, "how are you" is rude. A lover reads your breath, not your words.
- **The story:** A dry, competent agent lands on a chatty, thriving colony. The spectral flag that brought him is harvest fermentation. He must prove the world healthy with no testimony, his own included, by reasoning from chemistry alone. Then his own breath, read on the way out, shows he has come to love the place, and the doctrine counts that against his report.
- **From the stimulus:** spectroscopy became the only admissible way of knowing anything. The far-side base became a listening post built so it can't hear.
- **Why this lens:** the self as unreliable narrator, made into policy.
- **Suspected prior art:** the Strugatskys' Progressors and Banks's Contact, for the meddling frame. Neither rules testimony inadmissible.
- **Attractor broken:** it brushes "an institution that adjudicates". The conflict is a proof worked out in the field, not a tribunal.

## Card 2: It Was My Idea to Leave

- **Novum:** The service believes the choice blindness results. Johansson and Hall (Science, 2005) swapped the photos people had picked, and Hall et al. (2012) reversed their moral survey answers. Most subjects didn't notice, and defended choices they never made. So the service meddles by quietly swapping a world's record of what it decided, and lets the world adopt the swap.
- **How it works:** The swaps are small: a heading on a chart, a recipe, a ledger line. People build their own reasons for the swapped choice, and the service counts that as consent.
- **First-order consequence:** The service's own people treat a decision as something assigned to you, not something you wrote.
- **Second-order consequence:** One thriving world has learned the trick. Eleven agents have come home from it, each certain that leaving was his own idea, and their eloquent reports contradict each other.
- **Third-order consequence:** Agents keep sealed handwritten notes to themselves, and stop trusting their own convictions.
- **The story:** Agent twelve goes to catch the swap. Partway through, she finds her own sealed note says "do nothing", yet she is arguing hard for intervention. Did the world swap her, or the service before launch? A competent narrator audits her own enthusiasm as if it were a suspect, using timestamps, ink, and which arguments she is suspiciously good at. In the end she chooses with no way to know whether the choice is hers.
- **From the stimulus:** it travelled far. Only the far-side base remains, as the vault that holds the sealed notes.
- **Why this lens:** characters less unified than they believe.
- **Suspected prior art:** Dick's "We Can Remember It for You Wholesale", which implants memories. Here there is no drug or telepathy, only the mind's own confabulation.

## Card 3: The Cold Minute

- **Novum:** The service believes the peak-end rule. In Kahneman et al. (1993), people chose to repeat a longer cold-water trial because its last 30 seconds were slightly milder. Redelmeier, Katz and Kahneman (2003) found the same with colonoscopies. Since the remembering self judges a life, the service improves civilizations by sweetening their endings.
- **How it works:** Agents add a gentler tail to bad experiences. The memory improves even though the total suffering grows.
- **First-order consequence:** On the service's worlds, every meeting, meal and illness ends softly, by protocol.
- **Second-order consequence:** The target is a far-side lunar observatory town doing hydrogen-line spectroscopy of the cosmic dark ages, a real reason to build where the far side is radio-quiet. Its remembered-happiness scores are terrible: every surface shift ends with a deliberate minute in a cold-soak room.
- **Third-order consequence:** The town treats the remembering self as a second person it is careful not to flatter. A fondly remembered shift makes the next one careless, and a careless moment on the surface means vacuum.
- **The story:** A reluctant "ending technician" arrives to sweeten the town's last minutes: shift ends, breakups, funerals. He reasons out why they end things badly, then must decide whether to report a working safety culture as a pathology. His fix would make them happier in memory and get someone killed.
- **From the stimulus:** it stayed close. The far-side base and its spectroscopy are both the setting and the reason for it.
- **Why this lens:** a trait (fond memory) set against the pressure that selected it.
- **Suspected prior art:** I know of none in SF. The source is Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*.
