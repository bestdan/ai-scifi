# Everything You Look At

## Day 1. 40.0000

The first thing I fixed on the planet was a wheel. It belonged to a cart in a yard full of straw and broken pots, and it wobbled, and I am qualified in wheels. I trued it in twenty minutes. When I looked up, the cart was gone, the straw was gone, and a grey bird the size of a heron was standing where the cart had been, facing the other way, very deliberately not watching me. My number said 40.0000. It always says 40.0000. That number is how sure I am that this world is dying, forty being as sure as the Concern makes anyone, and while I carry it I can only read a missing cart as evidence of a society too traumatised to own one.

My name is Perpetua Lusk, and I was on my third mission for the Concern, which is the service I work for, which fixes worlds. Three clean missions get a field officer out of the mud and into the Actuarial Office on Mercer's Ring, where you set the numbers instead of carrying them in your head. I had brought a roll of spanners, axle grease, thin brass wedges for tight joints, and a two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle my sister had sent before I shipped out to this limestone hollow of wet yellow clay and boiled turnip greens.

The dry-stone cottages around the square were roofed with sagging thatch weighted by cracked clay jars, their doorways choked with split reed baskets and damp barley chaff. When I walked to the stone well-curb to mend its split wooden winding drum, two tall birds squatting by a rain-barrel immediately stood up, unhooked the bucket without glancing at me, dropped the rope down the shaft, and carried the bucket behind a wall so I could not look at it.

I knelt by the well-curb anyway, laid out my brass wedges, and looked around for a stone to tap one into the split oak. Forty paces off by a nettle ditch, a smaller bird with barred brown flanks crouched in the dirt, trying to hide a tiny treasure from the rest of the square. It had zygodactyl feet, two scaly toes gripping forward and two braced back, and was tucking braided copper wire and two green glass beads under a flat slate. The instant my face turned that way, the bird froze in panic at having been seen; its yellow eye caught the angle of my nose, and every nape feather stood straight up.

Frantically it hooked the wire and beads back out of the ruined hiding place with its rear claws, bolted ten paces behind the rain-barrel, pried up a cobblestone with its beak, and shoved its hoard into the dark earth underneath. Then it craned around the barrel stave to check whether it had escaped my gaze. I was still watching, never having seen a foot and beak used like carpenter's pincers. Seeing that my eyes had followed its treasure to the new hole too, the brown bird flattened its crest in defeat, stepped off the cobblestone, and walked away down the lane without looking back, leaving the wire and beads in the mud as ruined beyond saving.

"You spent it," said the heron-sized grey bird standing by the cart-tracks. She was addressing a clump of dock weeds ten feet to my left, her grizzled throat feathers ruffling. "Then you spent it again."

"I didn't touch it," I said, holding up my brass wedge. "It's still under the cobble by the barrel."

"It is in the dirt," the grey bird said in stiff Concern standard, which her people had learned from sixty years of orbital survey probes broadcasting greeting loops over their valley, "and the other half is inside your head, because you took a look at where she put it and where she moved it. If an outsider knows where a cache lies and what is in it, she can rob it in thought and follow the owner's toes next time. Once an outsider has seen a cache, it is spent, and moving it while you watch only spends the new hole too."

She shifted her two-forward, two-back toes in the mud, keeping the back of her narrow skull aligned with my nose. "I am Under-the-Cistern. Call me Cistern, since your mouth lacks the click in the middle. The flock drew straws behind the granary this morning and I lost, so I am here to deal with you while you ruin the furniture."

"I trued your cartwheel," I said. "It had a three-centimetre wobble."

"It was a very good cart," Cistern told the weeds. "Then you stared at the hub for twenty minutes with your eyes wide open, so the brothers had to abandon it in the nettle gulley."

I looked up at the corner of my vision, where my number hung in steady green digits against the overcast sky: 40.0000. Everything they valued, they hid when watched; anything seen was abandoned as spent; the surface was kept as worthless straw and broken pots because the surface was watched. With forty in my head, the diagnosis clicked together by itself: acute scarcity psychosis from multi-generational collapse, driving starving villagers to throw away their own carts whenever a stranger looked at them. They needed me terribly.

## Day 4. 39.9999

On the fourth morning Cistern walked me up the limestone ridge above the district, keeping two paces ahead with her tailfeathers squarely in my face, the height of local manners. In the thorn hedges, parents were feeding chicks from behind, tapping a nestling between the wings before reaching over its shoulder to drop a steamed root or salted mussel into its bill. A chick's first lesson is to take what it is given without turning round; when one hatchling twisted to peek at its father's claws, both parents hissed and rapped its crown.

"At fledging," Cistern told a boundary stone as we passed, "every chick digs one decoy cache in the square while the whole flock watches. It is the only cache a bird ever shows everyone, and we name you for it. Mine was a lead ribbon under the old cistern."

"And after fledging you never show anyone?" I asked, watching my boots.

"You show your trusted ones," Cistern said, shifting into the brisk clatter of gossip. "A mated pair in the Kiln-Wall flock showed each other four hundred sites in their first spring. Showing a cache to a trusted one is our deepest intimacy, and spends nothing. Trust is made by pressing things into a hand from behind in the dark, accepted unseen."

"Why doesn't a trusted one's sight spend it?"

"If I trust you, what you know is mine. That's what trust is for. Somebody has to remember where I put things." Cistern scratched the grey feathers at her throat with one scaly foot. "We hold thousands of sites and forget as we age; a trusted one is your second memory. A dying bird shows her caches to her trusted ones, who inherit by knowing. Dying with no trusted ones takes your caches with you, the worst death we know."

Suddenly Cistern cocked her eye toward the clouds and clicked her beak twice. Birds see in ultraviolet, where an orbital survey probe glints overhead like a flare. Instantly two hundred birds kicked over peat braziers, scattered root baskets, and ran in erratic zigzags while throwing barley chaff over their heads. I recognized the exact choreography from my mission briefing: the survey footage our office had logged as famine panic. Three minutes later, when the probe dropped below the ridge, everyone brushed off the chaff and went back to chewing roots.

"Greedy violet eye in the sky," Cistern muttered. "Our real works are a hundred metres down, where caches have always been. I am an assayer of the guild. Deep underground, our workshops, glass-lit furnaces, and one two-kilometre particle accelerator ring exist for our art, the table: every element held, while everything else we make, medicine, optical glass, and power, is a by-product of the art."

On the base of her wing, where she had brushed a bench downstairs, I saw a smudge of canary-yellow powder: refined lead-oxide assaying flux. Yet in the top corner of my sight, my number had only ticked from 40.0000 to 39.9999.

"I see the flux on your feathers, and I know that hillside panic was a drill for a satellite," I said. "It doesn't help, because of the installation, the implant behind my left eye that holds my certainty. It taps both my optic nerves and my hearing, records everything I see and hear, and sends the log home. My number is the base-ten log of the odds that your world is in crisis. It started at forty."

I looked at the green digits against the limestone. "Watching your probe drill moved my number one ten-thousandth of a point, and your telling me about underground furnaces moved it nothing at all. Talk is only testimony, and testimony moves the installation almost nothing, because a dying world can say anything about itself. At this rate I'd stop being sure in about four thousand years. A world that is fine and good at hiding, and a world in crisis that is good at public relations, produce nearly the same surface evidence. Even now, looking at that yellow flux on your wing, my head tells me you scavenged a pinch of old pigment to bluff an inspector. While my number is high, I can know facts that contradict the crisis, and still feel zero doubt. I'm certain you're dying. I'll stay certain for four thousand years unless you show me something that can't happen on a dying world."

"And what would that be?" Cistern asked the gorse bush.

"A cache of work only a living, working people could make, made in front of my eyes, not dug up," I said.

"Anything shown to an outsider's eyes is spent," Cistern said. "Why didn't your people set your certainty to the maximum, to one, if they wanted you so stubborn?"

"A belief set at exactly one can never move no matter what it sees, so an agent could never judge a world fixed and come home," I said. "The actuaries set it at forty, as high as possible short of unmovable, and gave us a window of 120 days. If I'm above zero on day 120, Remediation comes: ten thousand staff, all of them with cameras."

Every feather along Cistern's spine went rigid at the word *cameras*.

"Why not just give you orders?" she asked.

"Because of the Hessary Inquiry," I said, sitting on a wet stone. "The Concern began as a plain aid service whose assessors landed open-minded. On Hessary, a world in real famine, the assessor spent thirty-one years gathering evidence and being careful while two billion people starved to death. They keep her careful notebooks in a glass room in the Concern's archive, thirty-one years of them in neat blue columns. The Hessary Inquiry concluded that doubt kills, and made our three rules: issued near-certainty, the 120-day window, and audit-only evidence from cameras and microphones. I could edit my number to zero in my menu, and they would just void my report and send a fresh agent. So now they make sure we can't doubt."

Cistern stood quiet for a long moment, understanding that you cannot argue with ten to the fortieth power. "Come down off the ridge," she said flatly, changing the subject. "If you keep looking at that lime-kiln, we will have to tear it down before supper."

## Day 9. 39.9998

By the ninth afternoon I had spent eleven more caches by accident, including a dry-stone root cellar, a copper-hooped wash-tub, and an earthenware crock of salted anchovies left in the road because I glanced through a doorway while pitching my rain-tarp. Meanwhile, while handling three cracked water-jars abandoned near my tent, my fingers had found something my camera could not see inside the dark necks: the interior clay rims were cut with machine-ground ceramic screw-threads to a tolerance of half a thousandth of an inch, meant to take a vacuum-tight sealing cap. My certainty immediately explained the threads to me as pathetic relics: the villagers were using salvaged industrial canisters from a dead golden age to carry ditch-water because their own potters had forgotten how to fire a kiln. At dusk I opened a relay link to the Desk, our sector dispatcher three light-hours back, and typed a plain-language report documenting the precision vacuum threads inside the clay jars, adding in good faith that the artefacts were consistent with a collapsing society hoarding salvage from an older age.

While I waited six hours on a crate outside my tent for the signal to travel three light-hours there and three light-hours back, chewing a cold ration biscuit with only a tallow dip burning beside my boot, a fledgling came high-stepping through the barley straw. She was about two years old, speckled buff and soot-brown on the breast, and she had not yet mastered the adult habit of never looking straight at anything. Every three steps she turned her head, stole a bright-eyed peek straight at my bootlaces, and drew a low, warning hiss from two aunts perched in the dark behind the dry-stone wall.

"That is Under-the-Steps," Cistern called dryly from the blackness beyond the rain-barrel. "She was named four weeks ago for a shiny tin buckle she buried under the steps of the flock's meeting hall while forty relatives watched, and she still thinks being looked at is a game. Keep your hands in your lap or she will spend your boots."

Under-the-Steps hopped onto an upturned salt-barrel four feet in front of my crate, cocked her speckled head at forty-five degrees, and stared directly into my left eye. When I started to avert my face so I wouldn't frighten her, she gave an indignant squeak.

"Don't move your head," Under-the-Steps piped in thin trade-speech. "Shut your eyes."

Tired of being treated like a walking plague that turned carts and anchovies into carrion, I humoured her and shut them.

"Open," she commanded right in front of my nose. She was leaning forward on her two front toes, sighting along my nose toward my left pupil from six inches away. "Shut. Wait. Now open. Now shut. Open again."

By the scrape of her scaly zygodactyl claws shifting two inches left and two inches right along the barrel-head, I could hear her checking my left eye from three different angles in the dim tallow light. Behind the stone wall, nobody hissed at her; twenty adults were listening in dead silence to a game she had been coached to play.

"Keep them shut," Under-the-Steps said on the sixth round. A gust of damp night wind blew across the square, smelling of wet feathers, hot tallow, and crushed mint from the ditch, and someone pinched out the tallow wick beside my knee. Softly, dry claws padded through the straw behind my crate. A bird's beak tapped once against my right shoulder-blade, the exact warning rap parents on the ridge gave a nestling that tried to look round. Then two warm, scaly forward toes touched the back of my right wrist, pried my curled fingers open, and pressed a small warm pebble into my palm from behind while two rear toes folded my fist closed over it and held it shut for three seconds. I waited until the claws retreated behind the granary wall before slipping inside my tent, lacing the flap, and pulling my wool blanket over my head in the pitch dark.

Under the blanket, I rolled the warm pebble between thumb and forefinger. It was no pebble at all, but a twelve-sided block of heavy metal, its pentagonal faces lapped so mirror-smooth my skin wrung to them like gauge-blocks; when I twisted two opposite faces in the dark, it unscrewed silently along a hair-fine internal thread whose seam my thumbnail could not find when closed. Even as my fingertips smelled fresh cutting-oil in the threads, my certainty insisted a starving child had dug an ancient precision weight out of a burial mound.

In the corner of my dark sight, my number sat at 39.9998, untouched. Touch doesn't go in the log. The Inquiry only trusted cameras and microphones.

Six hours after I had sent my dispatch, just before dawn, my terminal chimed under the cot. I buried the twelve-sided metal block inside my wool sock and read the Desk's reply:

*Narrative assessments are not evidence under the Hessary rules. Your attached number is 39.9998. Continue.*

## Day 37. 39.9990

To stop myself from spending the district's tools by glancing at sheds, I unfolded my camp table in the square on day twelve and emptied out my sister's two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle of a harbour at dusk. By day thirty-seven I had the stone breakwater, the copper-lit herring boats, and most of the slate-blue water assembled face up in the open air. To the birds, displaying two thousand pieces of painted pasteboard face up under the sky where any stranger could take a look was either raving madness or a desperate public bid for a trusted one. After a week of arguing behind the walls, the flock decided I was begging for someone to share my memory.

I knew they had decided because Under-the-Steps trotted up behind my stool, reached one brown wing over my elbow with her face carefully averted, pinched a single dark-blue water piece out of the middle of the harbour, and bolted into the nettle ditch with it.

"She is making your board safe," Cistern explained from her perch on the dry-stone wall behind me, preening her grey throat. "Now that she has hidden one piece under the clay for you, no passing stranger can ever have a look at the whole harbour, so it cannot be spent."

"Tell her thank you," I said, staring at the rough grey hole in the cardboard water.

Things were handed to me in the dark daily now: a braided silver cooling-cable behind the latrine trench, a warm curved optical lens by the dead fire, a stoppered horn vial of purified salt tucked into my belt from behind. Every time my fingers measured the workshop finish in the dark of my tent, my certainty dutifully bent the evidence into another piece of pre-collapse salvage hoarded by desperate scavengers. And in daylight, whenever my eyes were open, the rule never softened: ten minutes before I sat down at the puzzle that morning, I had glanced past the granary at an elder holding a brass leveling weight on a builder's string against a wall, and he had instantly bitten through the cord, kicked clay over the brass weight in the mud, and stalked off, leaving another cache spent.

My terminal chimed on the table corner with the Desk's reply to my eighteen-page caloric mass-balance calculation:

*Hypothetical subsurface metabolic inferences carry an uncorroborated weight of −0.00001 under the Hessary rules. Your attached number is 39.9990. Continue.*

Rain began spotting the cardboard sails around the hole in the harbour. I pulled an oilcloth over the puzzle and opened the installation's maintenance overlay in the corner of my vision, where the manual override cursor blinked beside *40.0000*. I could key in zero right now and feel the warm iron band behind my left eyebrow let go.

"You have that grey look around your left eyelid," Cistern said over her shoulder from the wall, "like a bird trying to cough up a plum-stone. Edit the thing if you want to edit it."

"If I edit it, they bin my report and send someone else, fresh, at forty," I said, resting my thumb in the empty dent in the harbour water. "The Hessary rules void any officer who lowers her own number, and a replacement lands before my shuttle clears orbit."

I closed the menu and watched 39.9990 burn against the wet oilcloth. "On my first mission," I told the back of Cistern's neck, "they sent me to a dry world called Gennet at forty. For ninety days everything I saw in their adobe towns looked to my head like the last brave summer before the salt-table poisoned their wells, so I brought down orbital borers, trenched through three hundred miles of their limestone ridge, and built a thermal desalination grid. When I left at minus two, the elders gave me a carved pink salt-block and thanked me. Only after the issued certainty came out of my head on the ship home did I realise I had no way to know whether Gennet had ever needed my grid, or whether they just smiled and gave the dangerous woman a salt carving so she would take her boring machines away. I have never known, because my log was written at forty, and I would very much like to know."

My left temple didn't throb when I said it; the installation only policed certainty about this world, so a doubt about Gennet slipped right past the gate, cold and sharp and entirely mine.

When night fell black enough that I could not see my boots, I tied my wool scarf twice over my eyes. Cistern dropped off the wall behind my stool, turned my right palm up with two warm front toes, and pressed a sealed glass ampoule the size of my little finger into my hand. It was faintly warm from internal radioactive decay.

"That is californium, a synthetic heavy metal that does not exist in nature and decays in a few centuries, which we breed in our reactors as a target for the accelerator ring," Cistern murmured behind my ear. "In my table underground, we hold the long-lived elements as pure samples in sealed cells. The short-lived ones, like astatine, which falls apart in hours, francium, and most of the elements past einsteinium, last seconds or less than a blink, so no bottle can hold them. The only place a short-lived element is ever kept is in someone's memory of seeing it made live at the detector, and only a trusted one's memory counts as mine. My mate and I witnessed every short-lived element through 117 together before the lung-rot took him nine winters ago. Since he died I have made 118 and 119 alone, which do not count, and nobody has ever made 120. Last week I went down for a metal sample and dug under three wrong flagstones before I found the plug. I am forgetting my corners, Perpetua Lusk. If I die without a second memory, my table dies in the dark with me, and if I show your eye, it is spent. The flock is calling a moot, a full meeting of the district underground."

## Day 114. 39.9969

Six days before the window closed, my terminal woke me at dawn with a routing notice from the Desk:

*Advisory: At current trajectory (attached number 39.9969), threshold 0.0000 will not be met by Day 120. Remediation Task Force Seven (10,400 personnel, 42 orbital survey barges) is slaved to your beacon for jump execution at Day 120, 00:00 UTC. Please clear the primary landing hollow of livestock.*

That night, after the last tallow lamp in the square was pinched out, I shut my eyes and let Cistern bind a four-fold strip of black fuller's cloth around my head, tucked tight around my nose with her beak. Since day 110 she had been down in the deep workshop running the accelerator beam day and night, coming up only after dark to lead me. She hooked one rear toe into my belt and guided me behind the old cistern wall down a spiral staircase cut into the living rock. As we descended, the smell of wet clay gave way to a warm, dry draft of ozone, hot beeswax insulation, and deep limestone water. Far below our boots, the dressed basalt steps pulsed not with the whine of rotary machinery, but with the slow breathing of massive leather valves and what sounded like hundreds of bass flutes singing a four-note canon through stone pipes.

Cistern seated me on a curved basalt bench at the outer rim of a domed subterranean hall. In a flock moot, as they called their district assembly, everyone stood on the stone tiers facing outward toward the rock walls so nobody was watched while speaking.

"Speak plain," Cistern called from the tier below my knees, facing the masonry. "Tell them what comes on day 120, and why a pile of gold won't stop it."

"My number tonight is 39.9969," I told the dark hall, keeping my eyes shut behind the cloth. "If I'm above zero on day 120, Remediation comes: ten thousand staff, all of them with cameras. Every cache you have is spent by spring."

"What if we carry up two hundred ingots of pure gold and pile them by your tent at sunrise?" called a voice across the dome.

"It won't move my number past thirty-seven," I said. "The actuaries set my starting certainty at forty to sit above anything a collapsing world could fake, hoard, or loot from an older ruin. Gold and stable metals can be dug out of an ancient tomb, so showing me metal or cut glass moves the installation only about three orders of magnitude. The only observation that clears forty in one step is something that cannot be kept on a shelf because it decays microseconds after you make it. The installation's own physics model puts the chance that a world in crisis can make element 120 live, in a working accelerator of its own, at under one in ten to the forty-fifth power. That moves my number by forty-five."

The dome broke into an immediate clattering uproar as three hundred birds counted aloud what ten thousand helmet-cameras and radar barges would spend if they landed: the medical chemical benches, the optical-glass annealing vaults, the seed shafts, four centuries of family caches. Without the caches to purify metal and blow glass for, they would have to abandon the furnaces and the chemical benches where their medicines were made.

Then a heavy beak struck the basalt floor three times in the center ring, and the counting stopped.

"I am Seven-Holes," said an ancient voice in hard Concern standard. Cistern had told me about Seven-Holes, the flock's eldest, who as a fledgling seventy winters back had dug seven decoy holes in the square and filled only one, a joke the flock still told its chicks. She was not joking tonight.

"You," Seven-Holes said across the dome, "stopped by my south nest-wall on day nineteen to adjust your boot-strap and stared into the elderberry roots where my grandmother's brass surveying transit and three etched tin river-maps lay. Because you took a look at the oilcloth, they have lain spent in the mud for ninety-five days while the slugs walk over them. Now Under-the-Cistern asks leave to bring this outsider down to our two-kilometre ring, untie her rag, and let the thing in her eye take a look at the great hall and the detector while the beam runs. An outsider who sees one cache learns the depth of our stone, the smell of our wax, and the hand of the digger, so every cache on this world is weaker for it. I say we seal the stairways with rock and let her ten thousand dig in the mud."

"Their barges have ground-penetrating radar that sees through rock," Cistern answered from the tier below me.

"Then let them dig!" Seven-Holes cried, her claws scraping the flagstone. "If we show the outsider the ring hall, we spend the root of everything we make with our own claws. Their cameras won't spend one cache. They'll spend all of them, and the furnaces were only ever for the caches."

Nobody contradicted her on what the furnaces were for: their glass, their power, and the medicines that kept their chicks alive existed only as by-products of keeping the caches and the assaying tables, and if every cache was spent, the furnaces would go cold for good.

"If they come, every cache is spent by spring, and the medicine benches with them," Cistern answered steadily. "If I open my table to the thing in her eye on day 118, only the ring hall and my bench are spent, and your workshops stay dark and working. I am old, my mate is nine years dead, and last month I lost a sample plug under three flagstones. When I die, my table dies in the dark with me anyway. Let the machine's archive outlive me and remember where my elements sat, and keep your three thousand holes dark."

"We vote," Seven-Holes said coldly. "Two taps of the rear claw to grant Under-the-Cistern leave to spend the ring hall and her table on the outsider. One tap of the bill to forbid."

Nearly three hundred pairs of rear claws struck the basalt twice to carry the vote for Cistern, followed when the echo died by a single pistol-crack as Seven-Holes drove her beak once into the stone in formal dissent and walked out past my bench, her stiff wing-feathers brushing my knee without reconciliation.

## Day 117. 39.9968

On the hundred and seventeenth night, Cistern brought me down below the moot dome into her workshop with my eyelids shut tight beneath the doubled black cloth. The air was forty degrees warmer and vibrated against my teeth with the deep four-part nasal singing I had heard through the rock, mixed with the heavy rumble of underground water turbines spinning in the deep river beneath the floor to power the ring magnets, while the waste heat was carried away by the freezing subterranean stream so nothing showed from orbit.

At the end of the walkway Cistern lifted my right hand with her left foot and guided my bare fingertips onto an open collar of the two-kilometre accelerator conduit. Inside the cooling jacket I felt the superconducting wire, finer than sewing thread, laid turn against turn without a flaw, and along the bronze ribs the tiny crescent crimp marks where three generations of assayers had wound the coils entirely by beak and claw, holding tension with two rear toes while the horn notch of the upper mandible tucked each wire home.

Then she turned me to a long curved bench of polished black slate cut with recessed alcoves, and in total darkness walked my hands across her table.

"Every piece we pressed into your palm from behind in the dark," Cistern said, using her scaly feet to set my wrists over the first recesses and the smooth point of her beak to steer my index finger onto the tally notches cut into the slate lip, "the twelve-sided block, the lens, the silver cable, the warm californium ampoule, came off this bench and this ring, so your hands would know my table before your eyes were ever opened, the way a chick is fed."

With my eyes shut tight behind the cloth, I touched four of the long-lived sample cells: a light, chill glass bulb at the first alcove; a small, impossibly heavy metal bead halfway down the bench that dented my fingertip like lead shot; the warm quartz ampoule of californium she had placed back in its recess; and a tiny lead-shielded sapphire window beside it. Beyond those, in the alcoves for the short-lived elements, my fingers found no glass bulbs or metal beads, only polished brass stalls wired in silver braid to the detector of the accelerator ring behind the bench, where an element that lasted microseconds was held only by being made live while a trusted one watched.

At alcove 115, Cistern steered my finger onto the empty brass stall; her feathers smelled of hot slate, clean oil, and dried clover. She leaned her bill right against my ear and whispered in Concern standard the exact four-step alpha-decay energies in mega-electronvolts and the millisecond half-lives she and her mate had measured there ten winters ago.

Behind my closed eyelids, the green digits in the corner of my sight flickered from *39.9968* to *39.9961*.

I jerked my hand back and clapped my palm gently over her beak. "Don't speak," I whispered fiercely. "Shutting my eyes only shuts its sight; its ears never close. Ordinary talk is testimony and moves nothing, while those decay numbers are something the model trusts a hair more than talk, because a ruin's old records could hold them too, so my number just moved 0.0007."

Cistern went dead still under my hands, her rapid heart beating three times against my wrist, and then let out a tiny whistle of dry embarrassment.

"It shines when it looks, and it doesn't shine when you listen," she said quietly, pulling her claw back from stall 115 because she had spent a little of it by speaking its numbers. "We thought shutting your eyes shut all of it, and that talk was safe because talk never moved it."

I stood in the dark with one hand on stall 115 and the other on her warm shoulder as ninety days rearranged themselves in my head. "When my eyelids are open and the installation records sight through my optic nerves, its tiny relay lens behind my left pupil glows in the ultraviolet. That was Under-the-Steps's game on the salt-barrel."

"Shut your eyes, open them, shut them," Cistern said dryly, steering my fingers forward onto the empty brass stall of 117, the last square she and her mate had witnessed together. "Every time your eyelids came up, that greedy violet star lit up behind your left pupil, while your right eye stayed dark as a chick's, and when you shut both lids the star went out. We stopped hiding from you on day nine. We hide from the thing that looks out of you."

Every cart and leveling weight that had vanished in daylight since day nine had been snatched from the glowing violet lens in my left socket, and every piece from her bench pressed into my palm from behind in the dark had been how you build a second memory. By teaching my hands her table with my eyes shut, where the installation could log nothing, Cistern had made me her trusted one: what I knew was hers, so my knowing spent nothing, until tomorrow when I untied the cloth at the detector for element 120.

## Day 118. 39.9961

At dawn on day 118, standing beside Cistern at the end of the curved slate bench, I untied the four-fold black linen strip and opened my eyes for the first time underground. The vaulted basalt hall stretched a hundred metres under ribs of pale green glass tubing, where four hundred birds on three tiered timber scaffolds were treading the ash-wood rocker bars of the furnace bellows and vacuum pumps with their two-forward, two-back toes in unison, singing their four-part bass chorus while the two-kilometre beak-wound accelerator ring curved out of the western rock tunnel into the central detector behind Cistern's slate bench. Because my eyelids were up and the violet star shone in my left pupil, every one of the four hundred birds on the scaffolds stood facing outward into the dark basalt walls.

Cistern had kept the beam of titanium-50 atoms running into the spinning californium-249 target drum since day 110, because an atom of element 120 fuses only once every few days, and no one can say which hour it will strike. I pulled a wooden stool up to the detector screen above the hundred-and-twentieth stall and sat with both eyes wide open.

"Because the installation taps both of your optic nerves," Cistern said softly beside my elbow, her grey throat-feathers raised and her face turned three-quarters away toward the dark detector glass, "you cannot look for me without the machine looking for your office. Your seeing the atom completes my table, because you are my trusted one and your memory is mine; the thing behind your eye seeing it spends my table, because it is an outsider that reports home. It is the exact same look, completing my work and spending it together, and I have chosen it. Keep your eyes open, Perpetua Lusk."

I kept them open for eleven hours. By noon the warm draft from the pump scaffolds had dried my corneas until every blink felt like grit, and still the detector glass stayed dark. Whenever my lids drooped, I pinched the skin of my wrist until it bled and stared harder into the coordinate grid, refusing to let the atom flash while my eyes were shut.

For ten hours the hall itself moved my number nothing, because a ruin can have a hall and survivors to work it; the installation wanted the atom. In the eleventh hour, a silver bell inside the detector pinged, and a cold violet-white star branched across the screen in three alpha-decay tracks, ringing the bell three times in forty microseconds before the atom vanished. I held my eyes wide open, keeping the branching angle and the three silver notes in my memory for Cistern while the installation logged the decay spectrum and sent the packet home.

Down in the chasm, the four-part singing stopped on a single beat. My look had taken in the whole hall, the workshops and furnaces too, so Seven-Holes had been right. Four hundred birds stepped off the bellows bars, left their horn crimpers and glass blowpipes uncovered on the benches, and walked out in silence through the side-tunnels without turning round. Beside me, Cistern took her foot off the floor-bar, leaving the lead shutters open and every glass bulb untouched in its alcove. Once an outsider has taken a look, a cache cannot be saved by moving it. Her table was complete, and her table was spent, in the same forty microseconds. She stepped back, leaving forty-two winters of purified elements under the green light like cracked pots in a mud yard.

In the upper corner of my sight, the green digits blurred as the installation subtracted the forty-five points its model gave that single impossible atom from my number of 39.9961, and the warm iron band behind my left temple snapped inside out. A sudden, flooding tide of physical relief washed through my neck and chest, so clean it almost buckled my knees: the crisis was false, Task Force Seven was cancelled, the world was safe.

I took three steps after Cistern toward the foot of the spiral basalt stairs, breathing in that warm, sweet certainty, before I stopped on the bottom flagstone and recognized the exact texture of what I was feeling.

My number said −5.0039. I was exactly as sure as I had been, the other way, on the word of the same machine.

The same unaudited model that had watched two hundred birds scatter chaff under a probe and certified them as starving savages at forty had just graded its own homework at minus five point zero zero three nine, handing me a fresh certainty just as smooth and impervious to doubt as the old one.

At the top of the spiral stairs, in the dark archway before the daylight yard, Cistern stopped, and I shut my eyes without being told. Her scaly claws stepped behind my shoulder, her beak gave one light tap against my right shoulder-blade, and two warm forward toes pried my left fist open from behind to press a small, heavy, six-sided object into my palm before her rear toes folded my fingers tight around it.

"It's yours," Cistern said into the dark behind my ear. "Don't look."

When I opened my eyes a minute later, she was walking away across the mud square with her tailfeathers toward me. By that morning I had spent thirty-eight caches across the district by looking, including the yearling's copper wire, the cart, the elder's brass leveling weight, Cistern's ring hall and table, and the transit under Seven-Holes' nest-wall; none of them were ever touched again, and Seven-Holes never forgave me.

## —

When a field officer returns to Mercer's Ring, the medical technicians clear the mission number out of her head, but the recording installation behind the optic nerves stays in for life, logging everything she ever looks at for the Concern's archive. If I ever opened my eyes on Cistern's gift, the installation would log it and spend it; and if the Office ever added touch to the installation, even handling it with bare fingers in the dark would spend it too. So before I boarded the retrieval cutter on day 120, I sat in my dark tent with a wool scarf tied twice over my closed eyes, welded Cistern's gift inside a lead-lined steel specimen box by touch, and filed the latch seam flat.

In my final report I wrote plainly that our issued certainty of 40.0000 was defective and had forced the destruction of an advanced civilization's primary workshop before it could update, knowing that indicting the system ended my promotion to the Actuarial Office. The forty-microsecond recording of the ring hall and element 120 went into the Concern's archive, a witness that will outlive Cistern as she chose and never trusted; her world was struck from the crisis list, and the survey probes stopped coming over her valley. Twenty minutes after I signed off, the Desk sent its close-out note:

*Final telemetry logged at attached number −5.0039. Remediation Task Force Seven stood down. Narrative addendum separated from evidentiary record and forwarded to Administrative Review.*

Three months later, after I had been reassigned to dock inspection, a clerk in the Actuarial Office named Harriet Baines called me up to a room three floors above the glass archive where they keep the Hessary notebooks.

"Because the issued certainty staffs every mission in the Concern, your addendum triggered a service-wide review of the starting number for every future agent, Officer Lusk," Harriet Baines said in a dry, level voice, squaring a folder on her blotter. "More importantly, we have closed the unlogged channel you documented between days nine and one hundred and seventeen. Starting next quarter, we are adding sub-dermal tactile sensors in all ten fingertips to the standard installation refit, so touch goes into the audit log from now on. No future officer will learn one thing with her hands while her head logs another."

I noted that the Office had learned how to close the birds' trick much faster than it had learned to doubt itself, declined the fingertip refit by taking a permanent dock instructor's post that carried no field assignments, and kept the welded steel box on the shelf above my bunk, untouched and unlooked-at so it is never spent.

Next to the steel box leans the two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle of the harbour at dusk, glued to its oiled board and finished right down to the last copper-lit mast. One rough grey square is still missing from the middle of the slate-blue water where Under-the-Steps hid the piece under the clay to make it safe for me. Sometimes in the evening I sit at my table with my thumb resting in that empty dent in the cardboard harbour, thinking of the carved pink salt-block on Gennet and wondering whether thirty thousand people ever needed my desalination grid at all.
