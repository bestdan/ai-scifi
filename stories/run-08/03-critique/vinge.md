# Stage 3 critique: Vinge lens, run-08

**Blind spots, stated.** This lens has plain prose and technothriller
instincts, and it treats characters as functions. It pushes futurity even where
a story's power is human scale, so it will undervalue D and B. Chiang and Banks
should push back on those two. I judge Futurity by "which side of the horizon
is the viewpoint on", at about 5,000 words.

## A. The Prior

Futurity reaches F4 on the birds. The bird mind is the one non-human mind in
the field that shows reasoning on the page (it reasons about her gaze). The
service is F3: an issued prior is a policy tool, and the agent is a person in a
uniform. Still too familiar: "the service" as an aid agency, and the guild
debate, which is a committee scene. The viewpoint is on our side of the
horizon, which is right. The trick that holds is that the agent can read her own
log-odds, so her belief is visible to the reader without anyone claiming to see
inside a superintelligence. The Bayes risk is real, but it is arithmetic and
can be kept light. One change to push it further: let the birds model her
prior. They have worked out she is installed, and they hide things *from the
installation*, not from her. That puts the other mind a step ahead of the
viewpoint, shown only by effects.

## B. The Ending Technician

It reaches F3 and stays there. The lunar town is a safety culture, and the
service is offstage. Too familiar: the whole setting, a radio-quiet far side,
plus a retiring engineer and a hatch. It is today's people with a fresh
psychology idea attached. The viewpoint holds, but there is no horizon to look
at. The town's treatment of the remembering self as a second person is the best
piece of futurity here and it is a few lines. One change: make the remembering
self literally a second party in the town. It has a name, a seat on the shift
board and a ledger of what it has been told, so the custom is an institution
and not a mood. The sentimental landing is the likeliest failure.

## C. The Signatory

It has the highest reach, F4 to F5, because fork-and-rollback minds at a
million-fold speed are past the wall. It is also the one place where the story
would be tempted to claim sight into them. Familiar elements: the kiln-master
and the charter read as a guild and a law code. The viewpoint trick is slowing
the agent to local speed, which does hold, since the slow narrator is the only
one who can be written. But it is the "agent made smaller" attractor, and three
devices won't fit 7,500 words. One change: drop the slowing as a separate
device and let the firing itself be the narrowing. The agent loses nothing
first. The kiln simply runs at the town's speed.

## D. The Fee

It reaches F3, with a good F3 core: kinship redefined. The service is the
strange society. Too familiar: the dumpling stall, the foster brother, the
market council, which is a family drama in a market with a futuristic fee.
The viewpoint is a human, inside the horizon, fine. What isn't written is why
this has lasted two million years. If the service has fostered children for
that long, its children are the service, and the story doesn't use that. One
change: let the agent's own foster-fee be the example. Her world was paid for
in help, so she is the thing the fee bought, and she can price her mother's
forgery in the service's own currency. I expect this is the most human of the
five, and I'm the wrong lens to rate that.

## E. North for Lunch

It reaches F4. The zone boundary is a geography that sorts minds, which is
Vinge's own scaffold, and it is used as a political institution. Too familiar:
the train, the lunch and the dye-works owner. The persuasion plot is a sales
call. The viewpoint trick is the strongest in the field: an observer who is
losing sophistication mile by mile is a live demonstration of the wall. It
holds if the narration degrades in a way the reader can read, and it fails if
it turns into the narrator announcing "I am getting dumber". The gift being a
mind that can't go north without being lessened is a good second idea. The
risk is the diminished-agent attractor, but here the diminishing is the point
of the geography. One change: put the dining car on the line, so the gift
is talking to the agent through the boundary and the reader hears both
registers in one scene.

## Ranking

1. **A, The Prior.** The most distance, a non-human mind that shows its
   reasoning, and the decisive proof is a thing given at a cost.
2. **E, North for Lunch.** The best viewpoint trick and the clearest zone
   mechanic, with attractor risk.
3. **C, The Signatory.** Highest ceiling, too heavy for the length.
4. **D, The Fee.** Strong stakes, F3, and the service-as-strange-society
   needs to be shown.
5. **B, The Ending Technician.** Fresh idea, F3 and offstage service.

**Why A should win.** The brief's seed asks for a service sent to fix a world
that doesn't need it, and A answers the question the audit says the map leaves
open: how a service comes to believe a fine world needs it. It answers with the
agent's own belief, so the mechanism is on the page and not in a sensor. The
reader-profile pull is a mind that is genuinely not human, and the birds are
that: they reason about her gaze and play it. The estimate of 6,000 words
passes under the 7,500 ceiling, so load is not a reason to cut it. My one
reservation is the instrument-misreads attractor: A avoids it only if the
installed prior is told as policy, with the actuaries' reasoning stated, and
the world's hiding is told as culture. If the draft lets the world look
primitive because a sensor missed it, A falls into the cluster the audit
flagged. My push is for the birds to model her prior, and I would keep that
over the cache-shows-the-service turn if the length forces a cut.
