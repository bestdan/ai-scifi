# Everything You Look At

## Day 1. 40.0000

The first thing I fixed on the planet was a wheel. It belonged to a cart in a yard full of straw and broken pots, and it wobbled, and I am qualified in wheels. I trued it in twenty minutes. When I looked up, the cart was gone, the straw was gone, and a grey bird the size of a heron was standing where the cart had been, facing the other way, very deliberately not watching me. My number said 40.0000. It always says 40.0000. I took this as evidence of a society too traumatised to own a cart.

My name is Perpetua Lusk, and I was on my third mission for the Concern, which is the service I work for, which fixes worlds. Three clean missions get a field officer into the Actuarial Office on Mercer's Ring, where you set the numbers instead of carrying them. I had brought spanners, axle grease, and a two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle my sister had sent before I shipped out to this chalk hollow of wet marl and boiled turnip greens.

The dry-stone cottages were roofed with sagging thatch weighted by cracked clay jars, their doorways choked with split reed baskets and damp barley chaff. When I walked to the stone well-curb to mend its split timber windlass, two tall birds squatting by a rain-barrel stood up, unhooked the bucket without glancing at me, dropped the rope down the shaft, and carried the bucket behind a wall.

I knelt by the well-curb, laid out my brass shims, and looked around for a hammer-stone. Forty paces off by a nettle ditch, a smaller bird with barred brown flanks crouched in the dirt. It had zygodactyl feet, two scaly toes gripping forward and two braced back, and was tucking braided copper wire and two green glass beads under a flat slate. The instant my face turned that way, the bird froze; its yellow eye caught the angle of my nose, and every nape feather stood straight up.

Frantically it hooked the wire and beads back out with its rear claws, bolted ten paces behind the rain-barrel, pried up a cobblestone with its beak, and shoved its hoard underneath. Then it craned around the barrel stave to check my face. I was still watching, never having seen a foot and beak used like carpenter's pincers. Seeing that my eyes had tracked the move, the bird flattened its crest, stepped off the cobblestone, and walked away without looking back.

"You spent it," said the heron-sized grey bird standing by the cart-tracks. She was addressing a clump of dock weeds ten feet to my left, her grizzled throat feathers ruffling. "Then you spent it again."

"I didn't touch it," I said, holding up my shim. "It's still under the cobble by the barrel."

"It is in the dirt," the grey bird said in stiff Concern standard, "and the other half is inside your head. You took a look at where she put it, and when she moved it, you took a look at the move too. Now you have a look at both holes. If you know what is in a cache and where it lies, you can rob it in thought and follow her toes next time. Once an outsider has seen a cache, it is spent. Moving it while the outsider watches only spends the new hole too."

She shifted her two-forward, two-back toes in the mud, keeping the back of her narrow skull aligned with my nose. "I am Under-the-Cistern. Call me Cistern, since your mouth lacks the click in the middle. The flock drew straws behind the granary this morning and I lost, so I am here to deal with you while you ruin the furniture."

"I trued your cartwheel," I said. "It had a three-centimetre wobble."

"It was a very good cart," Cistern told the weeds. "Then you stared at the hub for twenty minutes with your eyes wide open, so the brothers had to abandon it in the nettle gulley."

I looked up at the corner of my vision, where my number hung in steady green digits against the overcast sky: 40.0000. Everything they valued, they hid when watched; anything seen was abandoned as spent; the surface was kept as worthless straw and broken pots because the surface was watched. I diagnosed acute resource-paranoia from multi-generational collapse, and felt certain they needed me terribly.

## Day 4. 39.9999

On the fourth morning Cistern walked me up the chalk ridge, keeping two paces ahead with her tailfeathers squarely in my face, the height of local manners. In the thorn hedges, parents were feeding chicks from behind, tapping a nestling between the wings before reaching over its shoulder to drop a steamed root or salted mussel into its bill. A chick's first lesson is to take what it is given without turning round; when one hatchling twisted to peek at its father's claws, both parents hissed and rapped its crown.

"At fledging," Cistern told a milestone as we passed, "every chick digs one decoy cache in the square while the whole flock watches. It is the only cache a bird ever shows everyone, and we name you for it. Mine was a lead ribbon under the old cistern."

"And after fledging you never show anyone?" I asked, watching my boots.

"You show your trusted ones," Cistern said, shifting into the brisk clatter of gossip. "A mated pair in the Kiln-Wall flock showed each other four hundred sites in their first spring. Showing a cache to a trusted one is our deepest intimacy, and spends nothing. Trust is made by pressing things into a hand from behind in the dark, accepted unseen."

"Why doesn't a trusted one's sight spend it?"

"If I trust you, what you know is mine. That's what trust is for. Somebody has to remember where I put things." Cistern scratched the grey feathers at her throat with one scaly foot. "We hold thousands of sites and forget as we age; a trusted one is your second memory. A dying bird shows her caches to her trusted ones, who inherit by knowing. Dying with no trusted ones takes your caches with you, the worst death we know."

Suddenly Cistern cocked her eye toward the clouds and clicked her beak twice. Birds see in ultraviolet, where an orbital survey probe glints overhead like a flare. Instantly two hundred birds kicked over peat braziers, scattered root baskets, and ran in erratic zigzags while throwing barley chaff over their heads. I recognized the exact choreography from my mission briefing: the survey footage our office had logged as famine panic. Three minutes later, when the probe dropped below the ridge, everyone brushed off the chaff and went back to chewing roots.

"Greedy violet eye in the sky," Cistern muttered. "Our real works are fifty fathoms down, where caches have always been. I am an assayer of the guild. Under the marl our deep workshops, glass-lit furnaces, and one particle accelerator ring exist for our art, the table: every element held. One through ninety-nine we hold as pure samples; from a hundred up, where heavy elements last seconds or less, we hold them only as atoms made live in the accelerator and seen at the detector by a trusted one. Everything else we make, medicine, optical glass, and power, is a by-product of the art."

On the base of her wing, where she had brushed a bench downstairs, I saw a smudge of canary-yellow powder: refined lead-monoxide assaying flux. Yet in the top corner of my sight, my number had only ticked from 40.0000 to 39.9999.

"I see the flux on your feathers, and I know that hillside panic was a drill for a satellite," I said. "It doesn't help, because of the installation, the implant behind my left eye that holds my certainty. It records everything I see and hear and sends the log home. My number is the base-ten log of the odds that your world is in crisis. It started at forty."

I looked at the green digits against the chalk. "Watching your probe drill moved it one ten-thousandth of a point, about zero point zero zero zero zero two seven four a day, a hundredth a year. At this rate I'd stop being sure in about four thousand years. A world that is fine and good at hiding, and a world in crisis that is good at public relations, produce nearly the same surface evidence. While my number is high, I can know facts that contradict the crisis, and still feel zero doubt. I'm certain you're dying. I'll stay certain for four thousand years unless you show me something that can't happen on a dying world."

"Why didn't your people set your certainty to one, if they wanted you stubborn?" Cistern asked the gorse.

"A belief at exactly one can never move, so an agent could never judge a world fixed and come home," I said. "The actuaries set it as high as possible short of that, and gave us a window of 120 days. If I'm above zero on day 120, Remediation comes: ten thousand staff, all of them with cameras."

Every feather along Cistern's spine went rigid at the word *cameras*.

"Why not just give you orders?" she asked.

"Because of the Hessary Inquiry," I said, sitting on a wet stone. "The Concern began as a plain aid service whose assessors landed open-minded. On Hessary, a world in real famine, the assessor spent thirty-one years gathering evidence and being careful while two billion people starved to death. They keep her careful notebooks in a glass room in the Concern's archive, thirty-one years of them in neat blue columns. The Hessary Inquiry concluded that doubt kills, and made our three rules: issued near-certainty, the 120-day window, and audit-only evidence from cameras and microphones. I could edit my number to zero in my menu, and they would just void my report and send a fresh agent. So now they make sure we can't doubt."

Cistern stood quiet for a long moment, understanding that you cannot argue with ten to the fortieth power. "Come down off the ridge," she said flatly, changing the subject. "If you keep looking at that lime-kiln, we will have to tear it down before supper."

## Day 9. 39.9998

By the ninth afternoon I had spent eleven more caches by accident, including a dry-stone root cellar, a copper-hooped wash-tub, and an earthenware crock of salted anchovies left in the road because I glanced through a doorway while pitching my rain-tarp. Meanwhile, while handling three cracked water-jars abandoned near my tent, my fingers had found something my camera could not see inside the dark necks: the interior clay rims were cut with machine-ground ceramic screw-threads to a tolerance of half a thou, meant to take a high-vacuum sealing cap. At dusk I opened a relay link to the Desk, our sector dispatcher six light-hours back, and typed a plain-language report stating that the surface squalor was camouflage against orbital optics and this world was doing completely fine without us.

While I waited six hours on a crate outside my tent for the speed of light to go there and come back, chewing a cold ration biscuit with only a tallow dip burning beside my boot, a fledgling came high-stepping through the barley straw. She was about two years old, speckled buff and soot-brown on the breast, and she had not yet mastered the adult habit of never looking straight at anything. Every three steps she turned her head, stole a bright-eyed peek straight at my bootlaces, and drew a low, warning hiss from two aunts perched in the dark behind the dry-stone wall.

"That is Under-the-Steps," Cistern called dryly from the blackness beyond the rain-barrel. "She was named four weeks ago for a shiny tin buckle she buried under the moot-hall steps while forty relatives watched, and she still thinks being looked at is a game. Keep your hands in your lap or she will spend your boots."

Under-the-Steps hopped onto an upturned salt-barrel four feet in front of my crate, cocked her speckled head at forty-five degrees, and stared directly into my left eye. When I started to avert my face so I wouldn't frighten her, she gave an indignant squeak.

"Don't move your head," Under-the-Steps piped in thin trade-speech. "Shut your eyes."

Tired of being treated like a walking plague that turned carts and anchovies into carrion, I humoured her and shut them.

"Open," she commanded right in front of my nose. She was leaning forward on her two front toes, sighting along my nose toward my left pupil from six inches away. "Shut. Wait. Now open. Now shut. Open again."

By the scrape of her scaly zygodactyl claws shifting two inches left and two inches right along the barrel-head, I could hear her checking my left eye from three different angles in the dim tallow light. Behind the stone wall, nobody hissed at her; twenty adults were listening in dead silence to a game she had been coached to play.

"Keep them shut," Under-the-Steps said on the sixth round. A gust of damp night wind blew across the square, smelling of wet feathers, hot tallow, and crushed mint from the ditch, and someone pinched out the tallow wick beside my knee. Softly, dry claws padded through the straw behind my crate. A bird's beak tapped once against my right shoulder-blade, the exact warning rap parents on the ridge gave a nestling that tried to look round. Then two warm, scaly forward toes touched the back of my right wrist, pried my curled fingers open, and pressed a small warm pebble into my palm from behind while two rear toes folded my fist closed over it and held it shut for three seconds. I waited until the claws retreated behind the granary wall before slipping inside my tent, lacing the flap, and pulling my wool blanket over my head in the pitch dark.

Under the blanket, I rolled the warm pebble between thumb and forefinger. It was no pebble at all, but a regular dodecahedron of heavy metal, its twelve pentagonal faces lapped so mirror-smooth my skin wrung to them like gauge-blocks; when I twisted two opposite faces in the dark, it unscrewed silently along a hair-fine internal thread whose seam my thumbnail could not find when closed.

In the corner of my dark sight, my number sat at 39.9998, untouched. Touch doesn't go in the log. The Inquiry only trusted cameras and microphones.

Four hours later, just before dawn, my terminal chimed under the cot. I buried the tungsten dodecahedron inside my wool sock and read the Desk's reply:

*Narrative assessments are not evidence under the Hessary rules. Your attached posterior is 39.9998. Continue.*

## Day 37. 39.9990

To stop myself from spending the district's tools by glancing at sheds, I unfolded my camp table in the square on day twelve and emptied out my sister's two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle of a harbour at dusk. By day thirty-seven I had the stone breakwater, the copper-lit herring boats, and most of the slate-blue water assembled face up in the open air. To the birds, displaying two thousand pieces of painted pasteboard face up under the sky where any stranger could take a look was either raving madness or a desperate public bid for a trusted one. After a week of arguing behind the walls, the flock decided I was begging for someone to share my memory.

I knew they had decided because Under-the-Steps trotted up behind my stool, reached one brown wing over my elbow with her face carefully averted, pinched a single dark-blue water piece out of the middle of the harbour, and bolted into the nettle ditch with it.

"She is making your board safe," Cistern explained from her perch on the dry-stone wall behind me, preening her grey throat. "Now that she has hidden one piece under the chalk for you, no passing stranger can ever have a look at the whole harbour, so it cannot be spent."

"Tell her thank you," I said, staring at the rough grey hole in the cardboard water.

Things were handed to me in the dark daily now: a braided silver superconducting jumper behind the latrine trench, a warm aspheric fluorite lens by the dead fire, a horn vial of anhydrous lithium salt tucked into my belt from behind. Yet in daylight the rule never softened: ten minutes before I sat down at the puzzle that morning, I had glanced past the granary at an elder setting a turned brass plumb-bob against a wall, and he had instantly bitten through the silk cord, kicked marl over the brass bob in the mud, and stalked off, leaving another cache spent.

My terminal chimed on the table corner with the Desk's reply to my eighteen-page caloric mass-balance proof:

*Hypothetical subsurface metabolic inferences carry an uncorroborated weight of −0.00001 under Hessary Schedule C. Your attached posterior is 39.9990. Continue.*

Rain began spotting the cardboard sails around the hole in the harbour. I pulled an oilcloth over the puzzle and opened the installation's maintenance overlay in the corner of my vision, where the manual override cursor blinked beside *40.0000*. I could key in zero right now and feel the warm iron band behind my left eyebrow let go.

"You have that grey look around your left eyelid," Cistern said over her shoulder from the wall, "like a bird trying to cough up a plum-stone. Edit the thing if you want to edit it."

"If I edit it, they bin my report and send someone else, fresh, at forty," I said, resting my thumb in the empty dent in the harbour water. "The Hessary rules void any officer who lowers her own prior, and a replacement lands before my shuttle clears orbit."

I closed the menu and watched 39.9990 burn against the wet oilcloth. "On my first mission," I told the back of Cistern's neck, "they sent me to a dry world called Gennet at forty. For ninety days everything I saw in their adobe towns looked to my head like the last brave summer before the salt-table poisoned their wells, so I brought down orbital borers, trenched through three hundred miles of their limestone ridge, and built a thermal desalination grid. When I left at minus two, the elders gave me a carved pink salt-block and thanked me. Only after the prior came out of my head on the ship home did I realise I had no way to know whether Gennet had ever needed my grid, or whether they just smiled and gave the dangerous woman a salt carving so she would take her boring machines away. I have never known, because my log was written at forty, and I would very much like to know."

My left temple didn't throb when I said it; the installation only policed certainty about this world, so a doubt about Gennet slipped right past the gate, cold and sharp and entirely mine.

When night fell black enough that I could not see my boots, I tied my wool scarf twice over my eyes. Cistern dropped off the wall behind my stool, turned my right palm up with two warm front toes, and pressed a sealed quartz ampoule the size of my little finger into my hand. It was faintly warm from internal alpha-decay.

"Curium-248," Cistern murmured behind my ear. "Number ninety-six. I hold one through one hundred and seventeen in my table down in the third gallery: ninety-nine pure samples in cells, and eighteen made live at the ring detector and witnessed by my mate before the lung-rot took him nine winters ago. I have made 118, 119, and 120 three times each since he died, and they do not count, because I stood at the detector alone. And last week I went down for my tellurium sample at fifty-two and dug under three wrong flagstones before I found the plug. I am forgetting my corners, Perpetua Lusk. If I die without a second memory, my table dies in the dark with me, and if I show your eye, it is spent. The flock is calling a moot."

## Day 114. 39.9969

Six days before the window closed, my terminal woke me at dawn with a routing notice from the Desk:

*Advisory: At current trajectory (posterior 39.9969), threshold 0.0000 will not be met by Day 120. Remediation Task Force Seven (10,400 personnel, 42 orbital survey barges) is slaved to your beacon for jump execution at Day 120, 00:00 UTC. Please clear the primary landing hollow of livestock.*

That night, after the last tallow lamp in the square was pinched out, I shut my eyes and let Cistern bind a four-fold strip of black fuller's cloth around my head, tucked tight around my nose with her beak. She hooked one rear toe into my belt and led me behind the old cistern wall down a spiral staircase cut into the living rock. As we descended, the smell of wet chalk gave way to a warm, dry draft of ozone, hot beeswax insulation, and deep limestone water. Far below our boots, the dressed basalt steps pulsed not with the whine of rotary machinery, but with the slow breathing of massive leather valves and what sounded like hundreds of bass flutes singing a four-note canon through stone pipes.

Cistern seated me on a curved basalt bench at the outer rim of a domed subterranean hall. From the rustle of three hundred pairs of wings and the click of six hundred scaly feet on the stone tiers, I realized every bird in the chamber stood facing outward toward the rock walls so nobody was watched while speaking.

"Speak plain," Cistern called from the tier below my knees, facing the masonry. "Tell them what comes on day 120, and why a pile of gold won't stop it."

"My number tonight is 39.9969," I told the dark hall. "If I'm above zero on day 120, Remediation comes: ten thousand staff, all of them with cameras. Every cache you have is spent by spring."

A dry hiss ran around three hundred throats. "What if we carry up two hundred ingots of refined gold and iridium from the fourth gallery and pile them by your tent at sunrise?" called a voice across the dome.

"It won't move my number past thirty-seven," I said, keeping my eyes shut behind the cloth. "The actuaries set the prior at forty specifically to sit above anything a collapsing world could fake, hoard, or loot from an older ruin. Stable metal and cut glass can be dug out of a tomb, so they move the installation about three orders of magnitude. The only observation that clears forty in one step is something that decays forty microseconds after you make it. The installation's own physics model puts the chance that a world in crisis can make element 120 live, in a working accelerator of its own, at under one in ten to the forty-fifth power. That moves my number by forty-five."

The dome broke into a clattering uproar as the flock counted aloud what ten thousand helmet-cameras and radar barges would spend: the three medical synthesis galleries under the east meadow, the optical-glass vaults, the seed shafts, four centuries of family caches behind the aqueducts. Then a heavy beak struck the basalt floor three times in the center ring.

"I am Seven-Holes," said an ancient voice in hard Concern standard. Cistern had told me about Seven-Holes, the flock's eldest, who as a fledgling seventy winters back had dug seven decoy holes in the square and filled only one, a joke the flock still told its chicks. She was not joking tonight.

"You," Seven-Holes said across the dome, "stopped by my south nest-wall on day nineteen to adjust your boot-strap and stared into the elderberry roots where my grandmother's brass transit and three etched tin survey plates lay. Because you took a look at the oilcloth, they have lain spent in the mud for ninety-five days while the slugs walk over them. Now Under-the-Cistern asks leave to bring this outsider down to our two-kilometre ring, untie her rag, and let the thing in her eye take a look at the great hall and the hundred-and-twentieth cradle. An outsider who sees one cache learns the depth of our stone, the smell of our wax, and the hand of the digger, so every cache on this world is weaker for it. And if the ten thousand come, they will see why we dig. Their cameras won't spend one cache. They'll spend all of them, and the furnaces were only ever for the caches."

Nobody contradicted her; all their deep industry, the furnaces, the glass, the power, and the medicine, existed only to serve the caches and the table.

"If they come, every cache is spent by spring," Cistern answered steadily. "If I open my table to the thing in her eye on day 118, only the ring hall and my bench are spent. I am old, my mate is nine years dead, and last month I lost my tellurium plug under three flagstones. Let the machine's log outlive me and remember where my elements sat, and keep your three thousand holes under the meadow dark."

Nearly three hundred pairs of rear claws struck the basalt twice to carry the vote for Cistern, followed by a single pistol-crack as Seven-Holes drove her beak once into the stone in dissent and walked out past my bench, her stiff wing-feathers brushing my knee without reconciliation.

## Day 117. 39.9968

On the hundred and seventeenth night, Cistern brought me down below the moot dome into the third gallery with my eyelids shut tight beneath the doubled black cloth. The air was forty degrees warmer and vibrated with the deep four-part nasal singing I had heard through the rock. As Cistern led me along a basalt catwalk with one warm foot wrapped around my wrist, I heard how a people without hands built a particle accelerator fifty fathoms under a turnip yard. Ten feet below the railing, teams of forty birds were treading the ash-wood rocker bars of the primary furnace bellows and vacuum pumps, two front toes locked over the cleats and two rear toes hooked under the return irons, throwing their weight in unison to their song while bronze manifolds vented every calorie of waste heat down into freezing subterranean karst aquifers invisible from orbit.

At the end of the catwalk Cistern lifted my right hand with her left foot and guided my bare fingertips onto an open collar of the two-kilometre accelerator ring. Inside the nitrogen jacket I felt the niobium-tin superconducting filament, finer than sewing thread, laid turn against turn without a flaw, and along the bronze ribs the tiny crescent crimp marks where three generations of assayers had wound the coils entirely by beak and claw, holding tension with two rear toes while the horn notch of the upper mandible tucked each wire home.

Then she turned me to a long curved bench of polished black slate cut with one hundred and twenty recessed alcoves, and for an hour, in total darkness, walked me through her table by touch. She used her scaly feet to set my wrists over each alcove and the tip of her beak to steer my index finger onto the tally notches before letting me touch the cell inside: chill liquid hydrogen at one, a dense bead of arc-melted osmium at seventy-six that dented my skin like lead shot, warm curium and californium at ninety-seven and ninety-eight, and the lead-shielded sapphire window of einsteinium at ninety-nine. From one hundred up, my fingers found only empty brass witness-cradles wired in silver braid to the ring detector, where heavy elements lived only by being made live while a trusted one watched.

At alcove 115, Cistern steered my finger onto the brass horns; her feathers smelled of hot slate, clean oil, and dried clover. She leaned her bill right against my ear and whispered in the rapid click-and-whistle assaying tongue of her guild the exact calcium-beam cross-section and four-alpha decay chain she and her mate had run there ten winters ago.

Behind my closed eyelids, the green digits in the corner of my sight flickered from *39.9968* to *39.9961*.

I jerked my hand back and clapped my palm gently over her beak. "Don't speak," I whispered fiercely against her feathers. "My number just moved seven ten-thousandths of a point. The parser on my auditory nerve just matched your nuclear decay terms against its lexicon."

Cistern went dead still under my hands, her rapid heart beating three times against my wrist, and then let out a tiny whistle of dry embarrassment.

"It shines when it looks," she said very quietly. "We knew it heard, but we thought it heard us the way you hear birds. It doesn't shine when you listen, so we thought it was deaf to words."

I stood in the dark with one hand on cradle 115 and the other on her warm shoulder as ninety days rearranged themselves in my head. "When the installation records through my optic tract, the beam-splitter behind my left pupil fluoresces in the ultraviolet. That was Under-the-Steps's game on the salt-barrel."

"Shut your eyes, open them, shut them," Cistern said dryly, steering my fingers forward onto the empty brass cradle of 117, the last square she and her mate had witnessed together. "Every time your eyelids came up, that greedy violet star lit up behind your left pupil, while your right eye stayed dark as a chick's. We stopped hiding from you on day nine. We hide from the thing that looks out of you."

Every cart and plumb-bob that had vanished in daylight since day nine had been snatched from the glowing violet camera in my left socket, and every tungsten block, lens, and curium ampoule pressed into my palm from behind in the dark had been how you feed a fledgling and build a second memory. By walking my hands across all ninety-nine samples and eighteen cradles with my eyes shut, Cistern had made me her trusted one: what I knew was hers, so my knowing spent nothing, until tomorrow morning when I opened both eyes at alcove 120.

## Day 118. 39.9961

At dawn on day 118, standing beside Cistern at the foot of the curved slate bench, I untied the four-fold black linen strip and opened my eyes. The vaulted basalt hall stretched three hundred feet under ribs of pale green uranium-glass tubing made in the deep furnaces. Below our stone gallery, on three tiered timber scaffolds spanning the chasm, four hundred birds were treading the ash-wood rocker bars of the great bellows and cryogenic pumps in unison, their grey and russet feathers rippling under the green glass light while their four-part bass chorus rolled off the wet stone arches and the cold aquifer manifolds roared beneath the floor. Around the perimeter of the cavern curved the accelerator ring, two kilometres of beak-wound superconducting torus wrapped in silvered foil and bronze collars, converging on the central detector block and Cistern's slate table of ninety-nine gleaming sample cells and twenty-one brass witness-cradles. Because my eyelids were up and the violet star shone in my left pupil, not one of the four hundred birds looked toward us; every head was turned outward into the dark rock.

Cistern stood at my elbow with her grey throat-feathers raised, facing three-quarters away toward the quartz coincidence plate above the hundred-and-twentieth cradle. Her right foot pressed the brass beam-gate pedal and her beak struck three vernier timing keys. Through the basalt flags came the sharp thump of the capacitor banks dumping a pulse of calcium-50 ions around the ring into the californium-249 target drum. Two seconds later the scintillator crystal flashed a cold violet-white star that branched across the etched grid in three unmistakable alpha-decay tracks within forty microseconds, ringing the tuned silver witness-bells on cradle 120 with three high pings. I saw it with my right eye as the trusted one holding Cistern's last square in memory, while behind my left eye the installation captured the spectrum and sent the packet home.

Down in the chasm, the four-part singing stopped on a single beat. Four hundred birds stepped off the bellows bars, left their horn crimpers, glass blowpipes, and silver spools lying uncovered on the benches, and walked out in silence through the dark side-tunnels without once turning round. Beside me, Cistern took her foot off the pedal. She did not close the lead shutters over the curium cells or lift a single glass bulb from the ninety-nine alcoves. Once an outsider has taken a look, a cache cannot be saved by moving it. Her table was complete, and her table was spent, in the same forty microseconds. She stepped back from the slate bench, leaving forty-two winters of purified elements sitting under the green light like cracked pots in a mud yard.

In the upper corner of my sight, the green digits blurred as the installation ran the one-in-ten-to-the-forty-fifth observation against my prior, forty minus forty-five, and the warm iron band behind my left temple snapped inside out. A sudden tide of physical relief washed through my chest: the crisis was false, Task Force Seven was cancelled, the world was safe. Looking at the green figure burning in my vision as Cistern walked toward the stairs, I recognized the exact texture of that relief.

My number said −5.0000. I was exactly as sure as I had been, the other way, on the word of the same machine.

The same unaudited model that had watched two hundred birds scatter chaff under a probe and certified them as starving savages at forty had just graded its own homework at minus five, handing me a fresh certainty just as impervious to doubt as the old one.

At the top of the spiral stairs, in the dark archway before the daylight yard, Cistern stopped, and I shut my eyes without being told. Her scaly claws stepped behind my shoulder, her beak gave one light tap against my right shoulder-blade, and two warm forward toes pried my left fist open from behind to press a small, heavy, six-sided object into my palm before her rear toes folded my fingers tight around it.

"It's yours," Cistern said into the dark behind my ear. "Don't look."

When I opened my eyes a minute later, she was walking away across the mud square with her tailfeathers toward me. By that morning I had spent thirty-eight caches across the district by looking, including the yearling's copper wire, the cart, the elder's plumb-bob, Cistern's ring hall and table, and the transit under Seven-Holes' nest-wall; none of them were ever touched again, and Seven-Holes never forgave me.

Before I boarded the retrieval cutter on day 120, I sat in my dark tent with a wool scarf tied twice over my closed eyes, welded Cistern's gift inside a lead-lined steel specimen box by touch, and filed the latch seam flat so I could never accidentally flip it open. In my final report I wrote plainly that the issued prior of 40.0000 was defective and had forced the destruction of an advanced civilization's primary workshop before it could update, knowing that indicting the prior ended my promotion to the Actuarial Office. Twenty minutes later the Desk sent its final close-out note:

*Final telemetry logged at posterior −5.0000. Remediation Task Force Seven stood down. Narrative addendum separated from evidentiary record and forwarded to Administrative Review.*

Three months after they stripped the mission prior out of my head on Mercer's Ring and reassigned me to dock inspection, a clerk in the Actuarial Office named Harriet Baines invited me up to a glass-walled office three floors above the room where they keep the Hessary notebooks. She poured me real coffee and told me my report had made a difference.

"Because the issued prior staffs every mission in the Concern, your addendum triggered a service-wide review of every issued prior, Officer Lusk," Harriet Baines said brightly. "The committee is modeling a reduction from forty to thirty-six point five for agricultural worlds, and more importantly, we have closed the channel divergence you suffered. Starting next quarter, we are adding sub-dermal tactile sensors in all ten fingertips to the standard installation refit, so touch goes into the audit log from now on. No future officer will be teachable by hand behind her installation's back."

I declined the refit, took a permanent dock instructor's post that needed no prior, and kept the welded steel box on the shelf above my bunk. Even after the Office began logging touch, I have never opened it or had a look at what Cistern pressed into my palm at the top of the stairs; as long as I never see it, it isn't spent. Next to the box leans the two-thousand-piece jigsaw puzzle of the harbour at dusk, finished right down to the last copper-lit mast, with one rough grey square missing from the middle of the slate-blue water where Under-the-Steps made it safe for me. Sometimes in the evening I rest my thumb in that empty dent and wonder whether the people on Gennet ever needed my desalination grid, a small doubt that no machine ever gave me or took away, and the only thing in my head I know is mine.
