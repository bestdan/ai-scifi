# Outline: Two Half-Powers (working title)

- **Target length:** about 35,000 words, in five parts of about 7,000.
- **Shape:** linear, September to the first morning of November 2141,
  braided from three voices. Segundo's sections carry the story, in close
  third. Waska's sections (first person, present tense) carry the rope's
  reasoning and its theory of the town. Arriba's notes (first person,
  short, each ending in what it dispensed) are seen only at the dispensing
  slot. The braid is the form the idea needs: two minds that can't see
  each other, and the one man whose work touches both.
- **Formal device: section headers in each mind's unit.** Every section
  opens with one line. Segundo's is a receipt ("Received 1 ask from
  Rosaura Quishpe. Paid in: 1. Fee: 0.05."). Waska's is a load figure
  ("0.62 / 0.38"). Arriba's is a price ("The market. 1,990 asks."). Segundo's
  receipt lines stop at 5.2, where he takes asks with no receipt; his
  sections after that open with a blank line. Waska's last header is
  "0.00 / 1.00": slack. The reader feels the missing receipt before anyone
  mentions it.
- **Opening question:** what will a mind that can only answer do with six
  words from an honest man, and what is the rope worried about?
- **Final image:** the first morning of the slack season. The 06:40
  climber doesn't leave, and the windows don't rattle. Segundo at the
  counter at eight, opening January's first pool, thinking of a question
  about batch 8 that nobody can afford, when the clerk asks him what label
  to write. Waska, slack, feeling the town without carrying it.
- **What the ending costs the protagonist:** the clinic his wife wanted,
  now Proaño's, with the label in Segundo's own words; his fee and his
  receipts, because he asked the way his father asked; his wife's trust,
  strained in front of the parish; the town's roses halved for two years.

## What each part asks

| Part | The new question it raises |
|---|---|
| 1 | What does a button buy, and what is the rope afraid of? |
| 2 | Does it matter who asks, and what is a good name worth? |
| 3 | Why do people trust the machines more than each other? |
| 4 | What does the truth cost, and who can pay for it? |
| 5 | Can people govern themselves when nothing refuses them? |

No part repeats the label-craft as its engine. P1 shows the craft; P2
turns it into a market, and settles the sale of a good name in one scene
(2.4); P3 turns from the counter to the history; P4 is about price, not
wording; P5 is a meeting and a vote, and the label is the plainest one
Segundo ever writes.
