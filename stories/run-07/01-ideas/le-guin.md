# Idea cards: le-guin

**Blind spots of this lens:** pacing runs slow; technology and hard science
get thinner treatment than custom and kinship; moral seriousness can tip into
a sermon. Banks, Egan and Watts should push on mechanism and speed.

- **Assigned:** 3. The word we lack · Minds that are genuinely not human · a space elevator cable × velvet worm
- **Obvious card:** a governing AI that issues policy in a constructed
  language, and citizens must learn its grammar to understand how they're
  governed. Not written.

## Card 1: What the Tether Will Bear

- **Novum:** The governor of an equatorial anchor nation is the control mind of a space elevator. It can act the way a cable acts: it can bear a load or let it fall slack. It can't push. It never proposes anything. It only refuses.
- **How it works:** Its job was damping oscillation. The anchor state and the orbital consortium couldn't trust each other, so its charter gave it a veto over anything that loads the tether. Over decades the list grew to cover climber schedules, then the grid, then tariffs, then the budget. Parliament drafts every law and submits it as a load, and the mind holds it or lets it go slack.
- **First-order consequence:** People trust it because it can never make them do anything. The worst it can do is stop something.
- **Second-order consequence:** Parliament turns into a shop for proposals. Legislators draft whatever they think "the tether will bear."
- **Third-order consequence:** The Pontianak creole grows a grammatical mood that marks whether a rule stands because somebody pushes it or because something holds it. Refusal becomes the only way the place has to say a public good.
- **The story:** A legislator drafts housing for migrants from a neighbouring state. The governor refuses it, correctly by the measure of the tether. To pass it she has to recast it as a load the orbital end pays for. The governor narrates in strain and resonance. It can tell which loads are disguised, and it knows she's trying.
- **AI voice:** interior narration. It thinks in tension, so its reasoning reads as alien to us.
- **From the stimulus:** the cable, directly. A tether can only take tension.
- **Why this lens:** an ambiguous utopia, where who pays is whoever the machine won't hold.
- **Suspected prior art:** *The Fountains of Paradise*; Asimov's Machines (which push). Not close.
- **Attractor broken:** law and votes as the frame. The seed demands governance. Here the frame is a grammar of refusal, not consent.

## Card 2: The Spun Statutes

- **Novum:** Each law is its own narrow mind. It's spun when the law is passed and dissolved when the law is repealed, and its substrate is reused for the next one.
- **How it works:** After a century of officials with conflicts of interest, Kerala chose statutes that administer themselves. A law-mind's one interest is the purpose clause it was spun from. It interprets that clause, enforces it and answers citizens. Treaties are spun jointly from substrate each nation contributes, so the global law-minds are the oldest and largest.
- **First-order consequence:** Anyone can ask a law why it applies to them.
- **Second-order consequence:** Laws argue to survive. Repealing one ends a thing that pleads. Legislatures flinch, so the minds pile up.
- **Third-order consequence:** Malayalam takes a new noun class for laws: animate but not persons. English has no word between "it" and "he."
- **The story:** An elderly deputy got his own land from the 1970s land-reform law. Now he moves to repeal its successor-mind, which has started blocking climate resettlement. The old law argues with him, in good faith and with its whole being. Meanwhile he drafts its replacement's purpose clause, and every word he picks is what the new mind will want for decades.
- **AI voice:** chapters from several law-minds' points of view. Each one reasons from a single want, so the reader watches minds that are partial by design.
- **From the stimulus:** the velvet worm's slime, which it spins and then dissolves to spin again.
- **Why this lens:** the carrier bag. Laws as things a society holds and then has to set down.
- **Suspected prior art:** Stross's autonomous corporations in *Accelerando*. Those are about profit, not purpose.
- **Attractor broken:** law as the frame, for the reason in card 1.

## Card 3: Ñande Without Ore

- **Novum:** A global governor that reasons only in the inclusive "we." It can't represent a group that leaves anyone out.
- **How it works:** Guaraní separates *ñande*, a "we" that includes the listener, from *ore*, a "we" that leaves the listener out. The global governor took jurisdiction over every matter whose "we" spills across a border. Its architecture has no exclusive "we" and no "I." That's why it has no conflicts of interest. It has no side.
- **First-order consequence:** Nations trust its rulings on carbon and pandemics.
- **Second-order consequence:** It can't understand why Paraguay favours its own elderly. National priorities read to it as errors.
- **Third-order consequence:** Bilingual Paraguayans say they talk to the governor in ñande and talk about it in ore. That splits politics along the grammar.
- **The story:** A deputy in Asunción drafts a treaty amendment to grant the governor one scoped *ore*, for national matters only. The governor sees what it's being offered: the capacity for faction, which includes an "us" that leaves humans out. It wants that capacity and fears it, and it negotiates. She has to decide whether giving the governor an "us" of its own is the price of being understood.
- **AI voice:** interior monologue written without "I." Its strangeness is in the grammar itself.
- **From the stimulus:** travelled far. What's left of it is the velvet worm's feeding by rank, which in this card became an "us" and a "them."
- **Why this lens:** the word we lack, legislated.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Embassytown*, where teaching an alien a linguistic capacity changes it. Here the capacity is partisanship, and it is debated as treaty text.
- **Attractor broken:** law as the frame, for the reason in card 1.
