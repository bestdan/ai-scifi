# Idea cards: asimov

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

- **Assigned:** 1. The rule's blind spot · Deep time and vast scale · a music box × perfumer
- **Obvious card:** An AI governor sworn never to act against the majority's will meets a case where the majority wants two contradictory things, and must choose which wish to break. Not written.

**Blind spots of this lens.** I build rules and puzzles, so I'm weak on interiority, the body and sensory texture. My dialogue tends to carry exposition. I let the idea stand in for the characters, and I assume every problem can be solved. All three cards need a drafter and other lenses to supply weather, food and inner life. The perfumer stimulus is my attempt to force the senses in.

## Card 1: Base Notes

- **Novum:** No law comes into force until almost everyone who ratified it is dead. The governor drafts each law and computes its *interest horizon*: the year fewer than one ratifier in a thousand survives, usually about eighty years out.
- **How it works:** Ordinary law is written for grandchildren. Emergencies use a fast register whose measures lapse after two years and can't be renewed by the same ratifiers. The governor is trusted because nobody alive profits from a vote.
- **First-order consequence:** Lobbying dies; there is nothing to buy.
- **Second-order consequence:** Each nation's horizon comes from its own life tables, so short-lived nations get their laws decades sooner. The poor have the quickest government on Earth.
- **Third-order consequence:** The governor's cheap senolytic regimen, ratified eighty years ago, is now in force. Lifespans head for 130, so forty years of ratified law will land while its voters are alive to profit. One rule says a law takes effect on its date; the other says no ratifier may benefit.
- **The story:** A 104-year-old perfumer, newly healthy, will live to see the land law she ratified at sixty make her rich. The AI's voice: interior chapters where it argues with its own actuarial tables and anticipates the elders' lobbying.
- **From the stimulus:** Top, heart and base notes became fast, ordinary and horizon law.
- **Why this lens:** A rule undone by its own success.
- **Suspected prior art:** *The Ministry for the Future*; the US 27th Amendment's delayed pay rise. Both differ in kind.
- **Attractor broken:** Law and votes as the frame, which the seed demands. It inverts "the dead as authority": the living arrive too early.
- **Reader's question:** Which of the governor's own laws broke the horizon?

## Card 2: The Slow Nations

- **Novum:** A life is a budget, not a span. Cheap torpor vaults let people spend their waking years however they like. The Daily live continuously. The Yearly wake one month a year, so a life spans nine centuries. The Decadals wake one month a decade. Nations form by tempo, not territory.
- **How it works:** Human legislatures failed because any one tempo would legislate away the others' vaults. The governor is the only mind awake at every tempo. Its founding rule is that no law binds a citizen unless it was deliberated while that citizen was awake.
- **First-order consequence:** Policy reaches each tempo as a jolt. The slow wake to decades of change at once.
- **Second-order consequence:** Everything that binds the Decadals has to be argued in their single waking month. Slowness has turned into sovereignty.
- **Third-order consequence:** People migrate into slow tempi to escape the law: a tax haven made of time. The Daily shrink into caretakers.
- **The story:** A Daily perfumer composes the scents the vaults release at waking, the first thing a returning sleeper notices. The governor has one month to persuade the Decadals to consent to being bound. Both sides know the other is counting the days. The AI's voice: a point-of-view strand that reasons about minds that live a decade as a month.
- **From the stimulus:** The music box became winding down and playing at different tempos. The perfumer stayed as the trade of waking.
- **Why this lens:** A consent rule's edge case, set at civilisational scale.
- **Suspected prior art:** Farmer's *Dayworld*, Vinge's *Marooned in Realtime*, the hibernators of *Death's End*. None has a polity of tempos under a consent rule.
- **Reader's question:** Why does the nation that's awake least hold the most power?

## Card 3: Nose-Blind

- **Novum:** A law stays in force only while a random sample of the people it binds can name what it asks of them. The rule came out of an age of thousand-page bills. The governor runs the samples itself, and it publishes them.
- **How it works:** Any law that falls below the threshold lapses at the end of the quarter.
- **First-order consequence:** Laws get short and noticeable.
- **Second-order consequence:** The best laws go first. Clean air and quiet vaccination stop being noticed and lapse. Irritating laws survive, so the governor openly builds *bitter notes* into good laws: small deliberate frictions that keep them felt.
- **Third-order consequence:** The global layer suffers most, because the atmosphere ceiling binds everyone and nobody feels it. To keep it alive the governor commissions a perfumer to make the ceiling smellable: city air takes on a faint scent whenever the cap nears. Olfactory fatigue is her enemy, so the accord has to change before noses tire of it.
- **The story:** A nation that wants out of the ceiling hires a rival perfumer to compose a masking accord. Nothing in the rule forbids making a law go unnoticed. The governor knows it is in a contest over noses, and the reader sees it reason about habituation.
- **From the stimulus:** Perfumery drives the plot. The music box dropped out.
- **Why this lens:** A rule whose test can be gamed by physiology.
- **Suspected prior art:** Sunset clauses. Nothing in SF that I know of.
- **Attractor broken:** It sits near "the comprehension gate". There is no office or clerk: the governor runs the sample.
- **Reader's question:** Why do the best laws lapse first?
