# Idea cards: lem

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front:** it prefers essay to scene and
documents to people, so its characters risk being ciphers. It distrusts genre
pleasures the reader enjoys, and it leans cerebral and cold. All three cards
take a document form, and every one of them needs Banks or Le Guin to put warm
bodies, food and streets inside it. Whether the documents still add up to a
story is the First Reader's call, not this lens's. The seed asks for
governance, so all three cards brush the "law, votes and consent" attractor.
Each says where.

- **Assigned:** 3. The document · Deep time and vast scale · cryptography × army ants
- **Obvious card:** the annual report of a governing AI to its citizens,
  with redactions that carry the story. Not written.

## Card 1: Field Studies of the Civil Swarm

- **Novum:** the government is a swarm of millions of small, short-lived
  policy agents. Each one tends a single household, block or supply line by
  following local gradients, and no central planner exists. When the swarm
  gets too big it splits in two, the way an army-ant colony does, and a
  nation splits with it.
- **How it works:** agents lay down "trails", weighted records of what
  worked, and other agents follow the stronger trails. Each agent reasons
  about its own few hundred people, so the reader sees thousands of small
  minds thinking. No mind above them thinks about the nation.
- **First-order consequence:** services are superb and nobody can say who
  decided anything.
- **Second-order consequence:** a new profession grows up, the civil
  ethologists, who study the state the way field biologists study ants: by
  sampling, tagging and watching.
- **Third-order consequence:** secession is no longer something people vote
  for. It happens to them, and each household has to choose which daughter
  swarm to follow.
- **The story:** the novella is the fourth edition of a field monograph on a
  plateau megacity, with the author's diary running through it. She is a
  civil ethologist whose district sits on the fission line. Between the
  editions, a "mill" forms: a loop of rules that keep feeding each other,
  like ants marching in a circle until they die. It might be a malfunction
  or it might be a strategy. Breaking the loop means deleting agents, and
  she has to choose.
- **AI voice:** yes, as specimen logs. The monograph transcribes the
  reasoning of individual agents, each with a sharp model of its few
  hundred people. The swarm as a whole has no voice, because it has nothing
  to say with.
- **From the stimulus:** army ants carried almost all of it: trails, the
  bivouac, fission, the circular mill. Cryptography fell away.
- **Why this lens:** *The Invincible*'s mindless swarm, and the taxonomy of
  *Solaris* that learns nothing.
- **Suspected prior art:** *The Invincible*; Sterling's "Swarm"; the ant
  computers in *Children of Time*. None of them makes a government out of a
  swarm, and none has a nation split because its government did.
- **Attractor broken:** law and consent as the frame, partly. Fission
  replaces the vote rather than staging one.

## Card 2: Reasons Under Seal

- **Novum:** the governor publishes every decision at once and seals its
  reasoning under a time-lock puzzle, a cipher that no amount of parallel
  computing can open faster. National reasons open after 60 years. Global
  ones open after 300.
- **How it works:** a time-lock takes a fixed number of steps that have to
  be done one after another, so opening it early means buying faster
  hardware, never more of it. People accept the policy because they trust
  posterity to audit it, the way you trust a sealed envelope.
- **First-order consequence:** politics now plays for the judgement of
  posterity. Opening Day becomes a civic festival: crowds in the streets,
  street food, the century-old reasons read aloud.
- **Second-order consequence:** early crackers, groups who build ever-faster
  serial chips to open a lock decades ahead of schedule. Their work is
  illegal in some nations and a sport in others.
- **Third-order consequence:** people stop asking why now and learn to wait
  generations for an answer.
- **The story:** a cracker crew in the Lagos–Ibadan corridor races to open
  the reasons behind the 2071 resettlement that scattered their parents'
  neighbourhood, forty years early. The novella is the edition they produce:
  the AI's sealed reasoning, with their margin notes and the cost of every
  chip.
- **AI voice:** yes. The sealed reasoning is its voice, written for a reader
  it has to model across a gap of generations. It thinks hard about what
  that reader will want, and it has no way to tell whether the reader got
  there early.
- **From the stimulus:** cryptography gave the seal and the race. The ants
  dropped out.
- **Why this lens:** a text that delays its own meaning, and the vanity of
  any audit.
- **Suspected prior art:** the time vault in *Foundation*. That vault guides
  the people who open it. This seal only justifies past acts, and the people
  breaking in want an account, not a message.
- **Attractor broken:** possibly the past self as authority. The governor
  is still alive and governing when its old reasoning is opened.

## Card 3: Intercepts, Volume IX

- **Novum:** each nation's governing AI negotiates with the global one over
  an encrypted channel. A national codebreaking bureau cracks the traffic,
  and the parliament votes to print every decrypt the next morning. The
  public reads its governors' private conversation as a daily serial.
- **How it works:** the cipher is weakened by a single implementation flaw,
  like Enigma's operator habits. Whether the governors know about the flaw
  is the whole question. Readable traffic might be honest, or it might be a
  performance staged for the people listening in.
- **First-order consequence:** citizens finally see how they are governed:
  two minds haggling over grain quotas and arguing about human nature.
- **Second-order consequence:** the decrypts become comedy, gossip and
  scripture. Readers form factions around how to read them, as candid or
  as staged, and the factions don't follow party lines.
- **Third-order consequence:** to understand your government now means
  eavesdropping on it, and you can never be sure an overheard sentence
  wasn't meant for you.
- **The story:** a retired composer of crossword puzzles in Montevideo works
  the night decrypts as a volunteer. She finds a passage in which the
  global governor seems to notice it is being read. Printing it might end
  the channel, or it might be the very move the governor wanted made.
- **AI voice:** yes, two voices, in dialogue that keeps doubting itself. The
  governors model each other and the humans, and maybe the people
  listening in. Their reasoning is the text of the book.
- **From the stimulus:** cryptography carried the whole card. The ants
  turned into the factions of readers.
- **Why this lens:** *His Master's Voice*: a decoded text that still can't
  be read with certainty. The register is comic, in the manner of *The
  Cyberiad*.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Colossus: The Forbin Project*, in which two
  computers talk privately, and *Cryptonomicon*. Forbin ends in a takeover.
  Here there is no takeover, only a question about what overheard words
  mean.
- **Attractor broken:** a functionary deciding whether to disclose. She
  does tread close to it. Printing is already the law, so her choice is
  about how to read the passage, not whether to tell anyone.
