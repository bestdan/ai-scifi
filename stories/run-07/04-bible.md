# Story bible: Two Half-Powers (working title)

## Premise

E, Two Half-Powers, as amended by the Decision in `03-synthesis.md`.

Ecuador, 2141. The country is governed by Waska, the control mind of the
world's first space elevator, whose anchor stands on the equator on the
slopes of Cayambe. Waska can only refuse: every law the Asamblea passes
reaches it as a load, and it bears it or lets it fall slack. It never
proposes anything. The mind the elevator's consortium answers to, which
everyone calls Arriba because it lives at the top of the cable, is the
opposite. It is past any human's understanding, and it may act on people
only when asked. Every adult on Earth holds ten asks a year, pooled by
brokers at public counters behind plain-language buttons, and Arriba
decides what each button dispenses. Nothing happens in Ecuador unless a
citizen asks Arriba and Waska agrees to bear what comes down.

Segundo Farinango is a broker at the Ayora counter in Cayambe, and the
honest one: he keeps receipts. He pools his clients' asks behind a button
labelled "the market", for the flower auction and night market his ward
lives off. Arriba dispenses, among the expected goods, 2,000 metres of
grey cord. Waska, weighing the coming replacement of its own lower
ribbon, feels a ring in the new batch's test sections that sits inside
tolerance and that it can't explain. Arriba knows why: a flaw in the
batch it made. It may not say so unasked, and nobody asks, because
everyone trusts that the rope would refuse anything unsound. The
constitution split knowing into two halves on purpose, and a plain
question that joins them costs what answering it takes. Segundo has to
find the question, and then ask his ward for the asks they saved for
their clinic, on his word, with no receipt.

## What the reader needs about the world

1. **Every adult has ten asks a year, and nothing from Arriba reaches
   anyone unless somebody asks.** People pool their asks through brokers,
   who write the button labels and take a fee, one ask in twenty. In
   Ayora most households hold six of their ten for the parish's December
   pool, the year's big button.
2. **Every law the Asamblea passes goes to Waska as a load.** Waska bears
   it or refuses it. It never proposes, amends or explains, and it can't
   make anyone do anything.
3. **The cable is the town's living.** Cayambe's roses go up it to the
   orbital stations; machines, medicine and orbital goods come down. The
   anchor employs one household in four.
4. **Arriba may not warn or advise anyone who hasn't asked, and it never
   lies.** It answers exactly what it is asked. Children learn the rule
   first, from the bread story.
5. **Waska's refusals come as numbers, not reasons.** A load figure and a
   margin, published on the counter's board. Engineers at the anchor, the
   strain readers, translate them for everyone else.

## The novum

- **What it is:** a constitution of two crippled powers. The national mind
  can only refuse; the global mind can only answer. Policy exists where an
  ordinary person's ask meets the national mind's consent.
- **How it works:**
  - **Asks.** Each adult (16 and over) gets ten asks on 1 January; a person
    turning 16 during the year gets the months' share on their birthday
    (two asks for a late-October birthday). An ask is a claim on Arriba's
    dividend, the surplus of the orbital economy it runs: beamed power,
    orbital manufacture, launch. Asks belong to people. A state, party or
    firm can't hold one, though a firm may ask its workers to pool behind
    its label. Unspent asks lapse on 31 December.
  - **Brokers.** Pooling is by contract, one ask at a time, through a
    licensed broker, who writes the label and takes a posted fee.
  - **Buttons.** A pooled ask goes in at a public counter behind a label a
    human writes, in plain language, at most six words. Labels and their
    pool counts are posted on the counter's board, for anyone to read.
    Arriba decides what the button dispenses: goods, a building, an
    inspection, money. It may dispense only what plausibly answers the
    label. It prices the answer in asks and fills it when enough have
    pooled.
  - **Who asks matters.** Arriba reads a label in the light of the
    broker's history and the pool's members. The same six words from two
    brokers can dispense different things.
  - **Questions.** A label may be a question. Arriba answers by doing what
    answering takes, and prices the doing. It answers literally and never
    lies. A narrow question gets a narrow, true answer. "What aren't you
    telling us?" is priced at the work of examining everything it knows:
    about 9 billion asks, the world's year.
  - **Answers are public.** An answer is posted beside its label.
  - **Loads.** Everything Arriba dispenses to Ecuador comes down the cable,
    so it is a load on the tether, and Waska may refuse it. Every law of
    the Asamblea is also submitted as a load: the bill, its costing and its
    schedule. Beamed power reaches ground receivers directly and is not a
    load.
  - **Load figures.** Waska's verdicts are published as two numbers: the
    load as a fraction of what it will bear, and the margin left. A
    refusal reads, for instance, "1.04 / −0.04". The strain readers turn
    them into Spanish.
  - **The audit.** The Accord's human auditors review a sample of
    dispensations against their labels. A dispensation that strays beyond
    "plausibly answers" is a breach. Three breaches in a year let the
    consortium's states cut Arriba's dividend share, which is the share
    that funds every ask.
- **What it costs:**
  - Nothing happens unless someone asks, so the poor ask small and the
    organised ask large. Brokers carry the old conflicts of interest at the
    human layer: they skim, sell labels and pool for whoever pays.
  - Nobody can be told what nobody asked about. The mind that sees furthest
    may not say what it sees.
  - Everyone trusts the rope to refuse anything unsound, and so nobody asks
    about what the rope can't feel.
  - The Asamblea has forgotten how to say yes on its own. It drafts what
    "the rope will bear", and every law is shaped to pass a refusal, not to
    do good.
- **Its rules and their edge cases:**

## The world's shape

- **Where power sits, and what it covers:** Waska governs Ecuador because
  Ecuador is the anchor state, and its veto covers anything that loads the
  tether, which by 2141 means every law. Two later tethers (Gabon, 2091;
  Kalimantan, 2103) have their own minds with narrower lists, because
  their states never had a Year of Two Budgets. Arriba answers the asks of
  every adult on Earth, but it governs nowhere: it only fills what people
  pay for. The rest of the world's nations keep human governments and meet
  Arriba only at the counter.
- **How it got that way:**
  - **2059–2068.** The consortium (eleven states and four firms) builds the
    first tether. Ecuador gives the anchor site on the equator at Cayambe
    for a share of the cargo, and insists on a veto over loads. The
    consortium accepts, because a mind that can only refuse can't take the
    anchor away either.
  - **2079.** The veto list grows to climber schedules.
  - **2088.** To the national grid, after a grid surge made the anchor reel
    hunt for three hours.
  - **2097, the Year of Two Budgets.** Two coalitions each pass a budget,
    each written by the cargo firms their leaders own. The Treasury pays
    both for four months. Climber slots are sold twice. Waska refuses the
    double cargo, the only institution that says no to anything that year.
  - **2098.** Arriba's unasked Sahel warning. Bread triples in Cayambe.
  - **2101.** Ecuador votes by referendum to submit every law to Waska.
  - **2104.** The Quito Accord binds Arriba to answer only, sets ten asks
    per adult, and licenses brokers.
  - **2110.** Ernesto Farinango, Segundo's father and one of the first
    brokers, takes a payment from the Proaño rose company and pools his
    parish's December asks behind its label, "the farm road". The road is
    built; the parish had wanted a water tank. In 2112 fees must be posted.
- **Where the reader meets that history:** the anchor tour (P1: 2068, the
  veto's reason, the children's bread story); Mama Dolores's stall (P3:
  2097 and 2098, lived, with the reader in the stall); Ernesto in his care
  home (P3: 2110, unrepentant).
- **Why people handed power over:** lived, not stated. Dolores cleaned
  the Treasury in Quito in 2097 and was paid by both budgets for four
  months; she still keeps both payslips. In P3 she shows them to Segundo
  and tells him which one she cashed, and why. The town's distrust of
  human government is in small things everywhere: the Asambleísta who
  owns the roses, the broker who skims, the way people check a load figure
  before they believe a minister.

## The consequence ladder

1. **First order:** nothing happens unless someone asks; brokers become
   the politics of the age. The Asamblea drafts only what the rope will
   bear.
2. **Second order:** the poor ask small and the organised ask large; a
   broker's history becomes a tradeable asset, because Arriba reads labels
   by who asks. People trust the two machines more than each other,
   because the machines can't push.
3. **Third order:** everyone trusts the refuser, so no one asks about what
   it can't feel. The constitution splits knowing in two, and the only way
   to join the halves is a plain question priced at the work. Arriba's
   silence turns out to be a price, not a wall, and the rich can afford to
   ask about everything.

## The society

- **Institutions:** the Asamblea (137 members, most of them owners of
  something that rides the cable); the counters (one per parish, with a
  clerk, a board and the brokers' benches outside); the anchor (Waska's
  housing, the reel, the climber yards, the strain readers' office and
  lab); the Accord's auditors; the parish council of Ayora, which runs the
  December pool.
- **Economy:** scarce: climber slots, asks in December, honest brokers,
  flat land at 2,800 metres. Worthless: rhetoric, manifestos (nobody reads
  a bill that hasn't been weighed), and Ecuador's own currency, since what
  matters is priced in asks.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:** extended Kayambi and mestizo
  families; grandmothers keep the market stalls and the children. The
  minga, the communal work day, is how the parish builds anything: every
  household owes labour, and the December pool grew out of it as a minga of
  asks. Rose workers cut from 05:00 to 13:00, so afternoons are family
  time.
- **Death:** buried in the parish cemetery; on 2 November families take
  maize drink and bread dolls to the graves. A dying person's last asks
  are often pooled for the family, a recognised kindness.
- **Taboos:** asking for yourself what your ward needs; reading someone
  else's ask history aloud; skipping the minga.
- **An ordinary day:** roses cut before dawn under plastic; the 06:40
  climber leaves the anchor and every window in Ayora rattles once; the
  counter opens at eight; the board updates at noon; the night market in
  the plaza from seven, lit by lamps the parish asked for in 2133; the
  June sun festival's masks hanging in kitchens all year.

## Minds

- **Waska.** It perceives the world as tension, load, ring and slack
  along 36,000 km of ribbon and in the anchor reel. Its memory is the
  ribbon's: every load it has carried is still in it as creep and fatigue,
  so it remembers by feeling its own wear, and it speaks in the present
  tense because the past is present in the cable. Its want is slack:
  everything it does tends toward carrying less. It knows each person as a
  load with a direction. After forty years of the Asamblea drafting
  disguised loads, it assumes every human is disguising one, and it is
  usually right. It finds obvious: that anything can be borne if spread
  over time, and that nothing should be borne that rings. It can't think
  about what a load is *for*, only what it does to the cable; it has no
  theory of good, only of strain. Its only voice to people is its
  published figures. It narrates its own sections in the first person.
  Its theory of Segundo: a small load that keeps arriving at odd angles,
  which it reads as a disguise and so weighs at full margin, every time.
  The theory is wrong, and it costs Segundo: in P2 the market's cold
  store sits at the anchor for two weeks while Waska weighs it. Waska
  never learns it was wrong.
- **Arriba.** It perceives the whole world's asks, every day, and the
  orbital economy it runs. It is past the horizon: its sections show only
  the part of its reasoning at the dispensing slot. It finds obvious what
  people mean behind what they label, and who they are when they ask. It
  keeps the Accord as a rule, not a penance; its sections show no remorse
  and no longing, only the rule, the label, the price and the audit
  margin. Its sections are short, first person, and each ends with what it
  dispensed. Its theory of Segundo: the son of the broker who pooled his
  parish for Proaño, buying for the flower auction, which Proaño owns. It
  is wrong about him. Whether the cord was meant as a warning is never
  settled: its note gives the reason it sent it (disposal) and states one
  fact (the anchor town reads ribbon), and says nothing more.
- **The two together.** Each knows half of batch 7's truth. Waska feels a
  ring in the batch 7 test sections on the anchor's test line, inside
  tolerance. Arriba knows the batch was cured during the solar storm of
  11–14 March 2138, that the sections nearest the oven's north wall carry
  voids that open under cyclic load, and, since Gabon's test line broke a
  sister section in 2140, roughly what that means. Neither can tell the
  other. Each has spent its life shaped around the other's half-power:
  Arriba has learned not to dispense what Waska will refuse, and Waska
  has learned to read the ask behind a load.

## Characters

### Segundo Farinango

- **Name:** Farinango is a Kayambi surname of the Cayambe valley; Segundo,
  a common rural Ecuadorian name for a second son.
- **Age and work:** 52, licensed broker at the Ayora counter for 27 years.
  Pools for about 2,100 of the parish's 7,800 adults.
- **Household:** his wife Lourdes, the parish dispensary's nurse; his
  daughter Sisa, who turns 16 on 26 October and gets her first two asks
  that day.
- **What he wants:** to be trusted as honest in a trade everyone believes
  skims. He keeps receipts for every ask, posts his fee on the bench, and
  has never pooled behind a firm's label.
- **What he's wrong about:** that trust is the same as receipts. He has
  never asked anyone to trust him on his word alone, because his father
  did.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** makes his craft, writing
  six words a mind will read generously, the one way Ayora gets anything.
  His history makes Arriba read him as Proaño's man, which is why the cord
  comes to his button and not Wilmer's.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** the parish fiesta's brass band,
  in which he plays the tuba badly every year; and the 06:40 climber, which
  he still watches leave from his roof.
- **Competence:** label-writing. He knows Arriba fills "the school" with a
  building and "our children's mornings" with teachers; he reads a button
  history like a chess player. Shown in P1 before any machine speaks.
- **The wound:** in 2110, aged 21, he watched his father take the parish's
  December asks on his word at the fiesta and pool them behind Proaño's
  label. The road got built; the water tank didn't. His father never
  apologised.
- **The scene where he changes his mind about something personal:** P5,
  when he stands on the bench and asks the ward for their December asks
  with no receipt and no reason, the way his father did. He doesn't
  resolve it there. He half-notices, and the reader sees more clearly,
  that part of what drives him is wanting to be the one who was right, and
  wanting his father to have been wrong in a way he isn't. He goes on
  asking anyway.

### Lourdes Imbaquingo

- **Name:** Imbaquingo is a Kichwa surname of the northern highlands.
- **Age and work:** 49, nurse at the parish dispensary, two rooms behind
  the counter. The December pool was to buy the clinic she has asked for
  since 2134.
- **What she wants:** the clinic; and for Segundo to tell her what he
  knows.
- **Role:** the cost, with a face. She gives her asks in P5, and she
  makes him pay for not telling her why.

### Sisa Farinango

- **Name:** Sisa is Kichwa for flower.
- **Age:** 16 on 26 October. Works afternoons at the auction, tying
  bunches.
- **What she can't do or doesn't understand yet:** she thinks her father's
  honesty is embarrassing and her grandfather's road was heroic. She
  can't yet hold both.
- **Role:** her first two asks, given on the night of the ward meeting;
  she ties the cord on the auction awnings in P2, which is where the
  reader first sees it.

### Mama Dolores Farinango

- **Age and work:** 78, Segundo's mother, sells boiled lupin beans with
  toasted maize from a stall in the night market. Cleaned the Treasury in
  Quito in 2097.
- **What she wants:** for Segundo to stop being his father's opposite and
  be his own man.
- **What she's wrong about:** that the machines are kinder than people.
  She trusts Waska completely and Arriba not at all, because of the bread.
- **Role:** the lived history (P3). In P5 she refuses: she won't give an
  ask to a question for Arriba, and says so in front of the ward.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** pasillo records, the slow
  guitar songs of her youth, which she plays at the stall.

### Ernesto Farinango

- **Age:** 81, in the parish care home, sharp and unrepentant.
- **What he wants:** to be visited, and to be thanked for the road.
- **What he's wrong about:** that the road settled the account.
- **Role:** the human conflict of interest, lived and funny. A coalition
  fixer in 2097, a broker from 2104. He explains, reasonably, why a man
  takes the only buyer's money when the parish's asks would otherwise buy
  half a tank. He knew nothing hidden and isn't secretly good.

### Jacinta Ulcuango

- **Name:** Ulcuango is a Kayambi surname.
- **Age and work:** 44, senior strain reader at the anchor, Segundo's
  cousin.
- **What she wants:** the replacement on schedule; she has trained for it
  for nine years.
- **What she's wrong about:** that Waska's figures are all of Waska, and
  that her lab can see what the rope can't.
- **Role:** the human who reads Waska. She knows ribbon by touch, and
  knows the cord is replacement-generation stock (batches 5 to 8) by its
  weave pitch, though an offcut carries no batch mark. Her lab's fatigue
  test comes back inside tolerance (P3), the same tolerance Waska rings
  in. In P4 she puts Waska's test-line figures beside the cord.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** her climbing gym in Cayambe,
  and her twin boys, nine, who think the climbers are rockets.

### Gustavo Proaño

- **Name:** Proaño, a long-established Quito-highland surname.
- **Age and work:** 61, Asambleísta for Pichincha and owner of the Proaño
  rose farms, 3,000 workers and a third of the cable's upward cargo.
- **What he wants:** the replacement on schedule, because his freight
  contracts run through it, and his workers paid. Both are real goods.
- **What he's wrong about:** that he has no conflict of interest because
  every law he votes for is weighed by Waska.
- **Role:** the human politics. In P2 he buys Wilmer's labels. In P4 he
  is the first to say what the question means: "We pay the maker to
  check the maker's work, and now the maker's customers will pay too."
  He could ask about batch 7 himself, and won't: a posted question about
  his own freight would sink his contracts whatever the answer. In P5 his
  workers pool their December asks behind his label, "Ayora's clinic",
  and he makes sure everyone knows.

### Wilmer Cuzco

- **Name:** Cuzco is a highland Ecuadorian surname.
- **Age and work:** 29, a broker on the next bench, 1,600 clients, fast and
  charming, posts the legal fee and takes favours on top.
- **Role:** the label market (P2). He copies "the market" exactly and gets
  lamps and awning frames, and no cord. With Jacinta's help, Segundo reads
  that as proof that Arriba reads the asker. Wilmer reads it as proof that
  a good history is worth money, and starts renting Segundo's. In P5 his
  clients' asks are the margin.

## Point of view and voice

- **Teller:** close third on Segundo for the human sections; Waska in the
  first person for its own; Arriba in short first-person notes.
- **Person and tense:** Segundo's sections, past tense. Waska's, present
  tense: its past is present as wear. Arriba's, present tense, at most
  about 400 words each, each ending with what it dispensed.
- **Register:** Segundo's: warm, dry, specific, full of the town's
  objects. Waska's: plain words used strangely, drawn from tension:
  "pull", "slack", "bear", "ring", "carry", "wear". Arriba's: courteous,
  exact, and plainly holding back what it isn't asked for.
- **Why this teller:** the broker is the only person whose daily work
  touches both minds. Waska's interior carries the reasoning and the
  theory of mind the brief requires; Arriba's short notes keep its
  horizon intact.
- **A sample paragraph in the voice (Waska):**

  > The six-forty pulls at me the way every morning pulls. Ninety tonnes,
  > roses and the post, climbing through the cloud at a steady eighty. I
  > carry it, and while I carry it I read the town. The town pulls too,
  > in smaller ways. The broker on the second bench is pulling again
  > today, a little sideways, as he always does. When a person pulls
  > sideways, they are hiding the true direction of the load. The man on
  > the second bench has pulled sideways for twenty-seven years, and I
  > have never found what he is hiding, so I weigh everything he sends
  > me at the full margin, and I always will.

## The numbers (traced)

- **Asks.** 10 per adult per year. Ayora parish has 7,800 adults: 78,000
  asks a year. By custom most households hold six for the December pool.
  In late October the parish holds about 47,000 unspent (6.0 per adult),
  earmarked for the clinic button, priced at 44,000.
- **Brokers.** Segundo pools for 2,100 adults, Wilmer for 1,600, three
  others for about 3,000; about 1,100 pool with no broker, through the
  parish council.
- **Fees.** One ask in twenty (5%).
- **The market button (September).** 2,100 of Segundo's clients give one
  ask each: 2,100 asks, less his fee of 105, so 1,995 go in. Arriba
  dispenses awning frames, lamps, a cold store and 2,000 m of cord, priced
  at 1,990.
- **The flaw.** Batch 7: 312 sections, cured 11–14 March 2138. The 35
  sections nearest the oven's north wall carry voids; the rest are sound.
  Failure of a flawed section is expected within 2 to 3 years of service.
- **The cheap question (P4).** "Is section forty sound?" costs 131 asks,
  the inspection of one section. Answer: "Section forty of batch seven is
  sound." True; section 40 sat in the oven's centre.
- **The catch-all (P4).** "What aren't you telling us?" is priced at about
  9 billion asks. Segundo laughs.
- **The question (P5).** Inspecting all 312 sections at 131 asks each:
  40,872 asks.
- **The ward's pool.** The ward meeting brings in 42,300 of the parish's
  ~47,000: Segundo's clients, most of Wilmer's, most of the others, and the
  council's. Named refusals: Dolores, the Pilataxi household (Proaño's
  foreman's), and about 600 others who hold back. With Segundo's fee of
  2,115, 40,185 would go in, 687 short. Waived, 42,300 leaves 1,428 over.
  The waiver is what makes the question affordable.
- **Sisa's asks.** 2, on 26 October.
- **Replacement.** The first ribbon was made in 2066, so its 75-year
  design life ends in 2141. Only the lower 40 km is replaced now. The
  slack season runs 1 November to 10 December 2141 (40 days).
- **The outcome.** Without batch 7's 35 flawed sections, the lower 40 km
  is rebuilt from batches 5, 6 and 8 and the sound sections of 7, with the
  bottom 9 km left on the old ribbon, derated. Climbers run at half load
  for two years, until new stock is made: half the roses go up.
- **The cable.** About 36,000 km to the station at geostationary height,
  plus the counterweight beyond. The anchor is at about 2,950 metres.

## Ending direction and cost

**What happens.** Arriba's dispensing for "the market" includes 2,000
metres of grey cord: offcut of batch 7, cut into awning lengths. Arriba's
note gives its reason. Batch 7 offcut can't go into any tether now, and
awning cord plausibly answers a market's needs, so dispensing it is lawful
disposal, and the auction where it will hang is the one whose owner's
freight rides the batch. The note also says that the anchor town reads
ribbon. It says nothing else. Sisa ties the cord on the auction awnings in
P2, where the reader, who has heard Waska's ring in P1, can see what it is
before anyone in the story does. Wilmer's identical label buys no cord.

Jacinta knows the cord is replacement-generation ribbon by its weave, but
an offcut carries no batch mark, and her lab's fatigue test comes back
inside tolerance (P3). In P4 she and Segundo put the cord beside Waska's
test-line figures: batch 7's sections ring 0.04 more than the others. It
is still inside tolerance, but it is the only thing that singles out a
batch. The batch records sit inside Arriba and open only to an ask.
Segundo tries the cheap question about section 40 and gets a true,
useless answer. He prices the catch-all and laughs. That leaves the plain
question about the whole batch, at 40,872 asks: the clinic.

He can't tell the ward why. He and Jacinta suspect, but they don't know,
and a broker who says "the cable is flawed" without an answer sinks the
town's contracts and his name. At the ward meeting on 27 October he asks
for their December asks on his word. Lourdes gives hers. Sisa gives her
two. Dolores refuses aloud. The tally is counted, with neighbours watching
who gives. Wilmer brings his clients over. He waives his fee. The button,
posted on 28 October, reads "Is batch seven sound?" The answer, posted on
30 October: 35 sections are not, with their numbers. On 31 October the
consortium resubmits the climb with the defect map, which derates the
batch below the climb's load, and Waska refuses: "1.04 / −0.04". The
slack season goes ahead on 1 November on the rebuilt plan, and for 40
days Waska bears nothing, so the Asamblea passes laws that nothing weighs,
provisionally.

**What it costs.** The clinic: Proaño's workers pool for it behind his
label, and the whole town knows whose clinic it is now. Segundo's fee, and
his receipts: he asked the way his father asked. Lourdes's trust,
strained. Half the roses for two years. And the world learns that
Arriba's silence was never a wall but a price, and the rich can afford to
ask about everything.

**Final image.** The first morning of the slack season. The 06:40 climber
doesn't leave, and the windows don't rattle. Segundo is at the counter at
eight, opening the first pool for January, and the clerk asks him what
label to write. Arriba's last note is short: what it was asked and what it
dispensed. Waska's last section is slack. It bears nothing. It feels the
town the way it always has, and for forty days it doesn't have to carry
it.
