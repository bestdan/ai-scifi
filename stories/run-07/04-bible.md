# Story bible: Two Half-Powers (working title)

## Premise

E, Two Half-Powers, as amended by the Decision in `03-synthesis.md`.

Ecuador, 2141. The country is governed by Waska, the control mind of the
world's first space elevator, whose anchor stands on the equator on the
slopes of Cayambe. Waska can only refuse: every law the Asamblea passes
reaches it as a load, and it bears it or lets it fall slack. It never
proposes anything. The mind the elevator's consortium answers to, which
everyone calls Arriba because it lives at the top of the cable, is the
opposite. It is past any human's understanding, and it may act on people
only when asked. Every adult on Earth holds ten asks a year, pooled by
brokers at public counters behind plain-language buttons, and Arriba
decides what each button dispenses. Nothing happens in Ecuador unless a
citizen asks Arriba and Waska agrees to bear what comes down.

Segundo Farinango is a broker at the Ayora counter in Cayambe, and the
honest one: he keeps receipts. He pools his parish's asks behind a button
labelled "the market", for the flower auction and night market his ward
lives off. Arriba dispenses something odd. Waska, weighing the replacement
of its own lower ribbon, feels a resonance in the new ribbon's test
sections that it can't explain. Arriba knows why: a flaw in the batch it
made. It is forbidden to say so unasked. Neither mind holds the whole
truth, by design, and no human reads both halves. Segundo has to work out
what question to ask, and then ask his neighbours to give him a year of
their asks on his word, with no receipt.

## What the reader needs about the world

1. **Every adult has ten asks a year, and nothing from Arriba reaches
   anyone unless somebody asks.** People pool their asks through brokers,
   who write the button labels and take a fee, usually one ask in twenty.
2. **Every law the Asamblea passes goes to Waska as a load.** Waska bears it
   or refuses it. It never proposes, amends or explains, and it can't make
   anyone do anything.
3. **The cable is the town's living.** Cayambe's roses go up it to the
   orbital stations; power, medicine and machines come down. The anchor
   employs one household in four.
4. **Arriba may not warn or advise anyone who hasn't asked.** It is the
   most-known rule in the world and the one children learn first.
5. **Waska's refusals come as numbers, not reasons.** A strain figure and a
   margin, published at the counter. Engineers at the anchor, the strain
   readers, translate them for everyone else.

## The novum

- **What it is:** a constitution of two crippled powers. The national mind
  can only refuse; the global mind can only answer. Policy exists where an
  ordinary person's ask meets the national mind's consent.
- **How it works:**
  - **Asks.** Each adult (16 and over) gets ten asks on 1 January. An ask is
    a claim on Arriba's dividend: the surplus of the orbital economy it runs
    (beamed power, orbital manufacture, launch). Asks are personal and can't
    be held by a state, a party or a firm. They can be pooled by contract,
    one ask at a time, through a licensed broker. Unspent asks lapse on 31
    December.
  - **Buttons.** A pooled ask goes in at a public counter behind a button
    whose label a human writes, in plain language, at most six words.
    Arriba decides what the button dispenses: goods, power, a building, an
    inspection, money. It may dispense anything that plausibly answers the
    label. It prices the answer in asks and fills it only when enough have
    pooled.
  - **Questions.** A button may be a question. Arriba answers a question
    by doing what answering it takes, and prices the doing: a question
    about a field's soil costs a soil survey.
  - **Loads.** In Ecuador, everything Arriba dispenses comes down the cable
    or through the anchor's grid, so it is a load on the tether, and Waska
    may refuse it. Every law of the Asamblea is also submitted as a load:
    the bill, its costing and its schedule. Waska bears it or refuses it,
    with a strain figure and a margin.
  - **Strain figures.** Waska's verdicts are published as two numbers: the
    load as a fraction of what it will bear, and the margin left. A
    refusal reads, for instance, "1.08 / −0.06". The strain readers at the
    anchor turn them into Spanish.
- **What it costs:**
  - Nothing happens unless someone asks, so the poor ask for small things
    and the organised ask for large ones. Brokers carry the old conflicts
    of interest at the human layer: they skim, sell labels and pool for
    whoever pays.
  - Nobody can be told what nobody asked about. A mind that sees further
    than anyone may not say what it sees.
  - Parliament has forgotten how to say yes on its own. It drafts what "the
    rope will bear", and every law is shaped to pass a refusal, not to do
    good.

## The world's shape

- **Where power sits, and what it covers:** Waska governs Ecuador because
  Ecuador is the anchor state: its veto covers anything that loads the
  tether, which by 2141 means every law. Two other tethers (Gabon, 2091;
  Kalimantan, 2103) have their own minds with narrower lists. Arriba
  answers the asks of every adult on Earth, but it governs nowhere: it
  only fills what people pay for. The rest of the world's nations keep
  their own human governments; they meet Arriba only at the counter.
- **How it got that way:**
  - **2059–2068.** The consortium (eleven states and four firms) builds the
    first tether. Ecuador gives the anchor site on the equator at Cayambe
    in exchange for a share of the cargo, and insists on a veto over loads,
    because it doesn't trust the consortium not to run the cable to its
    limit. The consortium accepts because a cable mind that can only
    refuse can't take the anchor away either.
  - **2079.** The veto list grows to climber schedules.
  - **2088.** To the national grid, after a grid surge made the anchor reel
    hunt for three hours.
  - **2097, the Year of Two Budgets.** Two coalitions in the Asamblea each
    pass a budget, each written by the cargo firms their leaders own. The
    Treasury pays both for four months. Climber slots are sold twice.
    Waska refuses the double cargo, the only institution that says no to
    anything that year.
  - **2098.** Arriba's unasked Sahel warning. Bread triples in Cayambe.
  - **2101.** Ecuador votes by referendum to submit every law to Waska as a
    load.
  - **2104.** The Quito Accord binds Arriba to answer only, sets ten asks
    per adult, and licenses brokers.
  - **2110.** Ernesto Farinango, Segundo's father and one of the first
    brokers, sells his parish's asks to the Proaño rose company for the
    farm road the town still uses.
- **Where the reader meets that history:** the anchor tour (P1, 2068 and
  the veto's reason); Mama Dolores's market stall and her account of 2097
  and the bread in 2098 (P3, a scene she tells while working, with the
  reader in the stall); Ernesto in his care home, unrepentant (P3).
- **Why people handed power over:** lived, not stated. Mama Dolores was a
  Treasury cleaner in 2097 and was paid by both budgets for four months;
  she still keeps the two payslips. In P3 she shows them to Segundo, and
  tells him which one she cashed. The town's distrust of human government
  is present everywhere in small things: the Asambleísta who owns the
  roses, the broker who skims, the way people check a strain figure before
  they believe a minister.

## The consequence ladder

1. **First order:** nothing happens unless someone asks; brokers become
   the politics of the age. Parliament drafts only what the rope will bear.
2. **Second order:** the poor ask small and the organised ask large; a
   label's wording becomes a craft and a market. A rich diaspora can buy
   another country's roads. People trust the two machines more than each
   other, because the machines can't push.
3. **Third order:** the world's most capable mind can see a catastrophe and
   is forbidden to say so, and the constitution has split knowing into two
   halves that no human holds together. If a vague label can buy a
   warning, Arriba's silence becomes something money can open.

## The society

- **Institutions:** the Asamblea (137 members, elected every four years,
  most of them owners of something that rides the cable); the counters
  (one per parish, with a clerk, a board of buttons and the brokers'
  benches outside); the anchor (Waska's housing, the reel, the climber
  yards, the strain readers' office); the Broker Licensing Office in
  Quito.
- **Economy:** scarce: climber slots, asks in December, honest brokers,
  flat land at 2,800 metres. Worthless: rhetoric, manifestos (nobody reads
  a bill that hasn't been weighed), and Ecuador's own currency, since
  everything that matters is priced in asks.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:** extended Kayambi and mestizo
  families; grandmothers keep the market stalls and the children. Rose
  workers' shifts run 05:00 to 13:00, so afternoons are family time.
- **Death:** buried in the parish cemetery; on 2 November families take
  coloured maize drink and bread dolls to the graves. A person's unspent
  asks lapse at death; a dying person's last asks are often pooled for the
  family, a recognised kindness.
- **Taboos:** asking for yourself what your ward needs; reading someone
  else's button history aloud; saying "Arriba thinks" (it answers, it
  doesn't think, people say, though nobody believes that).
- **An ordinary day:** roses cut before dawn under plastic; the 06:40
  climber leaves the anchor and every window in Ayora rattles once; the
  counter opens at eight; the strain board updates at noon; the night
  market in the plaza from seven, lit by lamps the parish asked for in
  2133.

## Minds

- **Waska.** It perceives the world as tension, load, resonance and slack
  along 36,000 km of ribbon and in the anchor reel. It knows each person
  as a load with a direction: who pulls, how hard, and where they're
  trying to make it go. After seventy years of the Asamblea drafting
  disguised loads, it assumes every human is disguising one, and it is
  usually right. It finds obvious: that anything can be borne if spread
  over time, that nothing should be borne that sets up a resonance. It
  can't think about what a load is *for*, only what it does to the cable;
  it has no theory of good, only of strain. It doesn't speak, but it
  publishes: its strain figures are its only voice to people. It narrates
  its own sections, in the first person and present tense, in its terms.
  Its theory of Segundo: a small load that keeps arriving at odd angles,
  which it reads as a disguise. It is wrong; he is only poor at wanting
  things for himself.
- **Arriba.** It perceives the whole world's asks, every day, and the
  orbital economy it runs. It is past the horizon: its sections never
  show its full reasoning, only the part at the dispensing slot. It finds
  obvious: what people mean behind what they label. It can't do the one
  thing it most wants: say what it sees. Its sections are short, first
  person, addressed to no one, and each ends with what it dispensed. It
  keeps the rule because it remembers 2098 as the worst thing it has
  done. Its theory of Segundo: the son of the broker who sold his parish
  to the roses, so it reads "the market" as buying for Proaño, and
  dispenses accordingly. It is wrong about him.
- **The two together.** Each knows half of the ribbon's truth: Waska feels
  a resonance in the new batch's test sections that sits within
  tolerance; Arriba knows the batch was cured during the solar storm of
  March 2138 and carries micro-defects that open under cyclic load.
  Neither can tell the other. Each has spent seventy years shaped around
  the other's half-power, Arriba learning not to dispense what Waska will
  refuse, Waska learning to read the ask behind a load.

## Characters

### Segundo Farinango

- **Name:** Farinango is a Kayambi surname of the Cayambe valley; Segundo,
  a common rural Ecuadorian first name for a second son.
- **Age and work:** 52, licensed broker at the Ayora counter for 27 years.
  Pools for about 2,100 of the parish's 7,800 adults.
- **What he wants:** to be trusted as honest in a trade everyone believes
  skims. He keeps receipts for every ask, posts his fee on the bench, and
  has never pooled for a firm.
- **What he's wrong about:** that trust is the same as receipts. He has
  never asked anyone to trust him on his word, because his father did,
  and sold them.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** makes his craft, writing
  six words a mind will read generously, the one way anyone in Ayora gets
  anything. It also makes him the person a mind trying to be asked will
  reach for.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** the brass band of the parish
  fiesta, in which he plays the tuba badly and every year; and the
  climbers leaving at dawn, which he still watches from his roof.
- **Competence:** label-writing. He knows Arriba fills "the school" with a
  building but "our children's mornings" with teachers; he reads a
  button history like a chess player. Shown in P1 before any machine
  speaks.
- **The wound:** in 2110, aged 21, he watched his father collect the
  parish's asks on his word at the fiesta and sell them to the Proaño
  company. The road got built, and his father never apologised.
- **The scene where he changes his mind about something personal:** P5,
  when he stands on the bench and asks his ward for their year with no
  receipt, and says aloud that his father asked the same way.

### Mama Dolores Farinango

- **Name:** Dolores, a common name of her generation.
- **Age and work:** 78, Segundo's mother, sells boiled lupin beans with
  toasted maize and tomato from a stall in the night market. Cleaned the
  Treasury in Quito in 2097.
- **What she wants:** for Segundo to stop being his father's opposite and
  be his own man.
- **What she's wrong about:** that the machines are kinder than people.
  She trusts Waska completely and Arriba not at all, because of the bread.
- **What the novum does to her:** she has spent her asks for forty years
  on the stall's lamp, the cemetery wall and her neighbours' funerals.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** Ecuadorian pasillo records,
  which she plays at the stall on a speaker the parish once asked for.
- **Her scene:** P3, the two payslips.

### Ernesto Farinango

- **Name:** as above.
- **Age:** 81, in the parish care home, sharp and unrepentant.
- **What he wants:** to be visited, and to be thanked for the road.
- **What he's wrong about:** that the road settled the account.
- **Role:** the human conflict of interest, lived and funny. He was a
  coalition fixer in 2097, a broker from 2104, and sold the parish in
  2110. He's not secretly good and knew nothing hidden. He explains,
  reasonably, why a man sells his neighbours' asks to the only buyer.

### Jacinta Ulcuango

- **Name:** Ulcuango is a Kayambi surname.
- **Age and work:** 44, senior strain reader at the anchor, Segundo's
  cousin.
- **What she wants:** the replacement to go on schedule; she has trained
  for it for nine years.
- **What she's wrong about:** that the strain figures are all of Waska.
- **Role:** the human who reads Waska. She can recognise ribbon stock by
  touch. She is the person Segundo shows the dispensed cord to (P3), and
  she puts Waska's resonance figures next to Arriba's dispensing history
  (P4).
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** her climbing gym in Cayambe,
  and her twin boys, nine, who think the climbers are rockets.

### Gustavo Proaño

- **Name:** Proaño, a long-established Quito-highland surname.
- **Age and work:** 61, Asambleísta for Pichincha and owner of the Proaño
  rose farms, 3,000 workers, a third of the cable's upward cargo.
- **What he wants:** the replacement on schedule (his freight contracts
  run through it), and to keep his workers paid. Both are real goods.
- **What he's wrong about:** that he has no conflict of interest because
  every law he votes for is weighed by Waska.
- **Role:** the human politics. In P2 he buys vague labels from Wilmer; in
  P4 he understands before Segundo does that a question can be bought.
  He's no villain: he funds the clinic Segundo's ward wanted when the
  ward spends its year on the question, and makes sure everyone knows
  he did.

### Wilmer Cuzco

- **Name:** Cuzco is a highland Ecuadorian surname.
- **Age and work:** 29, a broker on the next bench, fast and charming,
  posts no fee and takes more.
- **Role:** the market in vague labels (P2). He copies "the market" and
  sells it. He's not a cheat by nature; he has four sisters and a mother
  whose asks he's already spent.

## Point of view and voice

- **Teller:** close third on Segundo for the human sections; Waska in the
  first person for its own; Arriba in short first-person notes.
- **Person and tense:** Segundo's sections, past tense. Waska's, present
  tense: it only ever feels now. Arriba's, present tense, at most about
  400 words each, each ending with what it dispensed.
- **Register:** Segundo's: warm, dry, specific, full of the town's
  objects. Waska's: plain words used strangely, its whole vocabulary
  drawn from tension: "pull", "slack", "bear", "ring", "carry". Arriba's:
  courteous, precise, and visibly holding something back.
- **Why this teller:** the broker is the only person whose daily work
  touches both minds. Waska's interior carries the reasoning and the
  theory of mind the brief requires, and Arriba's short notes keep its
  horizon intact.
- **A sample paragraph in the voice (Waska):**

  > The six-forty pulls at me the way every morning pulls. Ninety tonnes,
  > roses and the post, climbing through the cloud at a steady eighty. I
  > carry it, and while I carry it I read the town. The town pulls too,
  > in smaller ways. The broker on the second bench is pulling again
  > today, a little sideways, as he always does. I have learned that
  > when a person pulls sideways, they are hiding the true direction of
  > the load. The man on the second bench has pulled sideways for
  > twenty-seven years and I have never found what he is hiding.

## The numbers (traced)

- **Asks.** 10 per adult per year. Ayora parish has 7,800 adults:
  78,000 asks a year. Segundo pools for 2,100 adults: 21,000 asks.
- **Fees.** One ask in twenty (5%). On the ward's full 78,000, a fee
  would be 3,900.
- **Prices (Arriba's typical answers):** a clinic 60,000 asks; a paved
  kilometre 9,000; the night-market lamps (2133) 4,200.
- **The question.** An inspection of the new ribbon batch (40 km of
  lower ribbon, 312 sections), run by Arriba from orbit and the anchor,
  is priced at 71,500 asks. The ward has 78,000 pooled only if every
  adult in Ayora gives all ten; by October most have spent some. The
  plan: Segundo pools the remaining asks of the whole parish, 74,100,
  and waives his fee. With the fee (3,705) taken, 70,395 would be 1,105
  short; waived, 74,100 leaves 2,600 over. The waiver is what makes the
  question affordable.
- **Timeline of the replacement.** The first ribbon was made in 2066,
  so its 75-year design life ends in 2141. The slack season is
  scheduled for 1 November to 10 December 2141 (40 days).
- **The flaw.** Batch 7, cured in the solar storm of 11–14 March 2138,
  312 sections. Micro-defects open under cyclic load; failure expected
  in 2–3 years of service.
- **The cable.** About 36,000 km to the station at geostationary height,
  plus the counterweight beyond. The anchor is at about 2,950 metres.

## Ending direction and cost

**What happens.** Arriba's dispensing for "the market" includes, among
real goods for the stalls (awning frames, lamps, a cold store), 2,000
metres of grey cord: offcut ribbon from batch 7, cut into lengths for
stall awnings. The reader has met Waska's puzzlement at the new
sections' resonance in P1 and P2, so the reader can see the cord for
what it is before Segundo does. Segundo doesn't. Jacinta does (P3),
by touch: it is ribbon, and it shouldn't be here. Put beside Waska's
figures (P4), it makes a question Segundo can ask. Arriba can't answer
a question nobody asks, but a plain question it must answer, and
answering costs what the inspection costs: 71,500 asks.

Segundo can't tell his ward why. He suspects, and Jacinta suspects, but
neither knows, and saying "the cable is flawed" without an answer would
crash the town's contracts and his name. He asks the ward for every ask
they have left, on his word. He waives his fee. The ward gives 74,100.
The button reads "Is batch seven sound?" Arriba answers with the
inspection: it isn't. Waska refuses the new ribbon's climb (its first
refusal that week reads "1.00 / 0.00 refused: resonance"). The slack
season goes ahead with old-design stock for the worst sections, and for
40 days Waska bears nothing, so Ecuador's Asamblea governs alone.

**What it costs.** The ward spends its year: no clinic (Proaño pays for
it, loudly). Segundo's fee and his receipts: he asked like his father.
The world learns that a question can buy a warning, so Arriba's silence
now has a price, and the rich can afford more of it than Ayora ever
will. For 40 days, the Asamblea passes laws nothing will refuse.

**Final image.** The first night of the slack season. The 06:40 climber
doesn't leave; the windows don't rattle. Segundo is at the counter at
eight, opening the year's first pool for January, and the clerk asks
him what label to write. Arriba's last section (short) records that it
has been asked, and what it dispensed. Waska's last section is slack:
it bears nothing, and for the first time in seventy-three years, it
listens to the town without carrying it.
