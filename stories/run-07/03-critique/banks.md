# Stage 3 critique: Banks lens

**Blind spots, stated.** This lens favours scale, set pieces and machinery, and it is weak at keeping a story small. A novella is nearer its natural form than a short story, but it will still tend to reward the premise with the most world in it. Weigh my ranking against Chiang on whether the feeling comes from the idea, and against Vinge on futurity. I have not checked prior art; the Cartographer does that.

Length note for every premise: the brief asks for 30,000 to 40,000 words and the "estimated minimum" lines all say 3,000 to 6,000. None is over the ceiling, but each needs three or four movements to fill a novella, and I say below where that is easy and where it is not.

## A. The Grudge-Keeper

**Plain pitch:** holds. "Sleep through winter so you wake without old feuds" is clear to an outsider, and the feud history gives the reason. The pitch's second sentence (a treaty resting on one old man) is the hook.

**Moral friction:** the best in the set. The governor decides "how much of autumn to tell" the spring selves, and it filters what news reaches Anders. That is a benevolent hegemon doing dirty work. Who signed off? The waking parliament that voted for the torpor. Nobody asked the sleepers about each spring's edit.

**Dirty work:** the governor, plus the unnamed designers of the pruning. Name the people who chose it, and have the story hold them to account.

**Humour:** the choir. A tenor who forgets each April that he loathes the bass section is a joke and also the novum. Use it.

**Scale and intimacy:** one concrete image of vastness is available: a whole nation asleep, with one mind awake over it in frost. Reach goes past the household (the freight corridor, the heating debt). The Banks move is "power that needs", and this is it: two governors that need a man they cannot replace.

**Enjoyable under the charter:** yes. The risk is the noble-death ending. Make Anders's choice cost the governors something other than his life.

**Seed gaps:** national versus global is only a pair of neighbours. At novella length add a scene where a larger body is invoked. Mechanics of law need a scene where the governor drafts and passes something while the electorate sleeps.

## B. The Heater List

**Plain pitch:** holds, narrowly. "Heat decides how fast people think" needs a scene, not a clause.

**Moral friction:** real. The governor cannot tell generosity from appetite. That is the nicest conflict of interest in the set and a Banks move in itself: a mind that wants something it cannot take.

**Dirty work:** the lottery jury, and the governor's own thermostat. The list of cooled habitats is the atrocity.

**Humour:** thin. A warmed man talking over his slow family could be funny, but nothing here promises it.

**Scale and intimacy:** a moon's heat budget against one family's pace is good. Reach is solid (whose children learn slowly).

**Enjoyable?** Cold, slow and rationed. It is the grimmest to live in.

**Failure:** speed of thought is hard to show without lecture, and it is the time-rate attractor the audit already flags. Over 30,000 words the single jury season is also thin.

## C. The Felt Law

**Plain pitch:** holds, and has the best hook in the set: "the laws that work best die first."

**Moral friction:** good but small. The law's own proposal to amend itself into something harsher is the strong beat, and the perfumers as lobbyists is a real cost.

**Dirty work:** the refinery bloc, and the law that would make the city irritable forever. Nobody is clearly responsible for the century's bribed officials who started it.

**Humour:** the best in the set: a city that smells of vetiver when it nears its pollution cap.

**Scale and intimacy:** Kochi, a nose and a clause. Scale is the weak point: where is the world? There is no global layer at all, and "AI governs" is true only of the statute-minds.

**Enjoyable?** Very. **Failure at novella length:** the single contest between two perfumers cannot hold 30,000 words without padding. Seed adherence on national versus global is the weakest here.

## D. The Overheard

**Plain pitch:** holds, and the cleaner-fish idea is vivid. It also plays well to non-genre readers.

**Moral friction:** thin. The stakes are epistemic. The brief calls for a morally serious thought experiment about consent and governance, and this is a puzzle about whether machines are honest.

**Dirty work:** the codebreaking bureau, which reads private conversations, and nobody in the premise is made to answer for it.

**Humour:** the most available of any option (crossword setter, comic stations).

**Scale and intimacy:** two stations and one retired man is good, but Reach is only "trust in the station". The twist ("the stations knew") sits close to the off-limits manipulation reveal.

**Enjoyable?** Yes. **Failure:** it is the premise that least engages "will people accept its policy" and the mechanics of governing. A novella of decrypts is the run-06 memo again.

## E. Two Half-Powers

**Plain pitch:** holds. "One machine can only say no, another only answers when asked" is clear, and the broker is a recognisable trade.

**Moral friction:** large. The global mind sees a disaster and is forbidden to say. Who signed off on that silence? The consortium, after its own abuses. That is a benevolent power that did terrible things being constrained by the memory of them.

**Dirty work:** the brokers who skim, the consortium's old abuses, and the charter that makes silence lawful. The people in the anchor grid who will pay for it are the stakes.

**Humour:** a button labelled "the market" and a ward negotiating plain-language buttons; this has room for wit. Hold the register deliberately, or it will be grey.

**Scale and intimacy:** the strongest. A tether to orbit is an image of vastness the reader can stand under, set against one ward's night market. Reach goes past the household by design.

**Enjoyable?** Yes, if the disaster is kept off flood and tide as the risks line says.

**Seed fit:** the only premise that answers all four of the seed's questions: governance by AI, acceptance (rations of requests), national versus global (two negative powers), understanding (a button anyone can read). Procedure is the action of scenes. It also fills 30,000 words: ration pooling, a consent hearing, a broker's scheme, a warning that cannot be said.

## Ranking

1. **E.** The only premise built on the seed's national-versus-global question, with real procedure, moral friction and room for wit.
2. **A.** The best moral friction and strongest sense of place; needs a third movement and a global note.
3. **C.** The best hook and humour, thinnest on scale and the seed.
4. **B.** A superb dilemma, but the time-rate attractor and the hardest to dramatise.
5. **D.** Fun, but least governance and nearest the off-limits twist.

## Why E should win

The brief asks for a thought experiment about consent and governance at novella length with the mechanics on the page, and E is the one that is built as a constitution: two powers that can each only refuse or answer, with no policy until a citizen asks and a national mind agrees to bear it. That gives every scene a procedure to run and every procedure a joke: a button's label, a ration pooled, a veto read aloud. The moral engine is the sentence the premise already contains, a mind that sees a catastrophe and is forbidden to say, and a second mind that can refuse but never warn. That is the best form of the Banks question, who signed off on this silence and who pays for it. Keep Dewi's own motives murky enough that she is a person with a market to protect, not a courier. Hold back the broker economy until the middle, and let the ending leave the charter's silence changed by a price, not by a speech.
