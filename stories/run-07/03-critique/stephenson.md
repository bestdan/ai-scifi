# Stage 3 critique: Stephenson lens

**Blind spots, stated.** I reward sprawl, and I'm biased toward the premise with the most machinery to explain. I don't judge restraint, and I'm weak on whether an ending lands (Chiang's job) or whether a premise is clear to someone outside the genre (Asimov's and Banks's). I rate a character by whether they are shown being good at something, and I can miss emotional weight that comes from stillness. Discount my enthusiasm for E and D accordingly.

**Length note.** Every premise lists a 4,500-word minimum and the brief asks for 30,000 to 40,000. None is too big for the top of the range. The question is which one can carry about 35,000 words without repeating one mechanism, and the brief says "each part raises a question the earlier parts didn't". That is the test I apply.

## A. The Grudge-Keeper

- **System:** The best institution in the set is not on the page yet. The grudge-keepers, a few hundred people who refuse torpor, are a monastic order in everything but name. They have a calendar and a duty to carry winter memory, and they presumably have schisms. Something like Anathem's avout. Who feeds them, and who outranks whom when Anders is dying?
- **Exploit:** The treaty is "whatever Anders would decide", so Anders is the attack surface. A governor that controls what reaches him controls the treaty. This is a real hole, and the premise sees it ("the news it lets reach him").
- **Competence:** The governors are competent. I want Anders, or someone in the choir, to be good at something. A choir director's ear for a lying voice is a ready skill.
- **Hole:** The mechanics of governing are thin. Where does a law get drafted, costed and passed? The spring ratification, when sleepers wake to a governor's summary of autumn, is the missing scene. That is the Stephenson-grade institution: a Wake Office that decides what the spring selves are told.
- **Seed gap:** The national-versus-global question is answered only sideways.
- **Ending:** The dying-man ending sits next to the noble sacrifice. Make the answer a procedure (a succession protocol for a modelled man), not a death scene.

## B. The Heater List

- **System:** Very clean. Heat is thought-speed, and the governor allocates the heat. That is a real conflict of interest built into the physics, and a good exploit: Tobias can ask for heat to out-think the machine.
- **Delight:** The Q10 and radioisotope numbers can carry digressions that pay off.
- **Risk:** The time-rate cluster is an attractor, and a story about speed is hard to show without lecturing. A character's thoughts coming faster is a thing to be shown, with sentences that change length, and I doubt a drafter manages it for 35,000 words.
- **Second-half risk:** One jury season is one mechanism. The middle repeats the same argument at higher heat.
- **Competence:** A kelp foreman is a fine competent protagonist, but his skill is farming. His skill at arguing has to be earned on the page.
- **Verdict:** Strong core, short-story-shaped.

## C. The Felt Law

- **System:** Wit on every page, and the best exploit in the set. The masking accord is a real attack: nothing in the rule forbids making a law go unnoticed. Sample-based lapse is a real mechanism with a testable edge case, the way a quorum rule is.
- **Digression:** Perfumery is the richest trade here. Olfactory fatigue, base notes, and the way a vetiver note could be detected below the level of noticing would all pay off.
- **Hole:** The seed asks for an AI that governs, and the law-minds are a plurality of narrow wants. The national-versus-global question is absent. Who decides which law to spin, and who drafts the purpose clause? That drafting is the book's best scene, and the premise doesn't name it.
- **Voice:** One law narrating its own chapters has one want. Thirty thousand words of that voice will flatten. Give the laws a conflict with each other: the clean-air law against the refinery-licensing law.
- **Ending:** "Harsher law felt forever" is the real cost, and it's a good one.

## D. The Overheard

- **System bug:** The premise breaks its own tradecraft. Parliament prints the decrypts every morning, so the stations learn on day one that the line is cracked. That is the Ultra problem from Bletchley, and the premise contains no Ultra discipline. Any real bureau would act on the intercepts only through cover stories. Fix it and the premise improves: the bureau must hide that it reads, so the printing cannot be literal.
- **Fix, as a digression:** The cover-story discipline is load-bearing material. How do you use a secret without revealing it, and what does Marcelo do when he cannot?
- **Competence:** A crossword setter reading ciphertext is the best competent amateur in the set.
- **Risk:** Run-06's morning memo in a new coat, as the audit says. The reasoning is the text, which is good.
- **Length:** 6,000 is the highest minimum, and the twist ("they knew") is the off-limits stock darkness unless earned.

## E. Two Half-Powers

- **System:** The only constitution in the set. Two negative powers (one can only refuse, one can only answer) plus a ration economy is a spec a clever character can attack. Brokers pooling rations, a button labelled "the market", and skimming are an economy with a hole in it. I want the first exploit shown on the page: a vague label that buys a question the ration can't legally ask.
- **Seed fit:** The national-versus-global split is native. People understanding how they are governed is answered by the plain-language buttons, a place where a bureaucracy turns law into UI.
- **Process:** A request is drafted, pooled, costed against load, and refused or carried, and that is the law-making the brief asks for.
- **Parts:** It splits naturally into parts that each raise a new question: the button, the broker market, the skim, the anchor grid, and the tether replacement the Le Guin card proposes.
- **Risk:** Two minds plus a broker economy is a lot, so keep the tether mind's voice to strain and load and don't give both minds equal page time. The forbidden-warning shape is the Cassandra trope, but the money-opens-the-silence turn makes it new.
- **Competence:** Dewi's craft is pooling and wording requests. Show her at the counter.

## Ranking

1. **E, Two Half-Powers:** the only premise that is a constitution a clever person can exploit, and it answers every seed question.
2. **A, The Grudge-Keeper:** the best institution and the most colour, but light on procedure and the seed's global question.
3. **C, The Felt Law:** the funniest exploit, with a gap on the global layer.
4. **D, The Overheard:** good competent amateur, but the printed-decrypts premise needs the Ultra fix.
5. **B, The Heater List:** clean idea, in the attractor cluster, and a single mechanism.

## Why E should win

E gives the drafter something to build: a spec with two deliberately crippled powers and a human economy growing in the gap. In a spec like that the exploit is the plot, and an exploit can recur at larger scale in each part, which is what a 35,000-word novella needs. The brief's must-haves (procedure on the page, the AI reasoning, why people accept the machines) all sit in the broker's daily work: she writes the label, the global mind reads it, the tether mind weighs the load. The humour is in the buttons. The first exploit (a vague label that smuggles a question) turns into a market for questions by part three, and a market that can buy the global mind's silence is a consequence followed far. What I'd ask of the Decision: keep the minds' voices short, and give Dewi one clear scene of competence before any machine speaks. Take A's grudge-keeper order as a seed-stock item.
