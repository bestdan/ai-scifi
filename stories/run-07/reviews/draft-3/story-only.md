# What the Rope Will Bear

## Part One: The Button

*Received: 1 ask, from Rosaura Quishpe. Label: our children's mornings. Fee: 0.05. Pooled: 0.95.*

At six in the morning the bench was cold enough to take the skin off a careless hand, so Segundo Farinango unlocked it with his gloves on. It was the second bench under the portico of the Ayora counter, oak, with a hinged lid and a padlock he had replaced twice in twenty-seven years. Inside it were the things a broker needed and nothing else: a slate with a cracked corner, a tin of pencils, a folding stool, and a stack of receipt books with carbon sheets between the pages, the kind the stationer in Cayambe sold by the box of twelve and nobody but Segundo bought. He set the stool out. He took the card from under the lid and hung it on its nail at the front of the bench, where anyone crossing the plaza could read it.

SEGUNDO FARINANGO. BROKER, LICENCE 0419. FEE: ONE ASK IN TWENTY. RECEIPTS GIVEN.

The last two words were his own. The licence office didn't require them and the other brokers on the portico didn't write them, and every few years one of them asked him whether he thought they made him look honest or only slow.

Across the plaza the bakery had its shutter half up and its light on, and the smell of the first trays came over the cobbles with the cold. Above the roofs to the east the mountain had come out of the dark. Cayambe kept its snow all year, right on the equator, and at this hour the snow was the pink of the inside of a shell while everything below it was still blue. The lower slopes were striped with the plastic roofs of the rose farms, row on row, beaded with a frost that would be gone by eight. The cutters had been under that plastic since five. He could see their headlamps moving inside it, small and yellow, a whole town of people bent over looking for something they'd dropped.

He crossed to the bakery for four rolls and a coffee. The baker had a sheet of paper taped to the inside of his glass, a copy of something off the counter's board, and he kept glancing at it while he made change.

"You've read it," the baker said.

"I read it last night."

The baker turned the sheet round so Segundo could read it again anyway. At the top was the label the four bakeries of the parish had pooled behind, three asks each: *Is Ortega's flour clean?* Under it, the price, 12, and the answer, one sentence long.

*The flour milled at Ortega's mill in Cayambe on 30 August 2141 contains no contaminant above the limits set by the national food standard.*

"Twelve asks," the baker said. "Three of them my wife's. We had that sack in July that made everything taste of candles. So it tells me the flour from Wednesday is clean. I know Wednesday's flour is clean. I baked it. You're eating it."

"You asked whether the flour was clean," Segundo said. "It went and looked at the flour there was."

"I meant all of it."

"All of it costs what looking at all of it costs. Every sack since July. Every day's milling." He tore a roll in half. It was very good, and he said so, and the baker was not consoled. "Ortega could ask. He has the asks."

"Ortega," the baker said, and made a noise in his nose, and took the next customer.

At twenty to seven every window in Ayora rattled once. It came across the parish as a single shiver in the glass, the bakery's window, the church's, the counter's, the panes of the houses going up the hill, and then it was over, and the dogs who had been asleep were awake and the ones who had been barking had stopped. Segundo went out from under the portico with his coffee to look. Four kilometres up the slope, where the anchor sat in its fences on the mountain's flank, the ribbon was invisible except when the low sun caught it, and then it was a hair of light going straight up out of the anchor's roof into nothing. Somewhere on it the six-forty climber was rising with the morning's roses and the post. For a few seconds he could see it as a bead, dark against the cloud that sat on the mountain's shoulder, and then the cloud had it. He had watched it go every morning of his life that he could remember, and he had never once seen it come out the top.

The clerk arrived at seven, an hour before she had to, so that the board would be updated before the brokers started talking at her. She was a broad woman from Olmedo with her hair in a net, and she raised the shutter on the counter's long window with a crank that shrieked on every turn. Behind the glass the board came on, a wall of grey panels taller than a man: labels down the left side, then their pool counts, their prices, and their answers, where there were answers. Most labels had none yet. They were waiting to fill.

"The bakers are taking their flour personally," Segundo said.

"The bakers can take it up with Arriba," the clerk said, and jerked her chin at the sky, at the mind at the top of the cable, which everyone in Ayora called Arriba, up there, the way you would point at a landlord's window. "It answered what they paid for. It always does."

At half past seven the children came through the plaza on their way to the school, in navy tracksuits, carrying their lunches in plastic bags. Three girls at the back had a clapping game going, the oldest game in the parish, and Segundo listened for his favourite part. In Spanish it rhymed. In plain words it went:

*Arriba, up the rope,*

*never lies and never tells.*

*It told us once when no one asked,*

*and the bread cost three times in Cayambe.*

On the third line the girls crossed hands and clapped each other's opposite palms, which was the hard part, and the smallest of them missed and they started again from the top. That was the first rule anyone learned, before the alphabet: Arriba never told you anything you hadn't asked, and never told you anything false. His mother could tell you about the bread, if you had an hour. The children only knew it went in the third line.

The first client of the day came at half past eight, perhaps nineteen, in rubber boots and a fleece with the big farm's mark on the chest. Her wrists were scratched in short pale lines, as every cutter's were by the second week, and she had green on her fingers from the stems. It was her rest day, she said, the first in eleven. She'd come from Tabacundo in June, where her family pooled through the parish council, and here she didn't know anybody, and a woman in her shed said to go to the second bench because that one wrote things down.

"Sit," Segundo said, and she sat on the edge of the bench because he had the stool.

She knew about the asks. Everybody did: ten on your screen on the first of January, each a small piece of what Arriba will do for you, yours and nobody else's, gone on the thirty-first if you didn't spend them. What she didn't know was what a broker was for, so he gave her that part and skipped the rest.

"One ask buys almost nothing on its own," he said. "So people pool. You give me an ask, by contract. I put it with two thousand others behind a label, and I keep one in twenty for the work. A label posted at the counter with a pool behind it, the counter calls a button, because that's what it is on the screens: a thing you press." He nodded at the board. "Every one of those is a button." He tapped the card. "That's my living, and my licence says I have to post it. Some brokers post one in twenty and take a bit more besides, in favours. I write down every one I take." He showed her a receipt book, the white top sheet and the yellow copy under it. "You give me one ask, I write you a receipt for a twentieth of an ask. When twenty people have paid me twentieths, I've earned one."

She looked at the receipt book with an expression he knew. People were never sure whether it was a kindness or an insult.

"My mother says it lies," she said. "Arriba."

"Your mother is wrong. It never lies. It answers what you paid for, exactly, and if you paid for the wrong question that's your six words, not its." He pointed across the plaza at the bakery's glass. "You see the flour?"

She had seen it. Everyone had seen it.

"The bakers asked whether Ortega's flour is clean. It cost them twelve asks, and they think the twelve were for the sentence. They weren't. Somebody had to go to Ortega's mill and test a day's milling, or something did, and that's what twelve asks buys: the testing. A question costs what answering it takes. Ask about one day's flour and you pay for one day. Ask about every sack the mill has ever ground and you pay for every sack."

"So you can find out anything."

"Anything you can afford to have looked at."

"And it won't just say. If something's wrong."

"Not if nobody asks. Not ever." He let her sit with that, which was the part of the speech he liked least, because people always looked up afterwards, toward the cloud, as if something up there were holding its breath. She looked up.

"The farm has a label," she said. "In the shed. They said we can pool behind it if we want."

"They can ask you to. A firm can't hold an ask, but it can put a label up and ask its workers to pool behind it, and plenty do."

"Do you do that? Pool for the farm?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because then I'd be working for the farm," Segundo said, and she nodded as if that were obvious, and it was, and it was also the only true thing he had ever found to say about it in twenty-seven years.

While she was reading his contract an old woman came across the plaza with a felt hat on and a wool shawl crossed over her chest, walking the way the old women of Ayora walked, straight-backed and quick, as if someone had just said something about her. She stood in front of the bench until the girl moved up, and then she sat too. She had her label already written, in pencil, on the back of a seed packet. *The school.*

"The teacher left in July," she said. "For Quito. The anchor company's school pays better. My granddaughter's class has had a substitute twice a week and nothing the other three days. They sit in the room. They draw. She has drawn me eleven horses."

"Señora Quishpe," Segundo said. He knew her. Everyone knew her; she had sold cheese in the plaza for forty years and was owed money by half the parish. "Let me show you something." He turned the slate so she could see it and called up the button history, the record of every button the counters of the valley had ever posted and what each had bought. He found *the school* four times in ten years. Olmedo, 2136: a roof, steel, with gutters. Cangahua, 2139: a new wing of two rooms. Ayora itself, 2131: paint, fourteen tins, and a boiler.

"It reads 'the school' as the school," he said. "The building. You'll get a boiler."

"We have a boiler."

"Then you'll get paint. It reads what you write. And it reads who writes it, which in this case is me, and I've never pooled for a building in my life, so it might think harder, but I wouldn't bet your ask on it." He took a pencil from the tin and crossed out *the school*, gently, and under it he wrote *our children's mornings*. "What fills a morning? Not a roof. A roof is there in the afternoons too. Somebody standing at the front of the room is what fills a morning."

She read it twice, her lips moving. "That's three words."

"It's enough. The other three would only give it room to misunderstand."

She paid him one ask. She held her screen out at arm's length, as old people did, and touched it where he showed her, and on his slate the ask arrived with her name on it. He wrote the receipt in pencil, pressing hard so the carbon would take, in the form he had used for twenty-seven years: *Received: 1 ask, from Rosaura Quishpe. Label: our children's mornings. Fee: 0.05. Pooled: 0.95.* He tore off the white sheet and gave it to her. She folded it twice and put it in the band of her hat.

"Now it goes on the board," he told her, and the girl from Tabacundo, who was listening with her mouth a little open. "Arriba reads the words, and reads who wrote them, and decides what answers them. It puts a price on that. When enough asks are in, it fills, and whatever it sends comes down the rope, and the rope has to agree to carry it. Nothing comes till somebody asks. Then the rope has its say."

The girl had watched all of this. When Señora Quishpe had gone she said her name was Luz Caiza, and that her little brother was in the fourth grade here and drew horses too, and could she put hers in the mornings. She put her thumb to his contract, and he wrote her a receipt, and she put it in her fleece pocket and zipped the pocket.

Ninety tonnes leave the reel and I take them, because I am the rope they climb.

When the reel starts it jerks once against the rock it is bolted to, and the jerk runs through the mountain into the town below, and every window there shakes. Then the weight is on me. Sixty-two hundredths of what I will bear for this climb. Thirty-eight hundredths left. That is the number they will post.

I take them the way I take everything, all along me at once. The climber is a weight at my foot, gripping, rising at eighty an hour up through the wet of the cloud on the mountain's shoulder, and then the cloud is under it and the air thins and the cold comes and the air ends. I feel all of that only as a change in how the weight sits on me. Above it there are eighteen more, spaced along my length like knots tied in a cord, each one a morning older than the one below it, the highest eighteen days out and nearly at the station. Coming down the other face of me are the descending ones, lighter, braking, giving back to the reel what the rising ones took. The station is thirty-six thousand kilometres up. It turns with the earth so that it hangs over this mountain always, and past it the counterweight swings outward and pulls, harder than every climber together. Between the reel and the counterweight I am stretched. I have never not been stretched. That is what I am.

At my foot is the town.

The town is a few grams. Less: the town is a rounding. Next to the counterweight it does not pull at all. But everything that comes down me comes down to it, and every load arrives with a label and a pool and the number of the broker who wrote the label. The town pulls on me in small ways all day, through those numbers.

This morning the Asamblea's bill on the tariff for orbital steel is on me too. It is a law, and a law is a load. They send me the bill, its costing and its schedule, and I feel what it will do: how much more steel will come down me in which months, which climbs it will crowd, where the strain will gather. Ninety-seven hundredths of what I will bear in those months. Three hundredths left. I bear it. I do not care what steel is for. I care what it weighs.

A label is a mark to me. I don't know what its words mean. I know which marks come down with which weights. When *the school* comes down, at this counter or at Olmedo or at Cangahua, it comes heavy and square and all at once, the same shape every time, and so do the marks that look like it. Most brokers' loads fall like the other loads filed under marks like theirs. I bear them, and I forget them except as the wear they leave.

Four-one-nine pulls sideways. It is a broker's licence number, and it comes on the manifest of every load he sends.

His loads fall like no others filed under marks like his. Where the others come heavy, his come light. Where the others come at once, his come in pieces, spread over months. Once, a drum kit, under a mark that at every other counter has come down as stone, and has never come down as anything that weighs what a drum kit weighs. Everything he sends arrives at an angle to the loads around it. I have learned what that angle means from the Asamblea, which has sent me bills dressed as lighter than they are every season for forty years: when a load falls unlike its kind, someone is hiding its true direction. I have found what the Asamblea hides almost every time. I have weighed four-one-nine since his number first came on a manifest, in a year the manifests call 2114, and I have never found what he hides. So I weigh everything he sends at the full margin, slowly, every way it could pull, and I always will.

That is the town. Now the test line.

At my foot, beside the reel, the consortium has hung pieces of the new ribbon on frames, to be worked before they carry anything. They are the replacement for my lowest forty kilometres, the length that sways most in the weather and takes every climber first. Machines pull them and let them go, pull them and let them go, two thousand times a day, which is what the weather does to me. The engineers wired the frames to me so I could feel the new ribbon as I feel my own. Four batches hang there. When the machines pull them they hum, low, one long note to each pull, and the hum runs up the wires into me. The engineers call it the ring. Five rings true. Six rings true. Eight rings true.

Seven's hum is a little off. Seven rings a little more.

Four hundredths more than the others, every cycle, the same four hundredths. Tolerance is a tenth.

I know one thing without having to weigh it: nothing that rings past tolerance should be borne. I knew it when I was only a damper on a cable and no one had asked me anything. And I refuse only what pulls past what I bear. A ring inside the rating pulls nothing. Seven rings inside tolerance. It hangs on a frame in a shed, and it does not pull on me.

I file it as noise. Still, the four hundredths sit in me unweighed, like a load that has been announced and not yet sent.

I have nowhere to keep a thing that does not pull. My memory is my wear. Every load I have carried is still in me, as creep in the fibre and fatigue in the joins, and I remember a thing by feeling where it has worn me. The ring has not worn me. So every morning I notice it again, as if for the first time, and every morning I put it down again where I found it.

What I want is less. Everything I do leans toward carrying less. When a climber reaches the station I feel the relief of it run all the way down me to the reel. I have never once been slack. In November they will take the tension off my lowest forty kilometres and change them, and I have been told the date, and I count toward it.

The six-forty passes ten kilometres. The air is thin around it. The town shakes off the morning and starts to pull.

At eleven Segundo left the bench with the clerk, who would watch it but not touch it, and took the fourth grade up to the anchor.

He had done it every September for nine years. The school asked a parent, no other parent ever volunteered, and he liked it: the bus grinding up the switchbacks between the greenhouses, thirty-one nine-year-olds with their faces pressed to the windows, the long fence coming into view. This year the school had sent a helper, a girl from the upper school who needed service hours, and the girl was his daughter, Sisa, and she had made it clear on the bus that she was here for the hours and not for him. She sat at the back with her earpiece in and dealt with the boys who stood on the seats, quickly and without mercy, and he was proud of her and kept it to himself.

The anchor sat on a shelf cut into the mountain at nearly three thousand metres. Up here the wind came off the snow, the children's breath showed, and the hard high light made everything look closer than it was. They got down in the climber yards, where tomorrow's roses were already coming in.

It was the children's favourite part, and his. Refrigerated containers stood in rows on the concrete, white, each the size of a small room, each stencilled with the mark of a farm. Workers in quilted jackets walked between them with scanners. Through an open door the children could see the roses themselves, racked in buckets, thousands of them, red and white and a pink like the morning snow, every bunch wrapped in paper with its farm's name. The cold that came out of the door smelled of cut stems and wet paper. A worker let the children stand in it for a moment, and they screamed with the cold and the smell of it, and Sisa told them to stop screaming, and they stopped.

"Where are they going?" one of the boys asked.

"Up," Segundo said. "To the station, and from the station to the habitats, where there are people who've never seen a rose grow. They pay for them in what the station makes. That's what comes down. Engines. Medicine. The panels on your roof. Your teacher's slate." He pointed at the fence, at the valley below, the plastic roofs and the town and the road to Cayambe. "One family in four in this valley works up here, or on the climbers, or in the yards. Everyone else grows the roses that go up. The rope is how we live."

The reel house was the size of the church in Cayambe and twice as tall, and inside it was warm and smelled of hot oil, and the children went quiet the moment they came in. The reel itself was a drum three storeys high, painted the grey of a pigeon. From the top of it the ribbon went up through a slot in the roof. That was all you could see: a grey band a little wider than a man's shoulders and thinner than a fingernail, rising off the drum and out into the daylight. It did not look like anything. It looked like a strip of tape a giant had left behind.

"That's it?" a girl said.

"That's the bottom of it."

"It's thin."

"It goes thirty-six thousand kilometres up," Segundo said. "If you drove a bus straight up it, without stopping, day and night, it would take you nineteen days. It's thin because it has to hold itself up the whole way."

He reached into his pocket for the length of string he brought every year and gave one end to the girl. "Pull."

She pulled, and he let her pull him a step forward, and the children laughed.

"Now push."

She pushed. The string folded up between their hands and lay there. She pushed harder, and it only folded more, and she looked at it with her lip out, as if it had cheated.

"A rope can pull," Segundo said. "It can hold. And it can go slack. It can't push. You can't push anything with a rope. Remember that, because it's going to be on your test." There was no such test. It was the most important thing he knew.

The strain readers' office was a glass box on a gallery above the reel, and on its outside wall, facing the gallery, was a smaller board than the counter's, with columns of figures on it, two numbers to a row. His cousin came out of the office when she saw the children. She was in a fleece with the consortium's patch on it and had a pencil behind her ear and climbing tape on two of her fingers, and she gave the children the look of a woman who had nine-year-olds at home and knew exactly what they were capable of.

"Your cousin reads the rope's numbers," Segundo told them, and the children looked at her as if he had said she could read minds.

His cousin found one row for them, low on the board, and tapped it. *Second climber yard, Cayambe. 1.06 / −0.06.* "That was a law," she said. "In March. The Asamblea passed it, to build another yard and send up more roses faster. Every law the Asamblea passes comes here, to the rope, before it's a law at all. The rope worked out what the yard would put on it. One point oh six of what it would bear, six parts too many. So the second number goes below zero. That means no."

"Why?" said the boy who'd asked about the roses.

"That's all it says. Six parts too many. It doesn't say if the yard was a good idea. It doesn't say how to fix it. A number, that's all." She tapped the glass. "We're strain readers. We turn the rope's numbers into Spanish, for the Asamblea and the papers and anybody who asks. That's my job."

A girl near the front had her hand up and was holding it up with her other hand. "What's the rope called?"

"Waska," said Segundo, before his cousin could. "The old word for rope, because that is all it says it is."

The children said it to each other, *Waska*, and some of them giggled, because it was the word their grandmothers used for a washing line.

On the reel's iron frame, at a child's eye height, was a brass plate gone green at the edges, and he made them read it out together: *2068. Nothing goes up or down this rope that the rope will not bear.* Ecuador had given the consortium the mountain, he told them, and asked for that line in return, for the mind that steadied the rope. Each side was afraid of the other. A mind that can only say no can't run the rope to ruin for money, and it can't take the rope away.

"Your grandmothers will tell you why the Asamblea sends it the laws too," he said. "Ask them. It's a long story and they tell it better than me."

"And Arriba?" said the girl who'd pushed the string.

"Arriba is at the other end," Segundo said. "At the top. Arriba can do almost anything, but only if you ask. And Waska can't do anything at all except say no." He looked at their faces and tried to say it so that they would remember it, and heard himself say it exactly as he had said it nine Septembers running.

"One of them can only say no, and the other can only say yes when you ask. So if you want anything, you ask, and you hope the rope agrees."

He let it sit. It was the whole country in two sentences. Nothing came down unless somebody asked for it, and nothing came down that the rope wouldn't carry, and in nine years he had never found a shorter way to say it.

The children thought about that. Sisa, at the back, rolled her eyes at the ceiling, where the ribbon went out.

"What if the rope wants something?" said the boy.

"It can't want anything," Segundo said. "It's a rope."

He said it easily, and the children accepted it, and his cousin, behind them, said nothing at all. But on the bus going down, with the boys asleep against the windows and the greenhouses flashing by, he thought of something he never mentioned on the tour. Whenever he pooled for anything heavier than a crate, the rope held it in the yard for a week or two before it let it come down to the town. Other brokers' loads came straight through. His waited. He had asked his cousin about it once, years ago, and she had shrugged and said the rope took its time with some things, and he had decided it meant nothing, because a rope can't want anything. He watched the greenhouses go by and decided it again.

His wife, Lourdes, came home at a quarter to eight with the dispensary on her clothes. It was a smell he would have known in the dark, disinfectant and wet wool and the sweet rubber of the blood-pressure cuff, and she brought it in with her every evening and took it off with her coat.

"Seventy-one," she said, before he could ask. She hung the coat on the hook by the door, over the tuba case. "Seventy-one people, two rooms, me and the girl from Cangahua, and the girl from Cangahua leaves at four."

The dispensary was behind the counter, two rooms off the portico with a bench outside, and he saw its queue every day from his own bench: mothers with babies inside their shawls, cutters with their hands wrapped in rags, old men who came every Tuesday to have their pressure taken and sit somewhere warm. By noon the queue went along the wall past the brokers. By four it went round the corner.

"Bad?" he said.

"A cutter's hand, opened by the shears to the bone, here." She drew a line on her own palm. "I closed it. A baby with a chest I didn't like. I sent her to Cayambe in the back of the Pilataxis' pickup with her mother holding the bottle, because we have one oxygen bottle and it's half empty, and if anyone else needed it tonight they'd have to wait for the pickup to come back." She sat at the table and took off her shoes without untying them. "So it was a day."

Sisa was at the stove with her back to them, stirring the soup, which was potatoes and cheese and the last of the avocado. They ate in the kitchen, as they always did, with the window steamed over and the radio low on the shelf. Sisa had toasted a pan of maize and put it on the table in a bowl, and they ate it by handfuls between spoonfuls, salted, still hot enough to burn. Segundo told them about the fourth grade and the string. Sisa told them that he had said the thing about the rope pushing nine years in a row, and that she had been on the tour herself in the fourth grade and he had said it then too, word for word.

"It's still true," he said.

"I'm not saying it's not true. I'm saying it's the same."

Lourdes's screen lit on the table beside her bowl. She looked at it, and then she put her spoon down. "They've posted it," she said.

He knew what she meant. Every January the parish council posted the December label, the one the whole parish pooled behind, and this year it was the label she had wanted since 2134. Seven years. December had gone to the irrigation channels, then to the Cayambe road, then to the school roof, and each year she had been in the parish hall when the vote went another way, and each year she had said, walking home, *next year*. This year the council had put up *Ayora's clinic*, and Arriba had priced it in January at 44,000 asks, and it had sat on the board all year with a pool count of nothing while the parish held its asks for December. Now the council had posted the meeting. She turned the screen round so he could see. *December pool: Ayora's clinic. Priced 44,000. Ward meeting 27 October.*

"It'll go," he said. "It's the only thing anyone's talked about."

"It went for the roof too. Until it didn't."

The December pool was older than he was. It had grown out of the minga, the work day every household owes the parish, when every family sent someone with a shovel or a pot of food to dig a ditch or raise a wall. When the asks came, the parish had done what it already knew how to do, and made a minga of them. Everyone in Ayora held back six of their ten for December: seven thousand eight hundred adults in the parish, six each. Nobody had passed a law about it. You simply didn't spend the six, any more than you would skip the minga, and in December the parish pooled them all behind one label and bought the one big thing it couldn't buy any other way. Lamps for the plaza, in 2133. The roof. Next, the clinic: four rooms, oxygen, a doctor two days a week, a bed for a woman in labour who couldn't get to Cayambe in time. Lourdes had a drawing of it, by an architect from Ibarra, folded in the drawer under the radio.

"And Sisa will have two of her own," Lourdes said, "on the twenty-sixth."

He had forgotten, or not forgotten, but not thought. Sisa would be sixteen on the twenty-sixth of October. A person who came of age partway through the year got their share of the months that were left, which at the end of October was two.

"Two," said Sisa. "And I already know what I'm doing with them. I'm giving them to the band, so they can pay somebody to teach Papá the tuba."

"The band thanks you," Segundo said.

"The band begs."

The tuba case stood by the door where Lourdes's coat hung over it. Practice was on Thursdays. He had played in the parish band at every fiesta since he was fourteen, badly, every year a little less badly, and the bandleader had once told him, not unkindly, that he was the only tuba in the province who played every note in the right place and none of them at the right time.

After the dishes he got out the slate and wrote the label.

He had been thinking about it for a week. The night market was in a bad way. The awnings over the stalls were canvas from before Sisa was born, rotten at the seams, and the October rains were six weeks off. Half the lamps the parish had bought in 2133 had gone yellow and two had gone out. At her stall by the church steps, ten metres from her son, his mother had kept her hands in her lap.

His father had seen it. Segundo saw him see it. He saw his father's eyes go to the stall and stop, and come away, and go on smiling at everyone else.

She pushed the hair off her forehead. "Four weeks. I wanted it to be nothing, and it's nothing, and I don't like it."

The change comes to the rope, to me, as a load.

The consortium sends it in the morning: the whole of it, every piece, at once. The lowest forty kilometres of me, taken off the reel and laid down, section by section, and new sections hung in their place from batches five, six, seven and eight, in an order they have written out. That I know. Two copies of one load. I have carried that before.

## Part Four: The Price of a Question

*Received: 3 asks, returned by Arriba. Label: the dispensary's nights. Fee: none. Pooled: none.*

The dispensary's nights filled on the Monday. Six hundred and ten asks had gone in, a few at a time over three weeks, and at noon the board posted what they bought: an oxygen machine that made its own from the air and would never run out, and a nurse from Cayambe two nights a week for a year, *607*, three returned. Lourdes read it on the board on her way in from the dispensary at one and came out to his bench and stood in front of it without saying anything, and then she put her hand flat on the oak lid, once, and went back in to her queue. That was all. He sat for a while afterwards looking at the place on the lid where her hand had been.

Jacinta sent for him that night. *Come up. Bring the receipt book. I need something to write on.*

The strain readers' office was the glass box on the gallery above the reel, where he had stood with the fourth grade in September, and at ten at night it was the only lit thing in the reel house. Below it the great drum stood still in the half-dark, and the ribbon went up off it through the slot in the roof into a sky he couldn't see. The office smelled of coffee that had been on the heat since the afternoon. Jacinta had three screens on her desk and paper spread over every other surface, and the drawer where she had locked the cord was open, and the cord lay coiled on top of a stack of bulletins like a grey snake asleep.

"Sit," she said. "Look at this. Tell me I'm mad."

She gave him a printed sheet. It was the anchor's weekly test-line bulletin, which he had never once read, though he had walked past it on the board up here a hundred times. Four rows, one for each batch of the new ribbon. Two numbers a row.

*Batch 5. 0.02 / 0.08.*

*Batch 6. 0.02 / 0.08.*

*Batch 7. 0.06 / 0.04.*

*Batch 8. 0.02 / 0.08.*

"The first number is how much it rings under the cycling, above a perfect section," Jacinta said. "The second is how far that is from tolerance. Tolerance is a tenth. Everything inside a tenth passes." She tapped the third row. "Seven rings four hundredths more than the others. Every batch rings a little. Seven rings a little more."

"It's inside."

"It's inside. It's always been inside. I've posted it myself every Monday since they hung the sections in May." She pushed a stack of the bulletins across at him, twenty-two of them, every one with its corner stapled, and he leafed through. *Batch 7. 0.06 / 0.04.* May. June. July. August. The same four hundredths, week after week, on the same line. "Twenty-two weeks. I read that number every Monday and I thought, seven's a bit lively, and I posted it, and I went home. It's inside tolerance. The rope bore the plan this morning, with seven in it. It's fine."

"So why am I here?"

"Because it's the only thing that's different." She took the cord off the stack and put it on the desk between them.

He saw it before she said anything more. He had spent twenty-seven years setting what came down against what was written on a label, and this was the same work. "Four batches," he said. "One of them rings. And one of them gave away two thousand metres of itself to an awning in Ayora."

"One of the four gave it. I can't tell you which."

"Arriba chooses what it sends. It doesn't choose by accident. Why throw ribbon away now, a month before they hang it? Why to my label?"

"Because offcut gets thrown away, and you're the nearest awning." She put her finger on the third row. "You're reading it like a label. I read it like a number. But in the whole of the rope's half, every figure the rope has ever published about the new ribbon, this is the one thing that picks out one batch. So yes. Two things next to each other on a desk. That's all we've got." She sat back. "Now here's the other half. You want the other half?"

The other half, it turned out, was not on her desk, because it wasn't anyone's. The ribbon was made at the station, in Arriba's ovens, in Arriba's orbit, under Arriba's management, as everything up there was. Every batch's record lived inside Arriba: the cure temperatures, the oven logs, the hour-by-hour of every section's making. What came down the rope to the consortium's engineers was a certificate per batch, one sheet, saying the batch had passed, with its cure dates and nothing else. Jacinta had the dates in her log, one line a batch: five in December 2137, six in February, seven from the eleventh to the fourteenth of March 2138, eight in April.

"March 2138," Segundo said. "That was the storm. The sky went green over Quito three nights running, and every screen in the valley went dark."

"The solar storm. The station rode it out. The certificate says so." She tapped the line. "Passed." The rope had never seen the oven logs. The oven had never felt the rope.

"That's the Accord," Jacinta said. "They keep them apart on purpose. The rope can't see Arriba's books and Arriba can't see the rope's. Each one checks the other. If they could see each other they could agree with each other, and then who'd check?" She shrugged. "Anybody can read both. The rope's half is up here on the board, in my bulletins. Arriba's half opens to an ask, like everything of Arriba's. It's just that the rope's half is twenty-two weeks of two numbers a batch, and Arriba's half is the whole station, and nobody's job is to read them together. My job is the rope's half. I've never asked Arriba anything about the ribbon in my life. Why would I? If the ribbon were wrong, the rope would know."

She heard what she had said, and was quiet.

Then she got up and took a headset off a hook by the door. "Come and listen to it," she said. "You should hear what four hundredths sounds like."

The test shed stood across the yard from the lab, a long tin hall lit with work lamps, colder inside than out. Down the middle ran a row of steel frames, each the height of two men, and in each frame a length of the new ribbon hung between two jaws, grey and flat, a little wider than his shoulders, as the ribbon on the reel was. Painted on the floor in front of each frame was a number: *5*, *5*, *5*, *6*, *6*, *6*, *7*, *7*, *7*, *8*, *8*, *8*. Thick black cables ran from every frame into a trench in the floor, and the trench ran out under the wall toward the reel house.

"That's how the rope feels them," Jacinta said. "Wired straight in. It feels these as it feels itself."

Every forty seconds or so the jaws pulled, at the same slow pace as her rig in the lab: a long heave, the motors groaning, the ribbons in all twelve frames drawn taut together, and held, and let go. Two thousand times a day. Weather, she said. That was what the weather did to the bottom of the rope, all day, every day: pull and let go.

She put the headset on him. It was cold on his ears and smelled of someone else's hair oil. She clipped a small black disc to the edge of the first frame, a *5*, and turned a knob, and stood back.

At first there was nothing but the motors. Then the jaws heaved, and the ribbon took the pull, and he heard it: a note. Low and clean and long, like a wet finger round the rim of a glass, swelling as the pull came and dying as it let go.

"That's the ring," Jacinta said. "All ribbon does it. It's a good sound. Means it's carrying."

She moved the disc to a *6*. The same note. To an *8*. The same, clean, a little brighter maybe, or he imagined it. Then she clipped it to the first *7*, and they waited for the pull.

It came. The note came with it, the same note. And under it, so faint that at first he thought it was his own blood in his ears, something else: a second sound, not quite the same pitch, a little behind the first, as if somebody in another room were humming the same tune half a beat late. Then the pull let go and both sounds died together.

He waited for the next pull to be sure. It was there again. Under the note, the other note.

"You hear it," she said, watching his face.

"Barely."

"Barely is four hundredths. The rope hears it a great deal better than you do. It's been hearing that every forty seconds since May." She unclipped the disc. "Inside tolerance. Every batch has a little of it. Seven has a little more." She looked at the three frames. "The test pieces are always cut from the first rack in the oven, the one along the north wall. Convention. Nobody remembers why. So these are seven's north-wall end."

He took the headset off. Without it the shed was only motors and cold and the heave of the frames, and the three ribbons marked *7* looked exactly like the nine that weren't.

Back in the office she turned one of the screens round to face him. "And then I went looking," she said, "because I couldn't sleep, and I'm a strain reader, and when strain readers can't sleep we read the other tethers' logs."

There were two other tethers in the world: Gabon's, at Libreville, built in 2091, and Kalimantan's, in 2103. The three anchors shared a bulletin, monthly, in four languages, two hundred pages of test reports that nobody but strain readers ever opened. Jacinta had read every one since she was twenty-five. She had found it in the August 2140 issue, page forty-one, in French, in a table of incidents.

"Why would they? It was a clamp. And they scrapped the piece with the clamp, so nobody can ask now why it parted. Nothing happened in this country unless somebody asked. Nobody had asked about seven. Gabon hadn't, the consortium hadn't, Jacinta hadn't, in twenty-two Mondays. So nothing would happen. The reel house was very quiet. Somewhere below them in the dark a pump cycled on and off.

"So tell the consortium," Segundo said.

"Or the oven logs," he said. "Those open to an ask too. Surely that's cheaper."

And the anchor has nothing to anchor. And this valley has nothing to send up."

He picked up the receipt book she had told him to bring and opened it to a blank page and did not write anything on it.

"How much does it cost to ask about one section?" he said.

"Like we asked twenty times and Arriba said it was fine twenty times. It makes it worse." She drank her coffee. "Sampling's for a batch you can throw away. Not one you're going to hang the town on."

He wrote the label carefully, counting the words on his fingers. *Is batch seven's section forty sound?* Six. He posted it at eight, when the clerk raised the shutter, and the board priced it within the minute, which was how the board did things: *131*.

He sent it to a hundred and forty of his clients, the ones he could most easily explain nothing to, one ask each, and by eleven he had them all, and a hundred and forty yellow carbons in his bag, and his fee of seven, and a pool of a hundred and thirty-three for a question priced at a hundred and thirty-one. Nobody asked him what it was for. People he had pooled for for twenty years read the label on their screens and touched them and sent him an ask, as if he had asked them for a match. He thought of his father on the bench. He wrote the receipts anyway.

Then it took three days. An inspection always took three days, whatever was paid. A question about a section of ribbon was not a crate of lamps; something had to go and look at it, up at the station where the sections waited in their racks, and Arriba did not hurry for a hundred and thirty-one asks. He sat at his bench on the Wednesday and the Thursday and pooled for other things, a new roof for the bus shelter, the music for the procession, and every time the board updated he looked at it, and every time the row beside his label said *pooled, answering*. He slept badly. He was on the roof each morning for the six-forty, as he had been all his life, and now when the windows rattled he found he was holding his breath until the climber went into the cloud, which he had never done before, and which was stupid, because nothing on that climber was batch seven, and nothing would be until the end of the month. Sisa found him up there on the Thursday in his coat at half past six and asked if he was having some sort of crisis, and he said probably, and she went back to bed.

The answer was posted at noon on the Friday. Half the portico read it, because a question was rarer on the board than a roof, and because it was Segundo's, and Segundo didn't ask questions.

*Is batch seven's section forty sound? Pooled 133. Priced 131. Returned 2.*

*Section forty of batch seven is sound.*

That was all. He read it four times. It was exactly true and it said nothing else whatever. It didn't say what sound meant, or what the inspection found, or anything about any section but forty. It had looked at section forty, he understood, the whole of it, every fibre, whatever Arriba did when it looked at a thing, and found it sound, and said so, and stopped, as precisely as a mason stops at the edge of the wall he's paid for.

Jacinta came down at one and stood in front of the board with her hands in her fleece and read it.

"Well," she said. "Now we know it's sound."

Wilmer, from the third bench, said, "What's batch seven?" and nobody answered him.

"A hundred and thirty-one," Segundo said. "For one section."

"Three hundred and twelve sections in a batch." She didn't need the carbon for this one. She said it as she said everything at the anchor, a figure read off a dial. "Three hundred and twelve times a hundred and thirty-one."

He did it on the back of a receipt anyway, because he wanted to see it written.

*40,872.*

Under it, smaller, he wrote what the twenty sections would have cost, *2,620*, and crossed it out again.

He looked at the big number. He knew the other number by heart. Everyone in Ayora knew it; it had been on the board since January. *Ayora's clinic. Priced 44,000.* The whole parish's December was about forty-seven thousand asks, and the clinic would take forty-four of them, and there would be three thousand left over for whatever the council liked.

"Forty thousand," Jacinta said, reading it upside down. She didn't say anything else. She didn't need to.

"Then a narrower one." He was a label-maker; narrowing was the whole of the trade. "You said the test pieces were the north-wall end, and they're what rings. So ask about the north wall. *Are batch seven's north-wall sections sound?* Six words."

"Which sections are those?"

"Arriba knows."

"Arriba knows from its oven records which section sat where. That's inside the batch. To answer about the north wall it has to find which sections were at the north wall, and look at them, and to be sure it's found them all it has to look at every one." She shrugged. "Price it. I'd bet you a week's pay."

A broker could have a label priced at the counter's window before he posted it; the price came back to him on paper and went nowhere else. Posted, even for a minute at zero pool, a question about batch seven would be on every screen in the valley and in every habitat that bought its roses here. He wrote two labels on a slip, the plain question and the north wall, folded it and passed it through the window. The clerk read them, looked at him over her glasses, keyed them in without a word, wrote two numbers on the back of the slip and passed it back.

*40,872.* *40,872.*

"Same," Jacinta said. "It's the whole inspection either way."

He sat for a long time with the slip in his hand. Then, because he couldn't think what else to do with his face, he picked up the slate and, very quickly, before he could stop himself, posted a third label in public at zero pool, the one it was safe to post because somebody posted it every year as a joke. *What aren't you telling us?*

The board priced it in under a minute, as it priced everything, and the price went right across the column into the next one.

*8,960,000,000.*

The clerk, behind her glass, said without looking up, "Somebody does that every year." She reached over and pulled it off the board.

And Segundo laughed. He couldn't help it. It came up out of him in the middle of the portico, at his own bench, with Jacinta beside him and Wilmer staring, a great helpless bark of a laugh at the size of the number, at the thought of a mind that would tell you everything it knew if you paid it a year of the world's asks, and that would tell you, for a hundred and thirty-one, that one section in three hundred and twelve was sound. He laughed until his eyes ran and he had to sit down on the stool. Jacinta didn't laugh. She waited until he'd finished, and then she picked up the receipt with *40,872* on it and folded it and put it in his top pocket, and patted it.

*Received: 1 ask, from Rosaura Quishpe. Label: the procession's music. Fee: 0.05. Pooled: 0.95.*

Luz Caiza was at his bench at seven on the Tuesday, before the counter opened, in her farm fleece with the hood up against the drizzle. She had been one of the hundred and forty. She had her receipt for section forty in her hand.

"They asked us in the shed," she said. "The supervisor. Yesterday, at the break. Who'd been asking Arriba about the rope. Everybody looked at each other." She turned the carbon over in her fingers. "I didn't say anything."

"I'm sorry. I should have thought."

"What was it for?"

He had known someone would ask. He had thought it would be Wilmer or Lourdes. He had not thought it would be a nineteen-year-old from Tabacundo with sap on her fingers, standing in the rain to ask him before her shift.

"I can't tell you yet," he said.

She looked at him for a moment. Then she nodded, as if that were an answer, and folded the receipt and put it back in the pocket she kept it in, and zipped the pocket.

"All right," she said. "I don't need to know. You wrote it down." And she went off across the plaza to the bus for the farm, and he sat with that for a long time, because it was the kindest thing anyone had said to him that month and he didn't want it.

Proaño came to the portico later that morning, on foot, in the poncho and the muddy boots, to post his December labels through Wilmer. He was in a fine mood. He had read the board.

"Section forty," he said, sitting down on the end of Segundo's bench without being asked, as if they did this every week. "Sound. My agent in the habitats rang me on Saturday to congratulate me on section forty. Somebody up there reads the Cayambe board, apparently. You've done my freight rate a favour."

"I didn't ask it for your freight rate."

"No. I know you didn't." Proaño looked at him with real interest. "That's what I came over to ask. What did you ask it for? You've never posted a question in twenty-seven years. My manager told me. He reads the board like a hawk." He waited. "Some reason of your cousin's at the anchor?"

Segundo had decided, at three that morning, to say nothing about batch seven. He held to it. "Suppose," he said, "someone wanted to know about all of it. Not one section. A whole batch of the new ribbon. Every section. What would happen?"

He watched Proaño understand. It took no time at all. Proaño was a man who had spent thirty years in a chamber where everything said was about something else, and he heard the question under the question at once, and Segundo saw him hear it: the easy face going still, the eyes going to the board and back.

"Which batch?" Proaño said quietly.

"Any batch."

"Seven, then. Since it's the one you've been asking about." Proaño leaned forward with his elbows on his knees and his hat between his hands, and spoke low, so that Wilmer on the next bench would have to lean to hear, and Wilmer leaned. "I'll tell you what would happen. The question would go on the board. *Is batch seven sound?* That's the day it goes on. The answer comes, what, two days later, three? In those three days every buyer in the habitats reads the Cayambe board, because their agents read it for them, and they see a question about the ribbon my roses ride on. They don't wait for the answer. Why would they? A question is a smell. They rewrite every contract I have at half, or they cancel, and they don't come back when the answer says sound, because by then they've bought their roses in Kenya. Whatever the answer is. The question does it." He spread his hands. "So nobody asks. Not me. Not the consortium, God knows; they'd be asking whether their own product is any good, in public, a month before they hang it."

"You could afford it."

"Of course I could afford it. Not my own workers; the association. Forty export firms, their workers and their workers' households, three provinces, all pooling behind the association's labels. We could ask about all four batches and the oven and the station's lunch, every week of the year." He smiled, but the smile was thin. "And I'd be a fool to. I'd sink myself asking. And for what? The rope bore the plan. The rope's never wrong."

Segundo said nothing.

Proaño looked at him for a long moment. Something moved in his face that might have been doubt, and was put away.

"You know what's funny," he said. "I'll tell you what's funny. We pay the maker to check the maker's work. The consortium buys the ribbon from Arriba's ovens, and the only thing on God's earth that could tell us whether the ribbon's any good is Arriba. And now the maker's customers will pay too. Anyone who wants to know. A hundred and thirty-one a section. It's on the board." He stood, and put his hat on. "You've shown everyone the price of a straight question, Farinango. I can pay it every week. Ayora can pay it once a year."

He went back to Wilmer's bench and sat, and posted his December labels, three of them, and laughed at something Wilmer said, loudly, so that the whole portico could hear him laughing. He didn't look at the second bench again.

When he had gone Wilmer came over, with the peanuts, and sat where Proaño had sat.

"I've never seen him go white," Wilmer said. "Not once. I've posted for him all year. Somebody's goats ate half a shed of his roses in July and he laughed about it for a week." He ate a peanut. "What's batch seven?"

"Part of the new ribbon."

"I know that. I can read. What's wrong with it?"

"I don't know that anything is."

Wilmer looked at him with his head on one side. Wilmer was no fool. He had decided early that cleverness was better spent on selling than on worrying, which is not the same thing.

"Whatever it is," he said, "you'll need asks for it. That's the only thing anybody ever needs." He brushed salt off his fingers. "I've got sixteen hundred people, and four histories I'm renting, and every one of them thinks I can get them a fridge. When you need asks, you come to me first. Not because I like you. Because I want to be in the room."

"There isn't a room."

"There's always a room," said Wilmer, and went back to his bench.

Segundo sat on his stool with *40,872* in his top pocket and understood that he was alone with it. Jacinta had her half. Proaño had his reasons. The consortium had its rule. The rope had borne the plan. Arriba would say nothing it wasn't paid to say. *The one thing in this country I've never had to check.* That was the whole of it, he thought. Everybody trusted the rope to refuse anything unsound, and so nobody ever asked about what the rope couldn't feel. And the only people in the world who could pay were the ones who would lose most by asking, and the ones who could least afford to.

At the end of the bench, Señora Quishpe was waiting with her screen out to put an ask into the music for the procession, and he wrote her a receipt.

*Received: 1 ask, from Lourdes Imbaquingo. Label: the procession's music. Fee: 0.05. Pooled: 0.95.*

On the Thursday he went to band practice, because he had missed the last one and the bandleader had begun to say things, and played badly for two hours in the parish hall, and at ten he walked up the hill to the anchor in the dark because Jacinta had said she'd be at the fence.

She was parked in the lay-by below the gate, where the bus turned, with the engine off. Her boys were asleep in the back seat under a blanket, one with his mouth open, both with their climbing shoes still on, and she was sitting on the bonnet with her knees up and a flask. He climbed up beside her. The metal was still faintly warm.

The fence ran away from them in both directions, chain link three metres high with wire on top, round the whole shelf of the anchor. Beyond it the yards were floodlit and empty. The reel house stood black. And above the reel house, if you knew where to look, there was the ribbon.

You couldn't see it. You never could, at night. What you could see were the climbers. They went up from the reel house roof in a dotted line, each with its running lights, red and white, the nearest one a cluster of lights a few kilometres up, bright as a plane, the next fainter, the next fainter still, a line of beads going straight up into the black and getting smaller and closer together, until somewhere far above they ran into the stars and he couldn't tell which were climbers and which were not. Nineteen of them, on the way up. He had known that his whole life. He had never counted them before. He tried, and lost count at eleven.

And at the top, straight overhead, so high that he had to lie back on the windscreen to see it, there was one star that was not like the others. It didn't twinkle. It didn't move. It never moved, and never set, and every child in Ayora was shown it as soon as they could point: the station, thirty-six thousand kilometres up, hanging over the mountain. Past it, out of sight, the counterweight.

He lay on the windscreen and looked at it. Thirty-six thousand kilometres. Nineteen days on a bus. He put his hand up and held it at arm's length against the sky, and the whole line from the reel house to the station fitted behind his little finger, and all of it was held up by a band thinner than his fingernail that he had tied round an awning with a bunch knot.

"It's the bottom that holds the rest," Jacinta said, beside him. She was looking up too. "People think it's the top. It's the bottom." And after a moment: "Eleven days. They splice seven in on the thirtieth."

He took the receipt out of his pocket, the one with *40,872* on it, and turned it over, and in the light from her screen he wrote down the parish on the back of it. His own clients, two thousand one hundred, six each held for December: twelve thousand six hundred. Wilmer's sixteen hundred: nine thousand six hundred. The three other brokers, three thousand between them: eighteen thousand. The ones with no broker, who pooled through the council, eleven hundred: six thousand six hundred. He added it up.

*46,800.*

"About forty-seven thousand," he said. "That's everything Ayora's got for December. The clinic's forty-four."

"And the question's forty thousand eight hundred and seventy-two."

"So it's one or the other. Not both." He looked at the figures. "And not even the question, unless nearly everyone gives."

Jacinta didn't say anything.

"I can't tell them why," he said. "I can't stand up in the parish hall and say the new rope is bad. I don't know it's bad. We've got a ring inside tolerance and a clamp in Gabon and some awning cord. If I say it and I'm wrong, I've sunk every contract in this valley for nothing and nobody will ever give me an ask again as long as I live. And if I say it and I'm right, I've sunk them anyway, before the answer comes. Proaño told me. The question does it." He turned the receipt over and over. "So I'd have to ask them for their December asks and not tell them what for. That's the price, Jacinta. Their December. All of it, the clinic's asks, every one. And I can't tell them what it's for until it's answered."

"On your word."

"On my word."

She drank from the flask and handed it to him. It was coffee with something in it.

"If I ask," Segundo said, "the clinic's gone, and they hang me if I'm wrong. If I don't, the rope comes down in three years with somebody's boys on the climber."

In the back seat one of her boys turned over in his sleep and said something about a rocket.

Jacinta looked at them through the windscreen for a while.

"Yes," she said.

He went home at midnight. Lourdes was at the kitchen table with the architect's drawing of the clinic unfolded in front of her, flattened at the corners with the salt and a cup and two oranges. She had pencilled things on it: where the oxygen machine would go now that they had one, a second sink. She looked up when he came in.

"You weren't at practice all this time."

"I went up to the anchor. Jacinta."

She looked at him. She had been looking at people across a dispensary table for twenty-five years, and she could tell a man who had cut himself from a man who was going to say he'd cut himself, and she looked at him that way now.

"What's going on with you and Jacinta?"

"Work."

"You don't work at the anchor."

"Her work. She wanted somebody to talk to."

Lourdes put her pencil down. "You've had a face on you for two weeks. You posted a question on the board. You've never posted a question in your life. Wilmer asked me in the queue what batch seven is, and I said I had no idea, and he looked at me as if I were lying for you." She waited. "Am I?"

He stood in the kitchen doorway with the receipt in his pocket. He could tell her. She would understand it faster than he had. She would understand all of it, the ring and the cord and the forty thousand and what forty thousand was, and she would look down at the drawing under her hands.

"It's nothing yet," he said. "When it's something, I'll tell you."

She looked at him for a long time. Then she picked up her pencil again and bent over the drawing, and drew a line he couldn't see from the door, and said, without looking up, "Your soup's on the stove."

He ate it standing up. She didn't speak to him again that night, and he lay awake beside her until the windows rattled at twenty to seven.

## Part Five: The Slack Season

*Received: 2 asks, by Sisa Farinango, on her sixteenth birthday. Label: her own. Fee: none. Pooled: none.*

Her asks came at midnight on Thursday the twenty-sixth of October, as everyone's did on their birthday, and Sisa was awake for them. Segundo heard her through the wall: the chime of her screen, and then a silence, and then her laugh, short and private, the laugh of someone who had been told about a thing all her life and found it smaller than she'd thought. Two. He lay in the dark and listened to her turn the screen over and over on the blanket.

In the morning he gave her a receipt book. It was a new one from the box of twelve, carbon sheets and all, and on the first page he had written a receipt in his own hand, in pencil, pressing hard: *Received: 2 asks, by Sisa Farinango, on her sixteenth birthday. Label: her own. Fee: none. Pooled: none.* She looked at it for a long time at the kitchen table with her coffee going cold, and he couldn't tell whether she was going to laugh at him or throw it at him.

"This is the most embarrassing present anyone has ever given anyone," she said finally, and put it in her school bag, and did not take it out again in front of him.

Thursday was band night. He didn't go. The bandleader sent a message that said only *tomorrow, then, at six, and bring the mouthpiece this time*, because the parish had asked the band to play the procession on the second, and the procession needed a rehearsal on Friday as well, and the bandleader had said so with the air of a man arranging his own funeral. Segundo spent the evening making a cake with Sisa from a recipe of his mother's, badly, while Lourdes worked late at the dispensary with the new oxygen machine humming in the corner of the second room, and at eight they ate the cake, the three of them and Dolores, who had come up the hill with a bag of toasted maize as a present and a record of the old slow guitar songs that she said Sisa would understand when she was older.

He told them after the cake.

He hadn't meant to. He had meant to tell no one until he was standing on the bench, because a thing said in a kitchen gets said in the queue at the bakery by nine the next morning. But Lourdes was across the table from him with her hands round her cup, and she had been looking at him that way for a week, and he found he could not let her walk into the meeting tomorrow and hear it with everyone else.

"Tomorrow at the ward meeting," he said, "I'm going to ask for the December asks."

Lourdes put her cup down.

"For the clinic," said Sisa, not understanding.

"For something else."

The kitchen was very quiet. On the shelf the radio was playing something with trumpets, very low.

"For what?" Lourdes said.

"A question. For Arriba."

"What question?"

"I can't tell you."

He watched her take it in. He watched her understand that the batch seven Wilmer had asked about in her queue, and the face he'd had on him for two weeks, and the night at the anchor, and the question on the board in October that he had never explained, were all one thing, and that the one thing was going to cost her the clinic, and that he was not going to tell her what it was.

"You can't tell me," she said.

"Not before it's answered. If I say it and I'm wrong it does harm that doesn't come back. If I say it and I'm right it does the same harm, before anything can be done."

"So you'll ask seven thousand people to give up the clinic for a reason you won't tell them." Her voice was perfectly level. "And you'll ask me."

"Yes."

"On your word."

"On my word."

She looked at him as she looked at a man in the dispensary who said the pain was nothing. Then she got up and took her cup to the sink and washed it, carefully, and dried it, and put it on the shelf, and went upstairs without saying anything else, and he heard the bedroom door close, quietly, which was worse than a slam.

Dolores ate a piece of cake.

"Your father," she said, "would have told her it was a road."

Sisa was looking at the stairs. She was sixteen and had been for nineteen hours and had two asks of her own on the screen in her pocket. She took the screen out and looked at it.

"I'll give you mine," she said.

"You don't know what it's for."

"I know." She put the screen away again and stood up. "But Mamá will hate it," she said, and went up after her mother, and he heard her knock.

He sat in the kitchen with the cake and his mother and thought about tomorrow. Whatever he was given tomorrow night he would take without a receipt, because there would be nothing yet to write on one but his name.

On Friday the twenty-seventh of October the parish hall was too small by six o'clock, and by half past it was clear to everyone that it would not do. The council released the parish's December asks only at its ward meeting, and it had posted the date in September; there was no other night of the year on which he could have asked for them. The December meeting was always full, because December was the minga of asks and nobody skipped the minga, but this year it was fuller, because the story had gone round the bakery by nine that morning exactly as he had known it would: Farinango was going to ask for December, and Farinango wouldn't say what for. People came who hadn't been to a ward meeting in ten years. They came with children, with grandparents in wheelchairs, with flasks. The band was still in the hall when the first of them arrived, rehearsing the procession, and had to stop in the middle of the slow march, and stood about with their instruments while the chairs filled and the aisles filled and people stood on the windowsills.

The council's chairwoman, who ran a shop that sold seed and fertiliser on the Cayambe road and had chaired the December meeting for eleven years, stood on the platform and looked at the hall and the doorway and the faces beyond the doorway out in the dark, and said into the microphone that they would hold it under the portico, where everyone could see the board. Nobody argued. The whole meeting picked itself up and went out across the plaza in the cold, under the lamps that the market had bought in September, and the band came too, because they didn't know what else to do, and carried their instruments with them. She was behind her glass in her hair net with the board lit up beside her, and when the chairwoman asked whether they could post a pool count tonight, before a label, she said that a broker could hold asks pending a label for a day under the Accord, that she had read the rule that afternoon, and that she had already set up the row.

The chairwoman read out the December label, as she did every year: *Ayora's clinic. Priced 44,000.* She read out what the parish held, as near as the council could say: forty-seven thousand, give or take. Then she said that Segundo Farinango had asked to speak, and stood aside.

He got up on the second bench.

He had stood on it before, to read a board aloud, to see over a crowd. He had never stood on it to ask for anything. The oak gave a little under his boots, as it always did, at the place where the lid was hinged. From up there he could see the whole of the portico and the plaza beyond it, full of people in coats and hats and shawls, faces turned up to him under the white lamps, children on shoulders, the band in a cluster by the church steps with their instruments catching the light, and his mother's stall shuttered by the steps, and his mother in her chair in front of it with a blanket over her knees, because she had brought her chair across the plaza to sit in.

He had thought all day about what to say and had decided on very little.

"I'm going to ask you for your December asks," he said. His voice came out smaller than he'd meant it to, and somebody at the back said *louder*, and he said it again, louder. "For a question. I'm not going to write you receipts, because I can't write you a receipt for something I won't tell you. I'm going to ask you to give them to me on my word."

And I can't tell you what it's for until it's answered. I know who wanted the clinic." He didn't look at Lourdes. He knew exactly where she was standing, by the pillar at the end of the portico, with her arms folded inside her coat. "I wouldn't ask if I thought there was another way. There isn't. I've looked."

He stopped. There was something else he had meant to say and he found he was going to say it whether he meant to or not.

"Thirty-one years ago my father stood on this bench," he said, "and asked you for your December. On his word. Some of you were here. I was there." He nodded at the tuba against the wall. Every one of you who's my client has a drawer full of them." He looked down at the crowd. "I'm asking the way he asked. I know what that sounds like. I'm asking anyway."

For a long moment nobody moved. Then his mother spoke from her chair, not loudly, but in the voice she used for the price of beans, so that everyone under the portico heard her.

"No," Dolores said.

Heads turned.

"I've never asked that machine anything in my life," she said. "Not one question. It told us something once, when nobody asked, and I bought bread with the same coins for a third as much. Batch eight had been cured in April 2138, in the same oven, in the same season, a few weeks after the storm. It rang true. Every Monday since May it had rung true. Arriba had answered exactly what it was asked, and it had been asked about batch seven, and it had said nothing whatever about eight, because nobody had paid it to. Eight was going into the rope higher up, in the new plan, where the load was less. Eight was probably fine. It rang true.

He thought about what it would cost to ask. Three hundred and twelve sections, at a hundred and thirty-one asks a section. He didn't need the back of a receipt. Forty thousand eight hundred and seventy-two. The parish had spent its December. In January it would have its ten each again, and its six for December, and a clinic to buy that it no longer needed, and a question nobody would want to pay for twice, about a batch that rang true. Proaño's association could pay for it every week. Proaño would not ask it, for the same reason he hadn't asked about seven.

Nobody was going to ask.

"Farinango," said the clerk, through her glass. She had her stylus ready. She had been waiting for some time. "January. What label do I write?"

Ayora counter, broker 0419, Wednesday 1 November. Label: none yet, a pool opened for January. Price: none. Pooled: nothing.

I read the broker. I read him one way for twenty-seven years and revised the reading on 28 October. This morning he wrote a label for the farms. I read him now as two histories: the one that spent a parish's December on a plain question, and the one that writes the grower's clinic. His January pool, when it has a label, will be read in the light of both. A pool with no label asks me nothing, and I price nothing.

It is the first of November, by the consortium's date, and the lowest forty kilometres of the rope, of me, are slack.

They let the tension off at midnight, slowly, a little at a time, and I felt it go. First the reel paying out, and the pull at my foot easing, and easing, and then the place where the pull had always been was empty. Above it I am stretched as I have always been, up through the cloud and the air and the cold to the station and past it, where the counterweight pulls outward and always will. But the bottom of me hangs loose from the reel and carries nothing. Nothing climbs it. Nothing comes down it. No climber, no law, no load.

I have never felt this. I do not know what to call it, and I have no wear for it yet. When it is over I will remember it by whatever it leaves in me, and I don't know what it will leave.

The town is still there. I feel it as I always have, in small ways, through numbers: the brokers posting labels, the Asamblea passing laws, the farms pooling for a clinic, four-one-nine among them at his angle. Every load the town makes this morning stops at the foot of me, where I am slack, and waits. For forty days I don't have to carry any of it.

In the shed the test line is still. They stopped it at midnight with everything else. For forty days nothing rings.
