# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A small-town broker in Ecuador pools his neighbours' yearly "asks" to get things from a mind in orbit. He finds out that a batch of the replacement ribbon for the space-elevator rope is flawed. Nobody will ask about it, because asking is costly and a question alone can ruin the growers. He spends the whole parish's December on one plain question, which saves the rope and costs him the clinic his wife wanted and his own reputation as the honest one.

2. He stood to lose his clients' trust, his reputation as the honest broker who writes receipts, his wife's goodwill, and the clinic, if he asked. He also risked being wrong and wrecking every contract in the valley for nothing. If he failed to ask, or if the batch wasn't bad, the first sections would go into the bottom of the rope. In two or three years they would part, the climbers on that length would come down, and the valley would have nothing to send up. The anchor would have nothing to anchor. He asked, was right, and lost most of what he stood to lose anyway: the clinic and his standing with Lourdes.

3. After the 2097 crisis, the powers split the work in two. Arriba can only answer what is asked and may never lie. The rope can only refuse what it can't bear, and every law passes through it. The aim was that no one would hold both halves again. The Accord (Quito, 2104) kept Arriba from speaking unasked, because in 2098 it warned of a Sahel famine and the people who heard first bought up the grain. Everyone also keeps the two apart as checks on each other. The cost of that design is that nobody checks what neither of them can feel. The rope has no view of Arriba's oven logs, and Arriba says nothing unasked. Asking costs a lot, so the rich can afford the questions that would hurt them least to ask, and the poor can't afford any.

4. Late on, I learned that Arriba made the ribbon in its own ovens, during the solar storm of March 2138. It had apparently known something was wrong and couldn't say so. The grey cord sent to Segundo as "surplus" was, it seems, as close to telling him as it could get. That recoloured Arriba's market section, which had puzzled me, and the rope's "ring" at seven. I also learned late that the rope can only weigh what it is told. It never felt the flaw in seven. It refused the climb only because the consortium cut the rating by a number. Finally, Dolores's story and the fiesta scene recast Segundo's receipts as a response to his father's method of asking, not just a habit.

5. Segundo asked for the parish's December on his word. The parish gave about 42,300 asks. He waived his fee, and the question went up as "Is batch seven sound?" Arriba inspected all 312 sections and answered that it was not sound: 35 sections cured near the north wall had voids that open after about two million cycles. The consortium cut seven's rating, so the rope refused the proof climb. Seven came out, and a new plan went in at half load for two years. The slack season began, with no climbers and the first laws passed with nothing to weigh. Proaño, who had lost half his contracts, offered to fund the clinic through the association, and asked Segundo to write the label. Segundo wrote "Ayora's clinic, from the farms." Lourdes was hurt but gave her asks anyway and stayed. Nobody is going to ask about batch eight. The rope is slack at the bottom for the first time.

6. Characters:
   - **Segundo Farinango:** the broker at the second bench, licence 0419, careful and honest, keeps receipts. The protagonist.
   - **Lourdes Imbaquingo:** his wife, the dispensary nurse, who wants the clinic.
   - **Sisa:** their daughter, turns sixteen on 26 October.
   - **Dolores Farinango:** Segundo's mother, sells lupin beans, distrusts Arriba and trusts the rope, has a bad right arm from carrying water.
   - **Ernesto Farinango:** his father, 81, in the parish home. A former fixer who cashed both payslips and took the December asks for Proaño's road in 2110.
   - **Jacinta Ulcuango:** his cousin, a strain reader at the anchor, who found the batch problem and was training to splice the new ribbon.
   - **Wilmer Cuzco:** a younger, charming rival broker (1172) who takes favours and rents other brokers' names.
   - **Gustavo Proaño:** the big rose grower and Asamblea member, whose firms ride the rope.
   - **Luz Caiza:** a nineteen-year-old cutter from Tabacundo who becomes Segundo's client and trusts him.
   - **Señora Rosaura Quishpe:** an old cheese seller, owed money by half the parish, the first to give.
   - **The clerk:** runs the counter's board and sets up the pool.
   - **The pork man's wife, the baker, the oldest cheese woman, the Pilataxis (the farm's foreman and family), the fifth-bench widower, the bandleader:** townspeople.
   - **The crew chief and the certifier:** the chief rides the six-forty; the certifier rebuilds the plan.
   - **Arriba:** the mind at the top, which answers what it's asked.
   - **Waska:** the rope's own name, a mind that can only say no.

7. What I'm still unsure about:
   - Whether Arriba deliberately sent the cord to warn him, or whether I've read my hope into it.
   - Why Arriba decides, as it does, to read Segundo's two-word label as the auction rather than the night market.
   - What happens on 10 December when the rope bears again and has to weigh the freight relief and the other laws passed in the slack.
   - Whether batch eight is really fine.
   - Why the rope holds Segundo's heavier loads for two weeks, and whether the rope is wary of him or of something else.
   - Whether Lourdes forgives him.
   - How the rope and the Asamblea got to the exact arithmetic of 1.04.

8. Terms:
   - **"ask":** I got it from the first Luz scene, where Segundo explains it, a yearly allotment of ten.
   - **"button"/"label"/"pool":** same scene.
   - **"Arriba":** from the clerk's line, and from the clapping rhyme.
   - **"Waska":** from the fourth-grade tour ("the old word for rope").
   - **"anchor", "reel", "climber", "tether":** from the tour and the description of the reel house.
   - **"strain reader":** I got it from Jacinta's explanation to the children, "we turn the rope's numbers into Spanish."
   - **"the Accord":** from Arriba's section, and again from Ernesto.
   - **"minga":** from Segundo's history of the December pool.
   - **"offcut", "batch", "cure":** from Jacinta in the lab.
   - **"derate", "map":** I got these roughly from the rules framed on the lab wall and Jacinta's later explanation.
   - **"tolerance":** from the bulletin explanation.
   - **"dispensed"/"dispensation":** only from Arriba's notes. I understood it as "handed out", and I'm not sure of the legal sense.
   - **"slack season":** from Proaño's coffee talk.

9. For the valley, the change is that the rope's bottom forty kilometres were nearly rebuilt from flawed stock. The fix cuts freight by half for two years, so fewer roses go up. That means less money and fewer jobs, and it means no clinic from the parish's own December. For the habitats and the other towers, Segundo's price also shows what a straight question costs: forty thousand asks, and the bill for checking everything is out of reach for ordinary towns. Only the firms can afford to ask about their own interests, and they have reason not to. The slack season shows the other side. For forty days the Asamblea can pass laws nobody weighs, and Proaño uses it for his own freight relief. So the broader change is that trust has become something that can be spent and sold. A good name now fetches a price, and the safety the rope provides depends on someone being willing to pay to ask.
