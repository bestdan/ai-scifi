# First Reader report: "What the Rope Will Bear", draft 2

I read it once, straight through, and stopped at the end of the story. I wrote this from what I remember of that read.

## Where I got lost

1. **The little "Received: 1 ask, from Rosaura Quishpe. Label: our children's mornings…" lines at the very top and before each section.** I didn't know what they were. I guessed they were receipts, because Segundo writes receipts a few pages later. I was fairly sure by the time he writes Señora Quishpe's. I worked it out from the scene, and I wasn't sure at first that the line belonged to the scene that followed.
2. **"He called up the button history…" (the Señora Quishpe scene, about a third of the way down the first section).** I didn't know what a "button" was. The first section is titled "The Button." I took it to mean the record of past asks, and the story never told me. I never got a definite answer.
3. **The first section told in the first person ("Ninety tonnes leave the reel and I take them, because I am the rope they climb").** At first I didn't know who was talking. Several paragraphs in I worked out it was the rope itself. The decimals above it (0.62 / 0.38) meant nothing to me until the school-tour scene, when the cousin read them off the board. Until then I only half followed the fractions.
4. **The rope's long paragraph about "four-one-nine."** I was lost for a moment about who four-one-nine was. I worked it out when it said "a broker's licence number," and the earlier card said LICENCE 0419. Then I was lost about why the rope distrusted him. It says his loads arrive "at an angle" to the others. I understood that it suspected him of hiding something, but I never quite felt why the rope's suspicion mattered. I worked out later that this was the reason his big orders were always held for two weeks.
5. **The second first-person voice ("I am the mind at the top of the cable, the one the town calls Arriba").** It took me a moment to see this was a different voice from the rope's. It was the voice that answers questions, and the rope was the one that says no. I sorted it out partly from that sentence and partly from the cousin's and Segundo's speeches on the tour.
6. **The Arriba reasoning about the label "the market."** This is the paragraph where it decides to send awnings, lamps and a cold store, plus the cord. I followed that it read the label as the auction's market. I did not follow why it sent two thousand metres of "offcut." The line "In the anchor town, people know ribbon when they hold it" stopped me. I couldn't tell who was meant to recognise it, or whether Arriba was doing this on purpose. I still don't know whether the cord was a hint or a disposal.
7. **The years.** 2068, 2097, 2098, 2101, 2104 and 2110 all come at me, and I couldn't keep straight which came before which. The plaque says 2068. The two budgets are 2097. The referendum is 2101. The Accord is 2104, and the famine is 2098. Dolores's and Ernesto's tellings made most of it clear. I never held it all in order. I stopped trying and took it as "a long time ago, the country had a bad time and divided the power."
8. **Why the rope's ruling at the end made sense.** Jacinta's explanation ("ninety-two parts of a smaller number") cleared it up for me, but only after the rope itself had said "I find I have weighed something that I did not feel." In the moment I didn't know what had changed. I followed after her explanation.
9. **The blank receipts in Part Five ("Received: . Label: . Fee: . Pooled: .").** I first thought something had been left out. By the second one I decided they were receipts he couldn't write, because there was no label yet. That felt right once I'd decided it, but I wasn't told.
10. **The ending: "Ayora's clinic, from the farms."** I followed the words. I wasn't sure at the time how bad it was for him to write it, or whether it was a trap Proaño had set. Wilmer's last line ("Now it's read you writing for the farms") helped, but I was still unsure what Arriba would do with that.

## Where I got bored

1. **The fourth-grade tour of the anchor (from "At eleven Segundo left the bench…" to the bus ride home).** By then I had been told twice how the asks, the brokers and the rope worked. Segundo explained it to Luz, and then the rope's own section laid out the town. I was waiting for the story to move, and instead the cousin read out numbers and the children asked questions. The string-that-won't-push bit was nice. The explaining of the whole country in two sentences ("One of them can only say no…") I had more or less already understood. I was waiting for the thing I'd been promised, which was the ring.
2. **The "Dispensed: frames and canvas for forty awnings, 540. Lamps, sixty, 300…" lists.** I read the first one. The later ones I skimmed.
3. **The middle of Part Two, the Wilmer co-signing business and the fifth bench broker.** I understood it and I liked Wilmer, but I was waiting for the cord to turn into something. It felt like a lot of broker politics.
4. **The sampling arithmetic at the bench (one in ten, twenty sections, the crossed-out box).** I followed it. I was waiting for him to post the question.
5. **The rope's sections in Part Three and the start of Part Four, "I weigh the change as though it might be two."** I wanted to get back to the people.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **The opening, the cold bench with the padlock and the card on the nail: "RECEIPTS GIVEN."** "The last two words were his own" made me like him straight away. The baker with his flour answer ("It went and looked at the flour there was") made me laugh.
- **The clapping rhyme and the girls crossing hands.** It made the country feel real.
- **The auction awnings arriving four metres wide for two-metre stalls.** I laughed. I loved that sixteen of them ended up over the night market and that "most of them had been sharing everything else with their neighbours for thirty years."
- **Wilmer asking for a receipt for his ask: "For your wife's oxygen. I want a receipt."** I didn't expect it, and it made me like him a lot.
- **Dolores and the two payslips.** This is the passage that got me. "A woman who cashes both is a thief. A woman who cashes neither is a fool. And I had a boy." Then "the rope was the only one that said no. So we gave it everything" in the same voice as the price of beans.
- **Ernesto under the lemon tree, and the water Dolores carried, her right arm that stops at the ear.** The turn where Segundo says "Mamá carried it" hit me hard. So did Ernesto's reply, "Her shoulder. Yes."
- **The fiesta memory: all the hands going up, and Dolores keeping hers in her lap, and Ernesto seeing it.** The line "the thing he had sworn against was a method, and that it worked" is the best thing in the story for me.
- **"What aren't you telling us?" priced at 8,960,000,000.** I laughed out loud, and so did he. Then the clerk says, "Somebody does that every year."
- **Luz Caiza: "I don't need to know. You wrote it down."** I wasn't expecting kindness from her, and I felt it.
- **The meeting at the portico.** Dolores's "No." Lourdes touching her screen six times without looking at him. Sisa's "Two," very loud. The count going up while everyone watches who touches their screen. The tuba against the wall like "a great brass ear." And the stunned feeling that he was glad to be right. I was moved there more than at any other place.
- **The board at four in the morning, Lourdes reading it with her lips moving, Dolores bringing the cup.** I felt that.
- **The windows not rattling.** It worked very well on me, both the first morning of the refusal and the first morning of the slack.
- **The rope refusing the climber: "I find I have weighed something that I did not feel."** That was the one place in the rope's sections where I felt something for the rope. I did not expect to care about it, and I did.
- **The end of the story with the unsolved row ("Batch eight… ringing true") and "Nobody was going to ask."** It stuck in me.

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared about **Segundo**, then **Dolores, his mother**, most of all. I cared about **Lourdes** and **Sisa**, and about **Jacinta**, whom I liked right away. I liked **Wilmer** much more than I expected to. **Luz** I cared about the way you care about someone who pays for it. And I ended up caring about **the rope**, which I did not expect to do when I first met it.

I'd have kept reading past page 2. Page 2 for me was the bakery, the clapping game and the first customers. By then I was already in, mostly because of the receipts line, the baker's flour and the old woman with the seed packet. I'd have been less sure at the first rope section, where I didn't know who was talking and the numbers meant nothing to me. I'd have kept going because I wanted to know what Segundo's careful face was hiding, and I did. If I'd had to give up anywhere, it would have been the school tour, and only because I'd been told the same thing already.

## The question I'm left with

Did Arriba send the cord on purpose, as a way of telling them without telling them? It answers only what is asked, but it chose the label, the offcut and the awnings, and then it said "I revise it" about Segundo. I can't tell whether it was warning anyone or just being tidy. And I can't tell whether it is sorry about Lourdes's clinic. Behind that: what happens in January, with batch eight "ringing true" and nobody willing to pay to ask?
