# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A small-town broker named Segundo Farinango pools his neighbours' yearly "asks" so they can buy things from a distant mind called Arriba. Over several weeks he works out that the new rope ribbon being hung at the bottom of the space tether is flawed, and he spends the whole parish's December pool on a plain question to prove it. The answer saves the crews and the tether but costs the town its clinic, half its freight for two years, and his standing as the plainly honest man.

2. He stands to gain a flawless record and his family's trust. He stands to lose the clinic his wife Lourdes has wanted for years, his good name if he turns out to be wrong, and her goodwill. He asks for the December asks without being able to say what they are for. If he fails to ask, batch seven goes into the foot of the rope, and in two to three years the bottom of the tether parts. The crews on that length die, and the valley loses the thing that keeps it alive.

3. Quito's Accord (2104) and the earlier referendum split the power on purpose. The rope (Waska) can only refuse, and the mind at the top (Arriba) can only answer what it is asked and has to be paid for each answer. This followed the Sahel famine, when Arriba spoke unasked and the grain was bought up, and the double-budget year of 2097, when every slot was sold twice. The aim was that nobody could hold "the whole knife." A side effect is that the rope is trusted to catch everything. Nobody pays to ask the one thing the rope can't feel.

4. Late things that changed my view:
   - The grey cord that Segundo tied round awnings was actually the new ribbon, and the "surplus" label was not innocent.
   - The test pieces were always cut from the north-wall end, so the ring everyone heard on the test line was the bad part of the batch.
   - A reference piece of batch seven had already parted in Gabon, and was written off as a clamp fault.
   - The rope's belief "if something were wrong I would feel it" turned out to be false. It was only reading numbers on a certificate.
   - His father's fiesta trick (asking on his face and word) was something Segundo ends up doing himself, after a lifetime of defining himself against it.

5. The asks are pooled (42,300, with Segundo waiving his fee). Arriba inspects all 312 sections and answers that batch seven is not sound, and names the 35 bad sections, which cluster in six runs against the oven's north wall. The consortium lowers batch seven's rating, so the proof climb comes out at 1.04 and the rope refuses it. Batch seven is pulled out, the 277 good sections go in higher up, and the old ribbon at the bottom stays, so everything climbs at half load for about two years. The slack season starts. Proaño's buyers cut him to half. The Asamblea passes self-serving laws in the gap. Proaño asks Segundo to write the farms' own clinic label, and he writes "Ayora's clinic, from the farms." Lourdes is hurt and silent but not gone. Batch eight, which rang true, will probably never be asked about.

6. Characters:
   - Segundo Farinango: broker, licence 0419, honest, keeps receipts, plays the tuba.
   - Lourdes Imbaquingo: his wife, a dispensary nurse, who wanted the clinic.
   - Sisa: their daughter, turning sixteen, embarrassed by his carefulness and loyal to him.
   - Dolores Farinango: his mother, sells lupin beans, keeps the payslips, has a bad right arm from carrying water.
   - Ernesto Farinango: his father, a retired fixer in the parish home who sold the December asks for the road.
   - Jacinta Ulcuango: his cousin, a strain reader at the anchor, who has twin boys.
   - Wilmer Cuzco: a rival broker, cheerful, who rents other brokers' names.
   - Gustavo Proaño: owner of the big rose farm and an Asamblea member.
   - Luz Caiza: nineteen, a cutter from Tabacundo, who trusts Segundo's receipt.
   - Señora Rosaura Quishpe: an old cheese seller who pays for the school and procession music.
   - The clerk, the baker, the pork man and his wife, the bandleader, the chairwoman, the crew chief, the certifier: minor.
   - Waska: the rope, a mind that can only refuse.
   - Arriba: the mind at the top, which answers only what is asked.
   - Pilataxi: the farm's foreman (I'm not sure whether this is the same family as the one with the pickup).

7. I'm still unsure about these:
   - Whether Arriba sent the cord on purpose.
   - What happens with batch eight.
   - Why the rope distrusts Segundo for twenty-seven years, and whether that was ever resolved.
   - Whether Lourdes forgives him.
   - How the goods in the market delivery were physically timed.
   - Whether the Pilataxis are one family.
   - How the lawmakers and consortium will treat the Asamblea's "dressed" laws on 10 December.
   - Whether Segundo's name is now worth less or more.

9. For the people beyond the main cast, the change means:
   - Crews and the anchor town are spared from a failure that would have killed them and gutted the valley's economy.
   - Cutters lose hours, since sheds go to three days a week.
   - Farms lose half their freight for two years.
   - Habitat buyers get a lesson in what a question costs: a plain question moves markets before it is answered.
   - Brokers learn that a good name can be sold.
   - Other tether nations (Gabon, Kalimantan) may have the same batch problem.
   - The town gets Proaño's farm-funded clinic, but under the farms' name.
