# Banks critique: run-03 stage 3

**Blind spots, stated.** This lens likes sprawl, set pieces and
worldbuilding, so it over-rates D, which has the most world in it. Short
stories aren't its natural form. It can't judge length discipline, formal
fit or mechanism rigor well; Chiang and Vinge should cut where I'd add.

## A. The Witness

- **Plain pitch:** holds. A reader outside the genre gets "a clever teenager
  needs a grown-up in the room" at once, because they've watched teenagers.
  The heat wave is a ready clock.
- **Moral friction:** real. The father's lie would be loving and would break
  the law. The rival's leave motion is a legitimate tool used as a knife.
- **Dirty work:** the rival member, who frames a safeguard as concern. Good,
  but he needs a face and a reason to want the chair.
- **Humour:** lives in the teenager: the blow-up with her best friend, a girl
  who can model a grid and can't model a sulk. Don't let the crisis eat it.
- **Scale and intimacy:** a city's lights against one kitchen. Give the
  vastness one concrete moment: a district she browns out, and what it costs.
- **Enjoyable under the charter:** yes. The risk is Nadia being right about
  everything while Dad is lovely and useless.
- **Warning:** "he knows she hasn't slept" is the father-knows-best drift.
  Keep the lie hers to read and hers to answer.

## B. The Mouth

- **Plain pitch:** holds best of the four. Everyone has seen a good idea
  ignored until a senior person repeated it.
- **Moral friction:** the richest here. Mi-rae is a loving sister and also
  the public owner of his mind. The "our mother used to say" lines are a
  small theft and a small gift at once.
- **Dirty work:** the hired mouths, and a legal-head law that makes a boy
  responsible for what adults will not hear. Who passed it, and why, wants
  one line.
- **Humour:** natural. A professional mouth embroidering an eleven-year-old's
  crop schedule is funny, and the farmers who half-know are funnier.
- **Scale and intimacy:** intimacy is excellent. Scale is thin: a hill town.
  One glimpse of the national picture (a mouth's guild, a rate card) would
  do it.
- **Enjoyable:** yes; pepper terraces, tea, a sibling pair.
- **Warning:** Mi-rae must not win by being wise. Joon's plan has to be
  visibly the better mind, and the Korean kin terms need plain-word cover.

## C. Foreseeable

- **Plain pitch:** holds, though "foreseeability statement" is law-school
  talk until it is shown at a kitchen table.
- **Moral friction:** the sharpest. The mother wants blame because blame
  means she acted. That is a proper moral position, not a plot device.
- **Dirty work:** the courts, who have handed children a verdict on their
  parents' freedom. Nobody signed off on that cleanly, which is the point.
- **Humour:** the bracketed predictions can be dry and funny, but a worker
  injured in a collapse sits under all of it. It is the darkest premise.
- **Scale and intimacy:** all intimacy. No vastness anywhere.
- **Enjoyable:** the least. A coming-of-age story wants a world to stand
  in, and this is a hearing room.
- **Warning:** the log twist works, but a reader may see it coming from the
  first prediction. Hold it back, or let them see it and dread it.

## D. The Long Minority

- **Plain pitch:** holds, and it is the most Banksian. A generation rushed
  into power protects its own children from its own childhood, and sends its
  parents back to school.
- **Moral friction:** the most, and spread across three generations. The
  surgeon-at-fourteen did harm to her parents, and now does good for her
  daughter, on a law that costs the old.
- **Dirty work:** it is the ministry's policy, and the mother signs it. She is
  the story's hegemon with a wound.
- **Humour:** the grandmother, sixty-eight and back at school, is the best
  comic figure on the list.
- **Scale and intimacy:** a rail tunnel against a mother's silence. Best
  match in the set.
- **Enjoyable:** yes.
- **Warning:** the seed. Oriel's wait weakens the gap, and the logbook leans
  toward the elder-knowledge drift. At 6,000 words it also sprawls, and I
  say that as the lens that would sprawl it.

## Ranking

1. **B, The Mouth:** the best plain pitch, the best humour and a moral
   friction that sits inside one family.
2. **A, The Witness:** a clock and a working mechanism, if the father stays
   an obstacle and not a sage.
3. **D, The Long Minority:** the richest world and a seed risk, with a
   length that tests the brief's ceiling.
4. **C, Foreseeable:** the best idea and the worst place to spend a
   weekend.

**Why B should win.** It is the only premise where the seed, the joke and the
moral cost come from one device. Joon is far smarter than every adult in the
hall, and the page can show it in his plans. The adults still won't hear it
from him, because they learn from age. That is a civilization's blind spot
and a family's tenderness in a single scene, and it asks something of
both siblings. The sister carries the dirty work, loves him, and might be
stealing him. It is also the premise that tolerates a light tone: the mouths'
embroidery is funny, and the harvest stakes are real without being grim. Fix
the thin scale with one glimpse of how many mouths the country employs, make
sure Joon's mind is the visible superior one, and the story is enjoyable
and hard-edged at once.
