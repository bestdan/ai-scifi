# First Reader report: draft 3 ("Weather Report")

## Where I got lost

- **Paragraph 3 (the injection, "Everyone born since 2043...").** Two things tangled me. Mum was born in 2018 and I was fifteen in June, but I didn't know what year it was, so I couldn't tell how the dates fit. I took it as roughly 2058 or so, and later saw "2056" and "Somewhere around 2090" and it held together well enough. Not worked out exactly; I just stopped trying.
- **"Hit. 4,388 of 4,871."** The first time, I didn't know what the numbers were. The next paragraph explains the log, so I worked it out by reading on.
- **The scaffold ties scene at dinner.** I understood that one set held in calm weather and two were needed for wind. It took me until Mum's speech to be sure.
- **Hearing, the judge's count.** "Four hundred and ninety-one misses" and "about three times in ten" on the starred ones. I didn't check the arithmetic and wasn't sure whether the judge was reading it right. I let it go.
- **The ending.** I wasn't sure why Tamsin put the phone in her pocket "without writing anything under it". I worked out that she'd left the prediction unscored, because I couldn't tell whether Mum stopped on her own or because of the log.

## Where I got bored

- **The explanation of the law (the "Here it is in plain words, a step at a time" passage).** I was waiting for the form to turn into something happening. It reads like homework. I understood it and the stakes were clear, but it was the one stretch where I was reading to be told rather than to find out.
- **The opening on the sixth floor.** Lightly. The stairwell fault, the biscuits, the teacher with the ruler. I liked the voice, but I was waiting for the mother to show up. The Priya cricket jokes are charming, though they went on slightly longer than I needed.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **"She'll say the tea's stewed and drink it anyway. Ninety-five." / "This tea's stewed," Mum said, and drank it.** Delighted. The log is funny and then, later, it isn't.
- **Mum scoring her daughter back: "You're thinking I'll have the last bit of garlic bread. Hit." ... "she has never once got it right, and she always gives herself the point."** I loved this.
- **"I was glad of the rule for the first time in five years, and I knew while I was being glad that it was the wrong thing to be glad of."** This hit me.
- **Kyle in the hospital with the leeks: "Tell Frey the leeks are fine."** and later, "She taught me to tie a tube at the top of a chimney." The warmest bit in the story.
- **The darts: "That one was mine. Don't tell me it drifted."** Then "I hadn't been going to say it drifted. I had been going to say it to myself." I felt that.
- **The moment I realised the Tuesday entry was the one about the ties.** I did not see it coming. When the shed roof entry came up and then "And here is Tuesday the third of June", I went cold. The line "The log has a number for how likely a thing is. It has nowhere to put how bad" stayed with me.
- **The judge: "She never wrote any of it down, which I've always considered the kindest thing she did."**
- **The ending, "Don't—" and the unscored guess.** I was moved, even though I wasn't sure what I was meant to take from it.
- **The viaduct climb: "a dip on its top face where the maker's hand had pressed it into the mould".** Quiet and lovely.
- **Priya: "I didn't see it coming. You'll have to let me have that one."** A good small turn.

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared about Mum first, then Tamsin. Mum arrived in the kitchen with brick dust in her eyebrows and I was hers from the stewed tea. Kyle and his leeks came next, and I cared more about him than I expected. I felt for Kofi too, briefly, in the settee scene with the pigeon feed.

I would have kept reading past page 2. The sixth-floor opening was pleasant but not gripping on its own. What carried me over was the kitchen scene and the log's first entry, which is a good trick. I'd have been in by then for the mother alone.

## The question I'm left with

Did Mum stop because she'd read the log and chose not to finish, or because the wind took the word? Tamsin can't say, and neither can I. A second one: would Tamsin have filed "could" if she hadn't been the one who knew about the ties? I couldn't tell whether she did it because it was true, because it was the lesser harm, or because she needed to be punished.
