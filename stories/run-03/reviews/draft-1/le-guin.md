# Critique of draft 1: le-guin

## Scores

| Group | Axis | Score | One line |
|---|---|---|---|
| Felt | Atmosphere | 4 | Kitchen, pub, ward and office are lived-in; the treated children's own life is a rumour. |
| Felt | Weight | 4 | "Don't—" lands, as the cold reader says; the Mum-reads-the-log beat comes close to tidy. |
| Craft | Structure | 4 | A dilemma frame, but darts, ward and Kofi's visit carry a gathering underneath it. |

## The one thing
The two untreated adults at the end desks are the story's best picture of who pays for this world, and they disappear after scene 1. Bring them back once, near the hearing or the basement exile, so the displaced are a chorus and Mum isn't the only one carrying the cost.

## Notes

1. **Scene 1, end desks (structural, small):** "Nobody waits for the second read. They know that. They bring in a tin of biscuits on Fridays." Then the yellow note: "LANDING BEARING, QUERY?" This is the best image of the cost in the draft. Priya's biscuit "from the Friday tin" in the corridor echoes it, but the teacher himself never comes back. A single glimpse would do, say him passing Tamsin on her way to the basement. Then the exile reads as joining the second read, not just as a punishment.
2. **Scene 4, the law paragraph (line-level):** "After the treated took over the schedules... They called it mercy, and they also called it evidence." The first reader called the neighbouring bus-driver paragraph "information delivered". The ledger needs the facts stated, so keep them. But enact one clause and cut the rest, for instance the boy's father on a shelf-stacking shift, so the narration needn't say "The boy on my floor's dad is limited. He stacks shelves now."
3. **Theme stated by the narrator (line-level):** "He'd done the thing I do, which is to think a person is a set of numbers you haven't got yet." And "I'd stopped thinking of her as someone you can say things to. I'd been treating her like weather. You don't argue with weather. You write it down." Keep the weather lines. They are earned and the cold reader loved them. Cut the Kofi sentence, which says the same thing a page earlier than it needs saying.
4. **Who raises the children (structural, light):** Mum was outgrown at ten, and we are told so only in the bible. One remembered moment would give the house its history, such as Mum realising she couldn't help with homework. Today the house has only the rule. The pencil on the gas bill ("Draw it") is a good start, but it hints at this history rather than showing it.
5. **Tamsin's name (line-level):** No one in the text calls her by it. Her mother's "love" or "you" is all we get. One use by Mum, at the darts or in the hospital, would anchor her.
6. **Scene 8, "I'm very boring, aren't I" (line-level):** Funny and true, but it comes close to a single-paragraph resolution. The unscored "Don't—" is the real ending, so keep the laugh brief.
7. **Dad, "Pike is his name" (line-level):** The sentence is awkward. It also muddles why Mum and Tamsin share a surname when Dad left.

## What to protect
- "She got a pencil out of the drawer and turned the gas bill over and said, 'Draw it.'" and "that was what her dad used to say about fish."
- "She scores me back sometimes, and she has never once got it right, and she always gives herself the point."
- The bin and the date: "'Second of July,' she said. 'Just so I know.'"
- Kofi on the settee holding the pigeon feed, in his school blazer, and "I don't know you at all. I've known you a year."
- The judge: "She never wrote any of it down, which I've always considered the kindest thing she did."
- The ending, entire: "I've got no number for that." Leave it unscored.
- The foreman who "found she'd left her keys in the gate."

## Blind spot disclosure
I favour domestic texture and mourn the displaced, so I may overvalue the end-desk pair and undervalue pace. The opening page may need cutting for momentum more than I want. I'm also weak on the legal mechanics (the 150-word statement limit, the count of misses), which Egan and Watts should judge.
