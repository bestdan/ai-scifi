## The one thing
The self-diagnosis paragraph ("I'd told myself that the rule meant I couldn't say anything... I never thought of either") contradicts scene 2, and the story doesn't notice. At dinner Tamsin does ask Mum, "What do the ties do?" and then "So what will you do?" She gets "Get it done Friday", reads the face behind it, and lets it go. So she did ask. She asked the question whose answer she already had at eighty per cent. Let the narrator catch herself here, or fix the line. The first option is the better one: it is confabulation, the self-story rewritten after the fact, and the hindsight version of her guilt is too articulate and too complete to be what she knew at the time.

## Notes

1. **Structural. The self-diagnosis, scene 6:** besides the contradiction above, it is a one-paragraph epiphany that names its own mechanism ("I'd been treating her like weather"). That is the style guide's banned shape. Have her work it out badly, or only half. What is the brain doing that she doesn't know about? An eighty-per-cent safety prediction was logged in the same format as the tea, with no alarm. That is the real horror, and it is not stated. One sentence could state it.
2. **Structural. The ruling and the denominator:** the story is honest that a miss proves only a wrong model ("the best it could find was my mistakes"). It never says the converse, that a hit doesn't show she couldn't choose. Predictable is not the same as unfree, and a log of what Tamsin chose to write down is selected. She logs when she feels sure. The judge treats 4,922 as a sample of Mum's life. One line from Tamsin on what she never logged would close the hole.
3. **Line-level. The judge's count:** "On the entries the witness marked as important... about three times in ten." The page never shows Tamsin flagging importance, and this contradicts "I'm nearer seven." Plant the flag earlier, or cut it.
4. **Line-level. The ending, "I could put her at fifty":** the number is good, but "I was glad anyway" softens. Watts would let the not-knowing stand without the gladness. This is a flinch, though a light one. The cold reader loved it, so I only flag it.
5. **Line-level. The stairwell:** differential shortening between a concrete core and a steel frame is real, but it is a tall-building problem. At seven storeys the movement is small, and a landing cracking by 2090 will make a structural engineer frown. Keep the sliding bearing. Make the block taller, or call the cause creep.
6. **Line-level. The treatment:** the cold reader never learned what "treated" means. Exposition isn't the answer, but one plainly stated physical fact would help, such as Mum's generation seeing the difference at a glance.

## What to protect
- The self-scoring: "She scores me back sometimes, and she has never once got it right, and she always gives herself the point." and "I gave it to myself." Both honestly show the bias, and the court uses it well.
- "At the time there was no chain of reasoning to set out. I looked at the drawing and the landing was wrong." Intuition first, rationalisation after.
- The judge's mother who never wrote anything down.
- "I've got no number for that. I could put her at fifty, and I'd be guessing."
- Mum's own observer effect: "I don't read it."

## Blind spot disclosure
I am hard on warmth and on tidy self-knowledge, and I may be asking a love story to be a case study. The cold reader found the guilt scene sharp, and the Le Guin and Banks lenses may defend it as the heart of the story. I did not check the legal procedure. I also have no standing on prose rhythm.
