# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A fifteen-year-old in Sheffield, who has spent years predicting what her mother will do and writing the predictions in a log, had written down in advance that her mother would take the shortcut on a scaffold that later failed and hurt a man. A court asks her whether her mother could have done otherwise, and she has to decide whether to hand over the log and tell the truth.

2. If she says her mother couldn't have chosen otherwise, her mother is cleared but is labelled "limited" and can never sign off safety work again, which ends her trade. If she says her mother could have, her mother is found responsible and gets a fine and a suspension, but keeps her signature. Filing anything means handing over the log, which shows the narrator saw it coming and said nothing. That could cost the narrator her own job, and the relationship with her mother. She files "could have" and attaches the whole log. Mum is fined three thousand pounds and suspended six months but is not limited. The narrator loses a term of checking work, and Mum goes quiet on her for a day and a half.

3. As I understood it, the treated children took over planning and design, and the untreated adults who did the physical work kept getting blamed for not keeping up with systems they couldn't follow. So from 2056 the courts began asking the person who knows a worker best, usually their treated child, whether the worker could have chosen otherwise. They called it mercy and evidence. The catch is that if you couldn't choose this time, the law decides it can't trust you to choose next time. I don't know why the treatment itself exists. The story doesn't say.

4. That the narrator had predicted the shortcut herself three days before it happened (the Tuesday entry at eighty per cent) and said nothing. I'd half suspected it from "I'll come to it", but it changed how I read the dinner scene and her gladness about the rule. Her guilt and the "weather" talk meant more after that. Also, the law that sounds like mercy is actually a trap for the parent.

5. At the hearing the judge ruled that Mum could have chosen otherwise: fined, suspended six months, not limited. The judge's reasoning was the log's misses, about 491 of 4,922, which showed Mum doing something other than expected. The narrator felt that was a thin reason, since a miss only shows her model was wrong. Later Mum read the whole log and laughed at how boring she is. The house rule against telling predictions ended. On the viaduct scaffold the narrator wrote that Mum would tell her not to look down. Mum began "Don't—" and stopped. The narrator can't tell if that was deliberate, and she looked down and wrote nothing.

- Mum (Freya Pike, "Frey"): scaffolder for twenty-two years, foreman for twelve. Best darts player at the Fat Cat. Takes the shortcut.
- Priya Shah: sixteen, the narrator's section boss, always wrong about cricket and judges. Kind to her.
- Kofi Mensah: thirteen, the firm's planner who set the one-day schedule. Keeps pigeons. Can't sign for anything.
- Kyle Whitlam: fifty-four, on Mum's crew for years, falls and breaks his leg, grows leeks. Doesn't want Mum called limited.
- Bev: Kyle's wife.
- Old maths teacher: does the second read at an end desk and leaves the yellow note, along with the former floor boss. Unnamed.
- Dad: left when she was six, lives in Cornwall, sends a puffin card each March. Surname Pike is his.
- Gran: rings on Sunday.
- The judge: untreated woman, retiring, with half-moon glasses. Her mother also could predict her.
- Kyle's lawyer: clicks a pen, wants "couldn't" so the firm pays.
- The firm's lawyer: from Leeds, wears a cardigan, says Mum chose.
- The foreman on the viaduct: a woman Mum trained, who lets them up.

- Whether the narrator's mother forgives her. The ending suggests so, but it's not said.
