## The one thing
The story's guilt rests on "I could have asked her one question at dinner... I never thought of either." Scene 2 shows her asking that question: "So what will you do?" Mum answers "Get it done Friday." She also asks about the ties and about Kofi signing off. Reconcile this. The better fix is to make the failure sharper: she asked, heard "Get it done Friday", scored it in her head, and did not ask the next question, "both sets?". She then knew exactly where the question would have gone and chose not to put it. That is the real thing she is wrong about: understanding a person as a trajectory, and so never interrupting it.

## Notes

1. **Scene 6, "I never thought of either. I'd stopped thinking of her as someone you can say things to."** Structural. This contradicts scene 2 (see above). The confession is also the story's emotional centre, so the contradiction costs the most here. Anyone who rereads the dinner will see Tamsin doing the very thing she says she never did.
2. **The narrator's withholding.** Structural. "I'll come to it" appears three times (scene 1, and twice in scene 2, "I'm putting it off because it's the reason for everything else"). A narrator teasing a reveal is plot suspense. The idea is better served by the Tuesday entry sitting unremarked in the log, in order, in scene 2, like all the others. The reader would then see the foreknowledge go by as quietly as the tea, and the horror comes from its being ordinary. The first reader says the hook worked, but it is borrowed from thriller form, and the idea doesn't need it.
3. **Hearing: "Is that a woman choosing? Or is that the weather?" / "It's both."** Line-level. The theme is delivered out loud, and neatly. Let her fail to answer, as she does two exchanges later ("I didn't have a better answer than that"). Cut "It's both" and the judge writing it down.
4. **The one unnecessary scene.** Cut the bus-driver's-son paragraph in scene 2. It is exposition the law paragraph in scene 4 does again, and the first reader named it as the place where the story stopped. Also cut the Gran phone call and probably the puffin card; they are checklist items for the father and the old person, and they don't turn.
5. **Scene 1 opening.** Line-level. The first reader was "reading politely". The stairwell is the right novum demonstration (a minute versus a week), but the cricket and the viaduct paragraph hold up the beginning. Move the viaduct to a sentence.
6. **"Treated" is never said to be a treatment.** The reader guessed. The ledger doesn't grade it, but one plain clause ("the treatment they give before birth") would settle what the page now leaves open.
7. **The law paragraph (scene 4).** Accurate, dense, and the right place. The sentence "If you couldn't choose this time, it can't trust you to choose next time" is the story's logical joke; give it its own line.

## What to protect
- The judge's "Four hundred and ninety-one times it shows her doing something other than what was expected of her", followed by Tamsin's "The judge was right for a thin reason... the best it could find was my mistakes." That is the philosophical heart: the evidence for freedom is the model's failures. Don't tidy it.
- "My own mother could do this to me... She never wrote any of it down, which I've always considered the kindest thing she did."
- The ending, unscored: "I've got no number for that. I could put her at fifty, and I'd be guessing."
- "I was glad of the rule for the first time in five years, and I knew while I was being glad that it was the wrong thing to be glad of."
- Mum: "She predicts my tea. ... I don't read it."

## Blind spot disclosure
I weigh logical consistency and the theme above momentum, so I may overrate the contradiction in note 1 and underrate how well the withheld entry pulled the cold reader through page 2. Banks may defend the hook, and the pub and hospital scenes carry warmth I'd treat as secondary. I also tend to cut scenes that aren't thematic, and the Gran call may be doing quiet world work I can't see.
