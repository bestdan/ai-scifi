# Story bible: Foreseeable (working title)

## Premise
**C, Foreseeable**, as revised in the Decision of `03-synthesis.md`.

A treated child gets a whole childhood of data on one person, and people are
more predictable than they like to believe. By fourteen the treated can
predict their untreated parents' words and choices about nine times in ten,
and the courts use it: in a negligence hearing they can ask a treated
relative whether the person could have chosen otherwise. Tamsin Pike,
fifteen, is a junior checker at the city buildings office and keeps a private
log of her predictions about her mother. When a scaffold her mother signed
off on collapses and breaks a worker's leg, Tamsin's statement could clear
her mother, but her mother wants to be blamed. And the court will want the
log, which shows Tamsin predicted the shortcut a week before.

The story is set in Sheffield, England, in June and July 2059.

## What the reader needs about the world
In plain words. The outline places each in the first third.

1. **Since 2043 nearly every baby gets the treatment before birth.** Treated
   children learn so fast that they finish school at eleven and do adult work
   by twelve to fourteen. Tamsin, at fifteen, checks building plans for the
   city.
2. **The untreated, everyone born before 2043, are being replaced at work by
   treated teenagers.** Mum's crew takes its schedules from a
   thirteen-year-old planner. Tamsin's own office used to be run by adults.
   The untreated still do the hands-on work, and they still sign for it,
   because the law says the person who signs for a scaffold must be an adult
   who can be blamed, and a thirteen-year-old can't.
3. **Treated children can predict the people they grew up with, about nine
   times in ten.** It isn't mind-reading. It is fifteen years of watching one
   person, with a mind that can turn all of it into a model. Families joke
   about it. In the Pike house there is a rule: Tamsin never tells Mum a
   prediction.
4. **For three years the courts have asked a treated relative whether an
   untreated person could have chosen otherwise.** If the answer is no, the
   person is cleared and the blame passes to the firm. But they are recorded
   as "limited": unable to choose, so never again allowed to sign for
   anything that people's safety depends on.

## The novum
- **What it is:** a treatment given before birth that raises intelligence
  by about 60 IQ points and makes learning several times faster. Nearly
  universal since 2043.
- **How it works:** the story doesn't explain the biology. It shows two
  things. First, raw ability: Tamsin sees in a minute what a team of adults
  would take a week to find in a building plan, and she can't explain how to
  her mother. Second, the consequence the plot turns on: a treated child who
  grows up beside an untreated parent builds a model of that parent from
  fifteen years of watching, and the model predicts the parent's next words
  and choices about nine times in ten.
- **What it costs:** parents stop surprising their children, and the
  children begin to treat their parents as weather: forgivable, and not
  quite responsible.
- **Its rules and their edge cases:**
  - Prediction needs years of close data. Tamsin can predict her mother, not
    a stranger. Kofi has no such data on Mum's crew.
  - Most hits are cheap: habits and routines. The rate falls on rare choices
    made under pressure, which are the ones that matter. Tamsin knows this.
  - It works treated-on-untreated. Treated people are hard for each other to
    predict, because each knows the other is modelling them and varies on
    purpose.
  - Telling someone a prediction changes what they do. That is why the Pike
    house has its rule. Mum knows about the log, and being watched has shaped
    her too.
  - Tamsin scores her own log. Nobody checks her ticks.

## The consequence ladder
1. **First order:** treated teenagers are better at nearly every desk job
   than the adults who trained them, and they take those jobs.
2. **Second order:** when an untreated worker fails at a system a treated
   planner designed, who is to blame? Since 2056 the courts have asked the
   person who knows the worker best, their treated child, whether the worker
   could have done otherwise.
3. **Third order:** being held responsible becomes something the young grant
   or withhold. To excuse a parent is to say she never chose anything.

## The society
- **Institutions:** the city buildings office, now run by treated
  teenagers; scaffolding firms with treated planners and untreated crews;
  the civil court, where an untreated judge near retirement hears negligence
  cases; school, a two-year course the treated finish by eleven.
- **Economy:** the untreated still do the hands-on trades, for now. The
  schedules, the designs and the checks have gone to the treated. Gaz is
  paid for his injury by the firm's insurer whatever the court decides.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:** untreated parents still raise
  treated children, who outgrow them at about ten. Many families carry on as
  families anyway. Tamsin's father left when she was six and lives in
  Cornwall; he sends a card. Mum's mother, Gran, is seventy-two and rings on
  Sundays.
- **Death:** unchanged.
- **Taboos:** saying a prediction out loud to the person it is about. Among
  the treated it is rude; in many homes it is forbidden.
- **An ordinary day:** Tamsin takes the tram to the buildings office, checks
  plans at a desk beside other treated teenagers, comes home, and eats
  dinner with her mother, who has been up a scaffold since seven. On
  Saturdays Mum plays darts at the Fat Cat, and Tamsin goes to watch.

## Minds
- **Tamsin (treated):** sees people as patterns and is usually right about
  them. She finds it obvious that her mother will do what her mother always
  does. She has stopped thinking of her mother as someone whose mind can be
  changed, only predicted.
- **Freya (untreated):** knows she is predictable and hates it. She knows
  her daughter is cleverer than her in every way she can measure. She holds
  on to one thing: what she does is her own choice, even when it's the
  choice everyone expected.

## Characters

**Tamsin Pike**, fifteen, treated, born March 2044.
- **Name:** from her father's Cornish family.
- **What she wants:** to keep her mother safe, at work and in court.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks predicting someone is the same as
  understanding them, and that a person you can predict can't be blamed. And
  she thinks saying nothing was the only thing the house rule allowed. She
  could have asked a question, or moved Kofi's date, and she never thought
  to.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** she has known for years how
  each conversation with Mum will go, and it has made her lonely in her own
  kitchen.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** the old brick railway viaduct she
  can see from the buildings office, which she has checked on paper and can't
  improve. In June a scaffold goes up on it for repointing.

**Freya Pike**, forty-one, untreated, born 2018, a scaffolder for twenty-two
years and a foreman for twelve.
- **Name:** a common name for her year; the crew call her Frey.
- **What she wants:** to be held to account, not excused like a child or a
  storm. She never says this outright; she says it about darts.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks Tamsin's predictions are a kind of
  contempt. Some of them are care.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** her daughter, her planner and
  soon her trade have all outgrown her.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** Saturday darts at the Fat Cat,
  where she is the best on the board.

**Kofi Mensah**, thirteen, treated, the scaffolding firm's planner.
- **Name:** Ghanaian-British family, Sheffield-born.
- **What he wants:** a schedule that works on paper. He thinks it did.
- **What he's wrong about:** that a crew running late is a number, not three
  tired people in the rain. He has no close data on them and didn't know he
  needed it.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** he gives orders to men older
  than his grandfather, and he can't sign for any of it.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** pigeons; he keeps two.
- **After the collapse:** he comes to the Pikes' door, frightened, to ask
  whether Tamsin's statement will put the blame on him.

**Kyle Whitlam**, fifty-four, untreated, scaffolder on Freya's crew.
- **Name:** a common name for 2005; he's been on Freya's crew since she
  started.
- **What he wants:** his leg to mend and to go back up.
- **What he's wrong about:** he thinks the fall was nobody's fault.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** his lawyer wants Freya
  cleared, because it puts the blame on the firm. Kyle doesn't want to be the
  reason she's called limited.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** his allotment leeks. His wife,
  Bev, brings the photographs to the hospital.

**Priya Shah**, sixteen, treated, the senior checker who runs Tamsin's
section.
- **Name:** British-Gujarati family.
- **What she wants:** to keep her section clean of conflicts of interest, and
  not to look like a child giving orders to her old teachers, two of whom
  still work there.
- **What she's wrong about:** she is sure the judge will clear Freya, and
  says so. She is wrong.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** she is the boss of people who
  used to teach her, and it makes her anxious, not rigid. She bends a rule
  once, to let Tamsin read her mother's file.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** cricket scores; she predicts them
  badly and loves that.

## Point of view and voice
- **Teller:** Tamsin.
- **Person and tense:** first person, past tense, told from a few weeks after
  the hearing.
- **Register:** plain, dry and quick. Short sentences when she's sure of
  herself, longer ones when she isn't. She is funny about her mother because
  she loves her. No slang except the few words in the Glossary.
- **Why this teller:** the gap is in her head. Her bracketed predictions let
  the reader watch her outthink her mother, and watch her be wrong.
- **The form:** before some exchanges with Mum, Tamsin's prediction appears on
  its own line in square brackets, with how sure she was. After it, she says
  whether it came true: "Hit" or "Miss." The log is the story's device and,
  at the end, its evidence. The last prediction in the story is left
  unscored.
- **A sample paragraph in the voice:**

  > [She'll say the tea's stewed and drink it anyway. Ninety-five.]
  >
  > "This tea's stewed," Mum said, and drank it. Hit. I'd kept the log since
  > I was nine, and I was right about her nine times in ten. I'm not proud of
  > the number. Most of it is tea. It's what you get if you watch one person
  > for fifteen years with a mind that keeps all of it. Mum knew about the
  > log. She called it my weather report.

## Glossary
Every coined, foreign or slang word the story uses. **Job** is what the plain
word couldn't do. **Clear at first use by** is the line or context that makes
the meaning plain to a reader who doesn't speak the language. A term with no
Job is cut, and the story uses the plain word.

| Term | Meaning | Job | Clear at first use by | Where the term comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| treated / untreated | born with the treatment, or before it existed | the world's own words for the gap; nothing plainer names it | scene 1: "Everyone born since 2043 is treated. Mum was born in 2018." | everyday speech |
| ties | the steel bolts and tubes that fix a scaffold to the wall it stands against | the shortcut is about them, and the trade's word marks Mum's world | scene 2: Mum explains them at dinner, "the ties hold it to the wall, that's all they do" | the scaffolding trade |
| limited | the court's finding that a person couldn't have chosen otherwise, so can't sign for safety work again | the legal word, which Mum hates and repeats | scene 4: Tamsin explains it in narration as she reads the form, before the letter arrives | the court |
| statement | the written answer a treated relative gives the court on whether the person could have chosen otherwise | what the court calls it; the letter's full name ("foreseeability statement") appears once, after the plain explanation | scene 4, in plain words; the full name only in the letter in scene 5 | the court |

No other coined, foreign or slang word goes in the story. No Sheffield
dialect for colour: the evening meal is "dinner", not "tea". "Mum" stays.

- **Graded rows.** Premise, Stakes, Why, Twist and Ending are fixed. Every
  story has them, and the stage 7 comprehension gate grades a cold reader
  against them. A story with no twist writes "none" in that row.
- **Traced rows.** Add one for each mechanism the plot turns on, including
  any number or date whose arithmetic the ending depends on (give the
  arithmetic in the fact column). They aren't graded, but each needs an
  explicit line.
- **Stated and shown.** Each graded row is made clear twice: once
  **stated**, in plain words (the explicit line), and once **shown**, as a
  consequence in a scene. A statement can't be a quoted document of more
  than about 150 words. Traced rows need only the explicit line.
- **Stakes come before the climax.** The Stakes row is made explicit in a
  scene before the climax, not only by the end.
- **"Never explained" is not a valid ledger entry.** Every row is made
  explicit by the final scene. A fact may be withheld, but only until the
  scene this table names.

## Ending direction
Tamsin chooses to hold her mother responsible, and herself with her. It
costs both of them: Mum's fine and suspension, Tamsin's term off checking,
and the end of the house rule, because the log is now public. What it buys
is that Mum is still someone who signs. The law's reasoning is not repaired:
the judge rules Freya responsible because the log shows she sometimes does
otherwise, which is right for a thin reason, and Tamsin knows it. The ending
is warm but not resolved. Mum's surprise in the last scene is small, Tamsin
can't tell if it was a choice, and she leaves it unscored.
