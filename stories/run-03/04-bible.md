# Story bible: Foreseeable (working title)

## Premise
**C, Foreseeable**, as revised in the Decision of `03-synthesis.md`.

A treated child gets a whole childhood of data on one person, and people are
more predictable than they like to believe. By fourteen the treated can
predict their untreated parents' words and choices nine times in ten, and
the courts use it: in a negligence hearing they can ask a treated relative
whether the person could have chosen otherwise. Tamsin Pike, fifteen, is a
junior checker at the city buildings office and keeps a private log of her
predictions about her mother. When a scaffold her mother signed off on
collapses and breaks a worker's leg, Tamsin's statement could clear her
mother, but her mother asks to be blamed. Then the court asks for the log,
and it shows Tamsin predicted the shortcut a week before.

## What the reader needs about the world
In plain words. The outline places each in the first third.

1. **Since 2044 nearly every baby gets the treatment before birth.** Treated
   children learn so fast that by twelve to fourteen they do adult work.
   Tamsin, at fifteen, checks building plans for the city.
2. **The untreated, everyone born before 2044, are being replaced at work by
   treated teenagers.** Mum's crew takes its schedules from a
   thirteen-year-old planner. Tamsin's own office used to be run by adults.
3. **Treated children can predict the people they grew up with, nine times
   in ten.** It is not mind-reading: it is fifteen years of watching one
   person, with a mind that forgets nothing. Families joke about it. In the
   Pike house there is a rule: Tamsin never tells Mum a prediction.
4. **The courts now ask a treated relative whether an untreated person could
   have chosen otherwise.** If the answer is no, the person is cleared and
   the blame passes to the firm. But they are recorded as "limited": unable
   to choose, so never again allowed to sign off on anything that people's
   safety depends on.

## The novum
- **What it is:** a prenatal treatment that raises intelligence by about 60
  IQ points and makes learning several times faster. Given since 2044.
- **How it works:** the story doesn't explain the biology. What it shows is
  one consequence: a treated child who grows up beside an untreated parent
  builds an accurate model of that parent, from fifteen years of watching
  with a mind that forgets nothing. The model predicts the parent's next
  words and choices about nine times in ten. It never reaches ten, because
  people do sometimes surprise.
- **What it costs:** parents stop surprising their children, and the
  children begin to treat their parents as weather: forgivable, and not
  quite responsible.
- **Its rules and their edge cases:**
  - Prediction needs years of close data. Tamsin can predict her mother,
    not a stranger.
  - It is about habit and character, not facts: she can predict that Mum
    will cut a corner when a crew is late, not the wind speed.
  - It works only treated-on-untreated. Untreated people can't do it back,
    and treated people are hard for each other to predict.
  - Telling someone a prediction changes what they do. That is why the Pike
    house has its rule.

## The consequence ladder
1. **First order:** treated teenagers are better at nearly every job than
   the adults who trained them, and they take those jobs.
2. **Second order:** when an untreated worker fails at a system a treated
   planner designed, who is to blame? Courts began asking the person who
   knows the worker best, their treated child, whether the worker could
   have done otherwise.
3. **Third order:** being held responsible becomes something the young grant
   or withhold. To excuse a parent is to say she never chose anything.

## The society
- **Institutions:** the city buildings office, now run by treated
  teenagers; scaffolding firms with treated planners and untreated crews;
  the civil court, where a judge (untreated, near retirement) hears
  negligence cases.
- **Economy:** the untreated still do the hands-on trades, for now. The
  schedules, the designs and the checks have gone to the treated, who are
  faster and cheaper per hour of thought.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:** untreated parents still raise
  treated children, who outgrow them at about ten. Many families carry on
  as families anyway: dinner, chores, arguments, with the child knowing how
  each argument will end.
- **Death:** unchanged.
- **Taboos:** saying a prediction out loud to the person it is about. Among
  the treated it is rude; in many homes it is forbidden.
- **An ordinary day:** Tamsin takes the tram to the buildings office, checks
  plans at a desk beside other treated teenagers, comes home, and eats dinner
  with her mother, who has been up a scaffold since seven.

## Minds
- **Tamsin (treated):** sees people as patterns and is usually right about
  them. She finds it obvious that her mother will do what her mother
  always does. She can't easily think of her mother as someone who might
  not.
- **Lorraine (untreated):** knows she is predictable and hates it. She knows
  her daughter is cleverer than her in every way she can measure. She holds
  on to one thing: what she does is her own choice, even when it's the
  choice everyone expected.

## Characters

**Tamsin Pike**, fifteen, treated.
- **Name:** an English name, from the Cornish family on her father's side.
- **What she wants:** to keep her mother safe, at work and in court.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks predicting someone is the same as
  understanding them, and that a person you can predict can't be blamed.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** she has known for years how
  each conversation with Mum will go, and it has made her lonely in her own
  kitchen.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** the old brick viaduct she can see
  from the buildings office, which she has checked on paper and can't
  improve.

**Lorraine Pike**, forty-one, untreated, a scaffolding foreman for
twenty-two years.
- **Name:** a common English name for her generation; the crew call her Lor.
- **What she wants:** to be held to account like a grown woman, not excused
  like a child or a storm.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks Tamsin's predictions are a kind of
  contempt. Some of them are care.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** her daughter, her planner and
  soon her trade have all outgrown her.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** Saturday darts at the Fat Cat,
  where she is the best on the board.

**Kofi Mensah**, thirteen, treated, the scaffolding firm's planner.
- **Name:** Ghanaian-British family, Sheffield-born.
- **What he wants:** a schedule that works on paper. He thinks it did.
- **What he's wrong about:** that an untreated crew running late is a
  variable, not three tired men in the rain.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** he gives orders to men older
  than his grandfather and doesn't know how to stand on a site.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** pigeons; he keeps two.

**Gary "Gaz" Whitlam**, fifty-four, untreated scaffolder on Lorraine's crew.
- **Name:** Sheffield; "Gaz" is what everyone has called him since school.
- **What he wants:** his leg to mend and to go back up.
- **What he's wrong about:** he thinks the fall was nobody's fault.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** he would be replaced by a
  machine a treated engineer designs, if anyone had designed one yet.
- **Something he loves outside the plot:** his allotment leeks.

**Priya Shah**, sixteen, treated, the senior checker who runs Tamsin's
section.
- **Name:** British-Gujarati family.
- **What she wants:** her section to stay clean of conflicts of interest.
- **What she's wrong about:** that rules settle what the right thing is.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** she is the boss of people
  who used to be her teachers.
- **Something she loves outside the plot:** cricket scores; she predicts
  them badly and loves that.

## Point of view and voice
- **Teller:** Tamsin.
- **Person and tense:** first person, past tense, told from a few weeks
  after the hearing.
- **Register:** plain, dry and quick. Short sentences when she's sure of
  herself, longer ones when she isn't. She is funny about her mother because
  she loves her. No slang except the few words in the Glossary.
- **Why this teller:** the gap is in her head. Her bracketed predictions let
  the reader watch her outthink her mother, and watch her be wrong.
- **The form:** before each exchange with Mum, Tamsin's prediction appears
  on its own line in square brackets. After it, she says whether it came
  true: a tick, or "Miss." The log is the story's device and, at the end,
  its evidence.
- **A sample paragraph in the voice:**

  > [She'll say the tea's stewed and drink it anyway.]
  >
  > "This tea's stewed," Mum said, and drank it. Tick. I'd been keeping the
  > log since I was nine, in a notebook and then on my phone, and I was
  > right about her nine times in ten. I'm not proud of the number. It's
  > what you get if you watch one person for fifteen years and forget
  > nothing. Mum knew about the log. She called it my weather report.

## Glossary
Every coined, foreign or slang word the story uses. **Job** is what the plain
word couldn't do. **Clear at first use by** is the line or context that makes
the meaning plain to a reader who doesn't speak the language. A term with no
Job is cut, and the story uses the plain word.

| Term | Meaning | Job | Clear at first use by | Where the term comes from |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| treated / untreated | born with the treatment, or before it existed | the world's own words for the gap; nothing plainer names it | scene 1: "Everyone born after 2044 is treated. Mum was born in 2019." | everyday speech |
| ties | the steel bolts and tubes that fix a scaffold to the wall it stands against | the shortcut is about them, and the trade's word marks Mum's world | scene 2: Mum explains them at dinner, "the ties hold it to the wall, that's all they do" | the scaffolding trade |
| limited | the court's finding that a person couldn't have chosen otherwise, so can't sign off on safety work again | the legal word, which Mum hates and repeats | scene 4: Tamsin explains it at work before the letter arrives | the court |
| statement | the written answer a treated relative gives the court on whether the person could have chosen otherwise | what the court calls it; the letter's full name ("foreseeability statement") appears once, after the plain explanation | scene 4, in plain words; the full name only in the letter in scene 5 | the court |

No other coined, foreign or slang word goes in the story. No Sheffield
dialect for colour: the evening meal is "dinner", not "tea". "Mum" stays.

- **Graded rows.** Premise, Stakes, Why, Twist and Ending are fixed. Every
  story has them, and the stage 7 comprehension gate grades a cold reader
  against them. A story with no twist writes "none" in that row.
- **Traced rows.** Add one for each mechanism the plot turns on, including
  any number or date whose arithmetic the ending depends on (give the
  arithmetic in the fact column). They aren't graded, but each needs an
  explicit line.
- **Stated and shown.** Each graded row is made clear twice: once
  **stated**, in plain words (the explicit line), and once **shown**, as a
  consequence in a scene. A statement can't be a quoted document of more
  than about 150 words. Traced rows need only the explicit line.
- **Stakes come before the climax.** The Stakes row is made explicit in a
  scene before the climax, not only by the end.
- **"Never explained" is not a valid ledger entry.** Every row is made
  explicit by the final scene. A fact may be withheld, but only until the
  scene this table names.

## Ending direction
Tamsin chooses to hold her mother responsible, and herself with her. It
costs both of them: Mum's fine and suspension, Tamsin's term off checking,
and the end of the house rule, because the log is now public. What it buys
is that Mum is still someone who chooses. The ending is warm but not
resolved: Mum's surprise in the last scene is small, and Tamsin doesn't
know whether she'll ever be surprised again.
