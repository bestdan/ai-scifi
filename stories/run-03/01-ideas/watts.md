# Idea cards: watts

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front.** It reaches for bleakness by
reflex, so all three cards are deliberately aimed at warm endings. Other
lenses should check whether they are earned. It builds characters to
illustrate a finding, so the people here need a drafter to give them wants
beyond the case study. It underrates ritual, meaning and joy as forces in
their own right. It front-loads density: each card leans on one finding, and
the story should carry no more than that one.

## Card 1: The Witness

- **Novum:** Treated teenagers do adult work at fourteen, but the law requires
  an untreated adult in the room for any consequential decision. Not to judge
  it: to be watched by.
- **Futurity:** F2. One generation out, with labour law and family life
  rebuilt around a hired presence.
- **How it works:** The treatment speeds up cortical learning, but limbic and
  prefrontal maturation stay on the old clock (Casey, Jones and Hare 2008;
  Steinberg 2008). The well-replicated peer effect: adolescents take far more
  risks when peers are watching, and adults don't (Gardner and Steinberg
  2005). An observer who isn't a peer damps it.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated teams run the infrastructure. Every
  team has a "witness."
- **Second-order consequence:** Witnessing becomes the job for displaced
  adults. It is paid, licensed and, the kids say, pointless.
- **Third-order consequence:** Parents who were smart enough to raise these
  children learn that their value is their age, not their minds.
- **The story:** Nell, fifteen, runs a regional flood authority. Her father is
  her assigned witness, and she wants to sign alone. During a storm-surge
  call, he finally admits he doesn't understand a word of it. She has to work
  out whether he matters anyway, and say so to him.
- **Why this lens:** The brain is doing something the character doesn't know
  about. Her judgement isn't wholly hers.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain* (enhanced children
  outpace adults). There, the adults are the problem. Here, the adults are a
  piece of neural equipment. I found no match for the witness role itself.

## Card 2: The Mouth

- **Novum:** Treated children know more than the adults around them, but
  adults can't learn from them. So their teaching goes out through hired
  untreated adults who speak it for them: "mouths."
- **Futurity:** F2. The flow of knowledge between generations runs backwards,
  and an institution grows up to hide that.
- **How it works:** Humans pick whom to learn from by prestige cues: age,
  status, and who other people attend to (Henrich and Gil-White 2001; Chudek
  et al. 2012). Natural pedagogy assumes the teacher is the elder (Csibra and
  Gergely 2009). A child's correct instruction gets weighed less and
  remembered worse.
- **First-order consequence:** Farm extension, medicine and civic briefings
  all go through mouths.
- **Second-order consequence:** A good mouth is worth more than a good mind.
  Mouths improvise, soften and translate.
- **Third-order consequence:** Treated children grow up with their own ideas
  living inside other people's authority.
- **The story:** Teodor, twelve, writes the soil plans for his grandmother's
  cooperative. Bram, forty-four, has been his mouth for three years. Teodor
  learns that Bram's additions, the jokes and the "my father used to say," are
  why the farmers comply. At the vote on the drainage scheme, Teodor wants to
  speak in his own voice. Doing that may cost the harvest.
- **Why this lens:** It treats teaching as an evolved trait, and asks what
  happens when its selective logic, that the old know more, flips.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Cyrano de Bergerac*, in its ghost-voice structure.
  It differs in kind: the cause is a cognitive bias at the scale of a whole
  society. I know of no SF match.

## Card 3: Second Childhood

- **Novum:** The treated cohort is economically productive by sixteen, so
  society gives the long juvenile period to the displaced parents. Adults go
  back to residential school, funded and supervised by their children.
- **Futurity:** F3. Parent and child swap roles: who provides, who plays, who
  sets the curfew.
- **How it works:** The human juvenile period is long because of learning
  under protection (Kaplan et al. 2000, on the embodied-capital model). Play
  is practice (Burghardt 2005). Adult brains stay plastic enough to retrain
  (Maguire et al. 2000; Lövdén et al. 2010). The protected learning years go
  to whoever has the most left to learn.
- **First-order consequence:** Forty-five-year-olds in dormitories, with
  stipends their children countersign.
- **Second-order consequence:** Children become guarantors, holding the purse
  strings and the veto.
- **Third-order consequence:** A generation of adults gets the adolescence
  they skipped, and their kids watch it.
- **The story:** Ruth, sixteen, an engineer, is guarantor for her mother Ana,
  who had her at nineteen. Ana wants to drop out and sail with a man she met
  in her pottery course. Ruth has the power to say no, and she hates sounding
  like the parent she never had. It is a coming of age for both of them.
- **Why this lens:** It asks what the juvenile period is for, then hands it to
  the people who now need it.
- **Suspected prior art:** Role-swap comedies (*Freaky Friday*), and Kress
  again. I know of no case where the parents' generation gets the juvenile
  period as policy.
