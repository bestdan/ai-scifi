# Idea cards: asimov

**Blind spots of this lens.** I build from a rule outward and look for the case
it breaks on, so these cards are strong on mechanism and puzzle and weak on
interiority, sensory texture and the body. I tend to let the idea stand in for
the character, to load dialogue with exposition, and to assume every problem
has a clean solution. Le Guin, Lem and Rajaniemi should push on whether these
people feel like people and whether the answer should stay open.

## Card 1: The Ordinary Reader

- **Novum:** A transition statute makes every decision by a treated official
  valid only once an untreated citizen has read a plain explanation of it and
  signed that they follow it. As the untreated age out, a few treated families
  choose to leave one child untreated, so the law will always have readers.
- **Futurity:** F3. A child is raised for a constitutional office, and what a
  sibling is changes.
- **How it works:** The statute defines "ordinary capacity" by treatment
  status, not by test score, because a test could be gamed. A reader who learns
  more is still ordinary in law.
- **First-order consequence:** Explainers become a profession, and plain
  writing becomes the most valued skill in government.
- **Second-order consequence:** The kept children are courted, lobbied and
  tutored hard.
- **Third-order consequence:** The more she is tutored, the less ordinary she
  really is, and the less the safeguard means.
- **The story:** Teodora, 17, untreated, with three treated siblings, is asked
  to sign a water-rights ruling she half follows. Her brother wrote the
  explanation. Signing keeps the family's standing; refusing exposes the
  safeguard as hollow.
- **Why this lens:** A law with a definition that fails in a case its makers
  didn't foresee.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain*; Chiang, "The Evolution
  of Human Science". Neither has a deliberately untreated child kept for a
  legal role.

## Card 2: Fact and Value

- **Novum:** The first treated cohort grows up under a public compact, taught
  in every school: the young lead on questions of fact, and defer to elders on
  questions of value.
- **Futurity:** F2. Town and nation reorganize around a split of authority by
  question type.
- **How it works:** Each council has a "sorting" step. A mixed bench labels
  each motion fact or value, then routes it to the treated or the elder vote.
- **First-order consequence:** Sorting becomes the real seat of power.
- **Second-order consequence:** Both sides learn to phrase a motion so it lands
  in their own pile.
- **Third-order consequence:** A generation learns to see values hidden inside
  facts, and the compact's line begins to look arbitrary.
- **The story:** Imre, 19, treated, sits on a river town's sorting bench with
  his great-aunt. The question is whether to breach a levee to save the town,
  drowning the orchard she planted. Either label decides the outcome. He finds
  a way to split the motion so each half is honestly one kind. The reader can
  find the split before he does.
- **Why this lens:** A Laws-style ambiguity, resolved by thinking.
- **Suspected prior art:** Asimov's own robot stories in shape. I know of no
  SF that splits governance by fact versus value across generations.

## Card 3: The Moving Mark

- **Novum:** Adulthood is set by examination, not age. The pass mark was
  written into law as the median score of current adults, so the test would
  never need revising.
- **Futurity:** F2. Majority, voting and office now follow a single number.
- **How it works:** Untreated adults were grandfathered in, but holders of
  public office must sit the exam again every ten years. Each year, treated
  passers raise the adult median.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated children reach majority at eleven or
  twelve.
- **Second-order consequence:** The mark climbs every year.
- **Third-order consequence:** Within a decade, no untreated official can pass
  the exam again. A whole generation is pushed out of office by arithmetic
  rather than by any vote.
- **The story:** Saoirse, 16, treated, is a junior examiner. Her grandfather,
  a magistrate, is due to sit the exam. She sees the norming clause, which a
  careful reader sees first. Reporting it gets the mark fixed, but the fix
  raises the age of majority for her own cohort and for her sister, who is due
  to sit it next month.
- **Why this lens:** Society as a system, and a rule whose drift no one
  anticipated.
- **Suspected prior art:** Asimov, "Profession" (exam day); Kornbluth, "The
  Marching Morons". The norm-drift mechanism differs in kind.
