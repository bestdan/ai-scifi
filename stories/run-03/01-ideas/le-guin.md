# Idea cards: le-guin

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens.** I am slow. My cards lean toward institutions,
kinship and talk, and away from plot engines, so they may give a drafter too
little forward motion for 4,000 words. I care little about how the treatment
works or what it costs to make, so mechanism here is thin and should be pushed
on by Egan or Banks. My moral seriousness can tip into a lesson; every card
below needs a critic watching for the sermon.

## Card 1: The Slow Year

- **Novum:** A treated teenager may not hold an adult post until she has
  served a year apprenticed to an untreated elder in a craft that "cannot be
  learned fast": cave-aging cheese, coppicing, reading a tidal flat. The whole
  handover rests on the belief that some knowledge only time can teach.
- **Futurity:** F2. One generation out; adulthood is redefined around the
  treatment, but parent and child keep their old meanings.
- **How it works:** The law was the price untreated voters asked in return
  for letting the treated take office early. The crafts on the list were
  picked because they need seasons, not insight.
- **First-order consequence:** Every treated sixteen-year-old spends a year
  in the mud with someone slower.
- **Second-order consequence:** Dying crafts get a guaranteed apprentice
  stream, and their untreated masters have a standing in public life again.
- **Third-order consequence:** Everyone keeps quiet about whether the year
  is needed, because the elders' dignity depends on it.
- **The story:** A girl apprenticed to an old oysterman works out the whole
  flat in six weeks: the spat cycle, the silt, the weather tells. She has to
  choose whether to say so, which would end the institution, or to spend ten
  months pretending kindly. The coming of age lies in what she finds that
  really is slow: the man himself.
- **Why this lens:** How one becomes an adult. An ambiguous utopia held up
  by a polite fiction, and the person it fails.
- **Suspected prior art:** Apprenticeship stories in general, Earthsea's
  among them. Kress's *Beggars in Spain* does the handover's resentment, not
  its courtesies. Nothing close on a ritual of slowness.

## Card 2: Elder Sister

- **Novum:** Kinship terms come to mark mind, not birth order. In a language
  with ranked sibling words (the setting borrows a Korean- or Hokkien-style
  system), a treated younger brother legally becomes the "elder": he
  inherits first, he speaks for the household, and the untreated older
  sister is now his younger.
- **Futurity:** F3. What "elder" means within a family has changed, in
  grammar and in law.
- **How it works:** The rollout year split families. Courts kept awarding
  care of aging parents to the treated child anyway, and the language
  followed the courts within a decade.
- **First-order consequence:** Children born before the rollout drop a rank
  in their own homes.
- **Second-order consequence:** There are forms of address a grown woman
  must now use to an eleven-year-old, and forms he is shy of using back.
- **Third-order consequence:** Untreated older siblings move to "slow
  towns", where the old grammar still holds.
- **The story:** A nineteen-year-old raised her brother after their mother
  fell ill. On the morning of his naming as elder, she has to decide whether
  to stay as his younger or to leave for a slow town. He doesn't want her to
  go, and has no word left that asks her to stay as what she was to him.
- **Why this lens:** Language makes the world: what these people can no
  longer say.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Beggars in Spain* (sleepless and sleeping twin
  sisters) is the nearest. It is about economics and resentment, not kinship
  grammar. Close on the sibling split, different in kind on the novum.

## Card 3: The Hearer

- **Novum:** No decision by a treated official takes force until it is
  explained to an untreated citizen, a "hearer", who signs that she has
  understood it. Comprehension is the consent of the governed.
- **Futurity:** F2. Institutions are reorganized around the gap; the
  people themselves are still recognizable.
- **How it works:** The hearers are a paid office for the untreated, drawn
  by lot, like jurors. The law asks one question only: did you understand?
  It has no box for "I understood and I refuse".
- **First-order consequence:** Treated officials must learn to explain, and
  explanation becomes the most prized skill of their generation.
- **Second-order consequence:** Hearing becomes the main public work and
  income of the untreated old.
- **Third-order consequence:** Hearers who can't follow sign anyway, to
  keep the stipend and their pride, so the consent is quietly hollow.
- **The story:** A seventeen-year-old's first ruling, on the flooding of a
  valley, goes to her hearer, a retired bus mechanic. He understands it
  completely and won't sign, because he disagrees. The law reads his refusal
  as a failure of her teaching. She can explain it again until he gives in,
  or she can file that he understood, which no hearer has ever been allowed
  to say.
- **Why this lens:** Who pays for this world, and do they consent? What the
  language of the law can't say.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "The Evolution of Human Science" has
  humans interpreting metahuman work, but as scholarship, not governance.
  Jury fiction in general. No close match on comprehension as franchise.
