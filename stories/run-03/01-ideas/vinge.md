# Idea cards: vinge

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front:** I push futurity even where the
story's power is in its human scale, and I reach for systems and schedules
before people. My characters tend to be functions and my plots lean
technothriller. These cards were built on purpose around one household or one
room each, but Le Guin and Chiang should push back on whether the people are
alive or just carrying the mechanism.

## Card 1: Going Down

- **Novum:** Treated people can take a short-acting, fully reversible drug
  that brings their cognition down to the untreated baseline for a few days.
  Families use it ritually so that treated children can be on the same level
  as their parents. By custom, a promise made while down is binding once you
  come back up.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kinship now involves a scheduled change of mind. The
  viewpoint trick pushes toward F4.
- **How it works:** The drug is a receptor antagonist that blunts the
  treatment's effect on synaptic consolidation. The effect wears off in about
  72 hours. Memories formed while down stay, but they stay at the resolution
  they were laid down with.
- **First-order consequence:** Holidays, deathbeds and handovers get "down
  weeks".
- **Second-order consequence:** The binding-promise custom grows up because
  the treated keep overruling what their slower selves agreed to.
- **Third-order consequence:** Some old practices survive only because a
  dimmed heir promised to keep them.
- **The story:** Nell, 17 and treated, is inheriting her untreated mother's
  tidal-mill cooperative. Before the handover she spends a week down beside
  her mother and promises to keep the mill's slow, wasteful rota. Back up,
  she can see it's inefficient. The promise binds. She has to work out what
  her slower self understood that she now can't.
- **Why this lens:** The narrator is the slow one, and the trick is new: a
  superintelligent viewpoint that is temporarily at our level and half
  remembers what it is like up there.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Flowers for Algernon* (temporary intelligence);
  Anderson's *Brain Wave* (the inverse). Neither has voluntary, ritual
  descent.

## Card 2: The Walker

- **Novum:** Any ruling by a treated official that affects an untreated
  citizen has to be "walked": explained to a lay panel of untreated citizens
  until they agree they can follow it. Rulings nobody can walk are void.
  Walking is a treated profession.
- **Futurity:** F2 edging to F3. The institutions are reorganized around
  making the new minds legible to the old ones.
- **How it works:** The rule is a statute, the Legibility Act, passed while
  the untreated still held the majority vote. The panels are drawn by lot,
  like a jury. Walkers train for years in analogy, staging and
  simplification.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated government is slow, and people trust
  it.
- **Second-order consequence:** The treated learn to design policy that can
  be walked, which is not the same thing as the best policy.
- **Third-order consequence:** Two kinds of truth grow up, the true and the
  walkable, and everyone is trained to feel the gap between them.
- **The story:** Ama, 18 and treated, is apprentice walker to the last
  untreated chair of a valley's water tribunal, who retires at the end of the
  month. The ruling in front of them is correct and can't be walked without a
  simplification that is slightly false. If she tells the truth, the ruling
  is void. If she walks the false version, she's the one who starts the
  lying.
- **Why this lens:** Who owns the intelligence, and what the slow side can
  demand of it.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "The Evolution of Human Science" runs in
  the reverse direction: humans interpret the metahumans. Explainable-AI
  debates.

## Card 3: Four Up

- **Novum:** The parents can't keep pace with them, so each yearly cohort of
  treated children is raised and taught by the cohort about four years older.
  A 14-year-old is a senior teacher, and culture turns over on a four-year
  cycle. The treated replace each other as fast as they replaced their
  parents.
- **Futurity:** F3. The words "elder", "parent" and "generation" now mean
  something different. Obsolescence arrives at about nineteen.
- **How it works:** The treated learn fast, and their peers' teaching beats
  their parents'. Each cohort builds tools, slang and methods that the next
  cohort outgrows.
- **First-order consequence:** Schools are run by teenagers.
- **Second-order consequence:** At twenty the treated find themselves where
  their untreated parents were: no longer needed by the young.
- **Third-order consequence:** Untreated parents become the experts in being
  replaced well, and the treated come to them for it.
- **The story:** Ines, 15, is teaching eleven-year-olds who will retire her
  by the time she's 19. She can teach them her fastest methods, which brings
  her own obsolescence closer, or she can hold back. Her untreated mother is
  the only person who has done this before.
- **Why this lens:** *Marooned in Realtime*'s left-behind, squeezed into a
  single childhood.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain*; Vinge, "Fast Times at
  Fairmont High". Neither has teaching between cohorts as its engine.
