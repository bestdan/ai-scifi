# Idea cards: chiang

**Blind spots of this lens.** Low on action, conflict and plot momentum. These
cards are domestic and measured, and they avoid antagonists. They may read too
quiet, or too polished. Let Banks and Watts push for scale and teeth.

## Card 1: The Teach-Back

- **Novum:** The untreated legislature redefined adulthood for treated teenagers: you come of age by teaching one untreated adult something they truly come to understand, and they swear to it.
- **Futurity:** F2. Society has reorganized the age of majority around the treatment.
- **How it works:** Intelligence tests proved useless, since every treated twelve-year-old passes them. Lawmakers wanted a test of whether the young could govern people slower than themselves. A sworn teach-back, with a cross-examination of the learner, is the one proof the old could still check.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated teens apprentice themselves to slow explaining.
- **Second-order consequence:** Untreated adults become sought-after and courted learners, and some sell their oaths.
- **Third-order consequence:** Being understood by your parent becomes a rite of passage, and being misunderstood a legal state.
- **The story:** Iness, sixteen, picks her father, a tiler, who refuses to sign until he actually gets it. She wants to teach him the proof she loves. To get it into his head she has to cut it down to a version she thinks is lesser. That version becomes the one they both share.
- **Form:** Her numbered attempts at the explanation, each shorter than the last, between the scenes.
- **Why this lens:** The question is whether understanding can be given, and what explaining costs the person who explains.
- **Suspected prior art:** "Flowers for Algernon" (numbered reports, inverted here); Chiang's "The Evolution of Human Science." This card differs in kind, because the test is the slower mind's understanding.

## Card 2: Foreseeable

- **Novum:** By fourteen, treated children can predict their untreated parents' words and choices with unsettling accuracy. Courts now ask treated relatives to file "foreseeability statements."
- **Futurity:** F2. Family law and liability are reorganizing around minds that can model their elders.
- **How it works:** No mind-reading is involved. A fast learner gets a whole childhood of data on one person, and humans are more predictable than they like to think. Accuracy runs around nine in ten, never ten.
- **First-order consequence:** Parents stop surprising their children.
- **Second-order consequence:** The treated begin treating the untreated as weather: forgivable, and not quite responsible.
- **Third-order consequence:** Being held to account becomes a courtesy the young must choose to extend.
- **The story:** Tamsin's mother is up for a hearing over a work accident, and Tamsin's statement could excuse her as "foreseeably limited." Tamsin wants to protect her, but excusing her means saying her mother never chose anything. Her mother asks to be blamed.
- **Form:** Before each exchange, Tamsin logs her prediction in brackets. The misses accumulate.
- **Why this lens:** Free will, and whether you can forgive someone whose limits you fully understand.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "What's Expected of Us"; Kress's *Beggars in Spain*. This card is domestic, and its question is responsibility, not determinism.

## Card 3: The Long Minority

- **Novum:** The first treated cohort was drafted into adult work at twelve by overwhelmed untreated institutions. Now thirty and running the legislature, they pass a law keeping their own treated children legally minors until twenty.
- **Futurity:** F3. Childhood stops being a developmental stage and becomes a deliberately granted status.
- **How it works:** Learning takes no time anymore, so childhood can no longer be justified as the time you need to learn. The law reframes it as protected time to be wrong without consequence.
- **First-order consequence:** Second-cohort teenagers fully capable of adult work are barred from it.
- **Second-order consequence:** Their untreated grandparents, out of work, become their companions and co-conspirators.
- **Third-order consequence:** The two generations that were never children together side against the one that was rushed through childhood.
- **The story:** Oriel, fifteen, petitions for exemption to join a bridge project. Her mother, a surgeon at fourteen who still won't talk about it, is the ministry official who will rule on the petition. Oriel wants the work, and her mother wants to give her the childhood she never had. To win, Oriel would have to prove her mother's childhood did her no harm.
- **Form:** Oriel's petition, interleaved with excerpts from her mother's twelve-year-old residency logbook, which Oriel obtains.
- **Why this lens:** The question is what childhood is for, once its old reason is gone.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Ender's Game* (children in adult roles); Kress. Here the enhanced generation is legislating childhood for its own children, which neither book does.
