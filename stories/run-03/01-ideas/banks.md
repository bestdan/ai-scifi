# Idea cards: banks

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front.** It sprawls. It loves a set
piece and will bury a short story under worldbuilding: institutions, offices,
whole bureaucracies of kindness. Short stories are not its natural form. Each
card below has been cut to one institution and one relationship, but Chiang
and Asimov should cut further. Its default shape is the powerful doing dirty
work, which can tip into cynicism. Under this brief's tone, check that the
humour and the warmth survive the draft.

## Card 1: Courtesy Section

- **Novum:** The treated generation, now running things at twenty, keeps a
  quiet civil office whose job is the dignity of the untreated. It arranges
  that their parents' decisions still matter, or appear to: real stakes where
  possible, staged ones where not.
- **Futurity:** F2. Society is reorganized around a handover nobody voted
  on, and that is managed like diplomacy with a proud neighbour.
- **How it works:** Caseworkers model each untreated official's goals, and
  route consequential choices to them where an error is cheap and
  reversible. Where it isn't, they build "soft rails": options pre-pruned so
  that every choice left is a good one.
- **First-order consequence:** The untreated keep working, and keep feeling
  competent.
- **Second-order consequence:** Being handled becomes the untreated
  generation's open secret and its chief etiquette. Nobody asks.
- **Third-order consequence:** The treated grow up fluent in benevolent
  deceit, and can't tell where it stops at home.
- **The story:** Inês, nineteen, gets her first solo case: her own mother, a
  harbour-board chair about to approve a dredging plan that is wrong but
  survivable. Inês can let her mother be wrong and pay for it, or prune it.
  Her mother, it turns out, has been running soft rails on Inês since she
  was four.
- **Why this lens:** Contact turned inward. A benevolent hegemon's dirty
  work, at kitchen-table scale.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Good Bye Lenin!*; Kress, *Beggars in Spain*;
  Banks's own Contact. None aims the managed-dignity apparatus at parents.

## Card 2: The Plain Reader

- **Novum:** No treated drafter can tell what an untreated reader will fail
  to follow. So the handover law gives a veto to "plain readers": untreated
  citizens whose confusion, formally logged, blocks any statute, contract or
  form until it is rewritten.
- **Futurity:** F2. Legibility to the slower mind becomes the constitutional
  check on the faster one.
- **How it works:** Readers are paid, sworn, and drawn by lot from the
  untreated, including the young who missed treatment. A reader marks
  passages "lost". Two marks on the same clause send it back.
- **First-order consequence:** Laws get short and plain. Drafting turns into
  a craft of explanation.
- **Second-order consequence:** Plain readers become the most courted people
  in the country, and drafters learn to write prose that feels clear while
  hiding things.
- **Third-order consequence:** Confusion turns into a kind of honour, and
  feigned confusion into a political weapon.
- **The story:** Dele, seventeen, untreated because his parents refused it,
  is drawn to read the Retirement Act that will replace the old judiciary.
  His treated sister wrote it. He understands every line, and suspects that
  it was built for him to understand. He must decide whether to sign off on
  what he can follow, or mark "lost" on what he senses but can't name.
- **Why this lens:** The most powerful entity needs something it can't take,
  which here is being understood.
- **Suspected prior art:** Plain-language law; *Flowers for Algernon*
  inverted; jury stories. I know no SF where slow comprehension holds the
  veto.

## Card 3: Sign-Off

- **Novum:** By the handover settlement, no treated person may take an
  office. It has to be given: each untreated holder sets a test of their own
  choosing for their successor, and signs off or doesn't.
- **Futurity:** F2. Inheritance of every office becomes a personal
  examination, and the slow generation keeps the last key.
- **How it works:** Tests are unregulated and unappealable, and can't be
  bought out. Most untreated holders sign within a week. A stubborn few set
  tests that learning speed can't shorten.
- **First-order consequence:** The handover runs through ten thousand
  private rituals instead of one election.
- **Second-order consequence:** The hardest tests are the ones that take
  time, such as a season, a full tide cycle, or a funeral attended, and the
  treated begin to learn patience as a credential.
- **Third-order consequence:** A generation that learned everything by
  twelve has to sit through a test it can't finish faster.
- **The story:** Two braided timelines. In one, Mairi at eight is taught by
  an untreated river pilot to read the Clyde. In the other, at twenty-three,
  she is the pilot's successor, refused for a year. The braid reveals that
  the test began on the day of the first lesson.
- **Why this lens:** Structure as a weapon, after *Use of Weapons*, and
  power that has to be given rather than taken.
- **Suspected prior art:** Apprentice tales; *The Karate Kid*'s
  "wax on" reveal is the danger. The braid has to reframe the elder's
  motive, not just the chores.
