# Idea cards: lem

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens:** contempt for genre pleasures readers enjoy; a
pull toward essay over scene; characters who turn into ciphers carrying an
argument; a bleak, cerebral default. The brief asks for honest optimism and
coming-of-age warmth, which this lens doesn't supply on its own. Banks and Le
Guin should push on whether these people are lovable, and the First Reader
should rule on whether each is still a story.

## Card 1: The Ditch Rota

- **Novum:** The treated generation, inheriting the world, is told to audit the old institutions and retire whatever can't justify itself. A treated girl finds that the dumbest system in her valley works for reasons nobody, her included, can recover.
- **Futurity:** F2. Society reorganizes around a decommissioning of adult institutions by their smarter heirs.
- **How it works:** The birth treatment holds the childhood plasticity window open into the twenties. Treated "auditors" get apprenticed to old institutions at fourteen. This one is a 300-year-old irrigation rota, kept in a paper ledger by an untreated ditch boss. It has nineteen rules nobody can explain ("the Moreno gate opens second after a hailstorm").
- **First-order consequence:** Old systems get replaced with models that are provably better on every measure anyone can name.
- **Second-order consequence:** Some replacements fail along dimensions nobody named. The old keepers can't say why, because they never knew either.
- **Third-order consequence:** A generation raised to understand everything meets the first thing it can't take apart: an artifact made by duller minds over centuries.
- **The story:** Inés, fifteen, has a model that adds 18% to the yield. It needs three of the nineteen rules cut. The ditch boss is retiring. Inés has to sign off the switch, run the old rota without knowing why it works, or find a third way.
- **Why this lens:** An uninterpretable artifact, with the smart reading the slow as alien.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chesterton's fence (an essay, not a story); Chiang's "Catching Crumbs from the Table" runs the other way round. No close match that I know of.

## Card 2: Report Cards

- **Novum:** Treated children teach the retraining classes for displaced adults. The story is told entirely as the term reports one twelve-year-old writes on her adult class, her father among them.
- **Futurity:** F3. Parent and child swap roles inside a public institution, with grades.
- **How it works:** The treated children learn about four times as fast. Untreated workers aged thirty to sixty are retrained by whoever learns the material quickest, and that's children. The form is a document: an official template with fields for "Progress", "Effort" and "Teacher's comments". The comments grow longer and less official over three terms.
- **First-order consequence:** Children hold formal authority over adults' livelihoods.
- **Second-order consequence:** Families take the classroom home. A father gets graded by the girl he still makes lunch for.
- **Third-order consequence:** The teacher learns what can't be taught at speed. She also learns that her father is actually bad at this, and that knowing so doesn't give her the right to say it.
- **The story:** Dorota wants her father to pass, because otherwise he loses the depot job. He won't. Her choice is between an honest report and a kind one, on an official form.
- **Why this lens:** A non-story form whose gaps carry the story, and the comedy of a bureaucracy faced with an inverted family.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Flowers for Algernon* is told as progress reports. This inverts it: the reports are written by the clever child about a slow parent, and nobody's intelligence changes. The form echoes Keyes, but the story is a different kind of story.

## Card 3: Nine Thousand Pages

- **Novum:** The treated cohort wins the legal right to take over the town council at eighteen, but it can't stop drafting the charter for the handover. Each clever mind foresees more failure modes than the last, and none of them will vote for a document they can prove is flawed.
- **Futurity:** F2. Institutions reorganize around a generation that can't act without proof.
- **How it works:** Intelligence raises the rate at which objections are found faster than the rate at which they're resolved. The charter has nine thousand pages of contingencies. Meanwhile the untreated mayor, sixty-one, keeps running the town on thin justifications that are mostly fine.
- **First-order consequence:** The handover is legally done and practically stalled.
- **Second-order consequence:** The duller adults keep governing because they're willing to be wrong.
- **Third-order consequence:** The cohort finds out that deciding is a skill in its own right, apart from thinking, and that their parents have it.
- **The story:** Tomasz, eighteen, is the charter's clerk. He can see seven flaws in the current draft. The mayor has a bad heart and a boat. Tomasz has to sign something he knows is wrong, or let the man he's replacing die in office.
- **Why this lens:** Satire of rational ambition, close to a Cyberiad constructor's fable, played warm.
- **Suspected prior art:** Anderson's *Brain Wave* and Kress's *Beggars in Spain* deal with the politics of enhanced minds, but neither is about paralysis by foresight. Lem's *Cyberiad* has the tone but not the premise.
