# Weather Report

The buildings office has the sixth floor of a glass block on the Wicker, and on Tuesday the third of June I found the fault in the stairwell before ten.

There are forty desks on the sixth floor. Thirty-eight of them have teenagers at them, checking plans for the city: every new block, extension and loft in Sheffield comes past us before anyone pours anything.

Everyone born since 2043 is treated. Since that year nearly every pregnant woman in the country has been given an injection, and it makes the baby far cleverer than its parents and lets it learn several times faster than they ever could. Mum was born in 2018, before there was an injection, so she's untreated, like every adult her age. School for us is a two-year course you finish at about eleven, and by thirteen most of us are at work. I was fifteen that June and I had been checking structures for two years.

The other two desks are at the far end, by the kettle. My old maths teacher sits at one, and the woman who used to run the whole floor sits at the other. They do the second read: every plan we pass goes down to them, and they go through it again with a ruler and a pad of yellow notes, and by the time they've signed it the foundations are usually in. Nobody waits for the second read. They bring a tin of biscuits in on Fridays.

The stairwell was in a seven-storey block of flats for Attercliffe. The drawings came in at twenty to ten and I saw it at nineteen minutes to. The landings were cast into the concrete core at one end and rested on the steel frame at the other. Concrete goes on shrinking for years after it's poured, and steel doesn't, so the core would sink a few millimetres against the frame, slowly, for decades. Somewhere around 2090 the fifth-floor landing would crack along its steel end, and a man carrying shopping up it would feel the step go soft under his foot. I can set that out now in a paragraph. At the time I just looked at the drawing and the landing was wrong.

I sent it back to the architects with a fix, a sliding bearing at the steel end, and sat there wanting to tell someone. Out of the window was the viaduct. It runs across the bottom of the valley, a quarter of a mile of brick arches that nobody has run a train over since before Mum was born, and its parapet is level with my eyes when I sit up straight. I've checked it on paper, the whole length, from the 1840s drawings the archive scanned, and I can't improve it. There's nothing else in the city I can say that about. That week a crew had started a scaffold against one of the middle arches, for repointing, and every morning it was a level higher.

Priya Shah, who runs my section and is sixteen, came past with her tea and read my note in the time it took her to blow on it.

"Good," she said. "Nice. And if it had been on someone else's desk you'd still have had to report it. Any safety risk you know of, any site in the city. That's the first rule on this floor." She says it to everyone, about once a week, because she's anxious. "Yorkshire will be two hundred for one by lunch, by the way. Flat pitch, no cloud."

At lunch Yorkshire were thirty-one for four. Priya read the score out across the floor and laughed until she had to put her sandwich down. She is wrong about cricket every single day and it is her favourite thing.

On the Thursday the Attercliffe drawings came back up from the end desks with a yellow note stuck to the stair sheet, in my old teacher's careful capitals: LANDING BEARING, QUERY? He'd found it. It had taken him two days with a ruler, and he knew my fix had gone out on Tuesday, because it was stapled to the front. He'd written the note anyway. It's the job he's got left, and he does it properly.

On Monday the ninth Priya came to my desk at a quarter past eleven holding her phone out and said, "It's your mum," which she has never said before.

"We've had one come off," Mum said. "The wind. Kyle's gone down a level and done his leg. He's all right. He's swearing. I'm going with him to the Northern General."

It was blowing hard that morning; you could see it on the viaduct, where the repointing crew's sheeting was cracking out like a sail. Priya told me to go. On the tram I worked out how far Kyle had fallen. A level on a scaffold is two metres. He'd landed on the boards of the level below.

His leg was broken below the knee, both bones, and pinned by teatime. He was fifty-four and grey and propped up in a bay of six beds with his leg in a frame, and when I came round the curtain he was showing a nurse a photograph on his wife's phone. His wife is Bev. She had brought the pictures from the allotment, because he'd asked for them before he asked for anything else.

"Look at that," Kyle said to me. "Eighteen inches. Tell Frey the leeks are fine. That's all I care about."

The form was in my inbox at work on the Monday. I read it at my desk with the viaduct out of the window and the scaffold on the middle arch nearly at the parapet.

It took me four minutes, and the law behind it took me the rest of the morning. Here it is in plain words, a step at a time.

The treated took over the schedules and the designs. The untreated kept doing the work. When something went wrong, the untreated got the blame, for failing at systems they couldn't follow.

The courts thought that was unfair. So from 2056 they started asking the person who knows a worker's mind best whether the worker could have chosen otherwise. For an untreated adult that person is usually a treated child, because nobody can predict you like your kid. The courts called it mercy. They also called it evidence.

If the child says no, the worker couldn't have chosen otherwise, the worker is cleared, and the blame goes to the firm. The court takes a "couldn't" without checking it, because it protects the worker.

If the child says yes, the worker could have, the worker can be found negligent, fined and suspended. The court checks a "could" against the child's written record, because it exposes the worker to blame.

The catch is in the first one. The court reasons like this. If you couldn't choose this time, you can't be trusted to choose next time. So the court records you as limited, and a limited person can never again sign for anything that people's safety depends on. The boy on my floor's dad stacks shelves now.

It doesn't fall on the treated. We rarely sign anything before sixteen, and nobody can predict us as we predict our parents. The untreated can't keep up with us, and the treated vary on purpose when they know they're being modelled.

What the court wanted from me was called a statement: my written answer, as the person who knew her best, to whether Freya Pike could have done otherwise on the sixth of June. The form said in bold that a statement must come with the witness's whole written record of predictions about the person, if one exists. Mine existed.

If I wrote that she couldn't have done otherwise, she'd be cleared, and she'd never sign for a scaffold again. Clearing her and recording her as limited wouldn't be two things the court did. They'd be one thing.

- **Stakes.** Scene 5: "If I wrote that she couldn't have chosen otherwise, she'd be cleared and the firm would take the blame ... And Mum would be limited for the rest of her life ...
