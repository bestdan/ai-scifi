# Weather Report

The buildings office has the sixth floor of a glass block on the Wicker, and on Tuesday the third of June I found the fault in the stairwell before ten.

There are forty desks on the sixth floor. Thirty-eight of them have teenagers at them, checking plans for the city: every new block, extension and loft in Sheffield comes past us before anyone pours anything. Everyone born since 2043 is treated. Mum was born in 2018. School for us is a two-year course you finish at about eleven, and by thirteen most of us are at work. I was fifteen that June and I had been checking structures for two years.

The other two desks are at the far end, by the kettle. My old maths teacher sits at one of them, and the woman who used to run the whole floor sits at the other. They do the second read. Every plan we pass goes down to them, and they go through it again with a ruler and a pad of yellow notes, and sign a sheet, and by the time they've signed it the foundations are usually in. Nobody waits for the second read. They know that. They bring in a tin of biscuits on Fridays and put it on the printer for everyone.

The stairwell was in a seven-storey block of flats for Attercliffe. The drawings came in at twenty to ten and I saw it at nineteen minutes to. The landings were cast into the concrete core at one end and rested on the steel frame at the other. Concrete goes on shrinking for years after it's poured, and steel doesn't, so the core would sink a few millimetres against the frame, slowly, for decades. Somewhere around 2090 the fifth-floor landing would crack along its steel end, and a man carrying shopping up it would feel the step go soft under his foot.

I can set that out now in a paragraph. At the time there was no chain of reasoning to set out. I looked at the drawing and the landing was wrong.

I wrote it up and sent it back to the architects with a fix, a sliding bearing at the steel end, and then I sat there wanting to tell someone. Priya Shah, who runs my section and is sixteen, came past with her tea and read my note in the time it took her to blow on it.

"Good," she said. "Nice. Yorkshire will be two hundred for one by lunch, by the way. Flat pitch, no cloud."

At lunch Yorkshire were thirty-one for four. Priya read the score out across the floor and laughed until she had to put her sandwich down. She is wrong about cricket every single day and it is her favourite thing.

She'd have found the stairwell herself in two minutes, so telling her didn't count. The only people on the floor it would have taken a week were the two at the end desks, and you don't tell your old maths teacher that.

On the Thursday the Attercliffe drawings came back up from the end desks with a yellow note stuck to the stair sheet, in his careful capitals: LANDING BEARING, QUERY? He'd found it. It had taken him two days with a ruler, and my fix had gone out on Tuesday morning, and he knew it had, because it was stapled to the front. He'd written the note anyway. It's the job he's got left, and he does it properly.

From my desk I can see the viaduct. It runs across the bottom of the valley, a quarter of a mile of brick arches that nobody has run a train over since before Mum was born, and its parapet is exactly level with my eyes when I sit up straight. I've checked it on paper, the whole length, from the 1840s drawings the archive scanned. I can't improve it. There's nothing else in the city I can say that about. That week a crew had started putting up a scaffold against one of the middle arches, for repointing, and every morning when I came in it was a level higher.

That evening I wrote an entry in my log about Friday. I'll come to it.

It took me four minutes, and the law it came from took me the rest of the morning, so here it is in plain words. After the treated took over the schedules and the designs, the untreated people who did the work kept being blamed for not keeping up with systems they couldn't follow. The courts didn't like it.
