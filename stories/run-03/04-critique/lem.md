# Stage 4 critique: Lem lens

**Blind spots, stated.** I undervalue scene and warmth, and I am suspicious of
the genre pleasures this brief asks for (coming-of-age, optimism). Weigh
Banks, Le Guin and the First Reader against me on whether it is still a story.
I say nothing here about the biology or the arithmetic.

1. **[must-fix] The only thing intelligence does in this world is predict.**
   Sixty IQ points is shown as one skill, and Kofi and Priya are ordinary
   teenagers with pigeons and cricket. The treated are humans with a
   spreadsheet. Give the gap one moment that isn't prediction: something
   Tamsin sees or does at the buildings office that her mother can't be told,
   and Tamsin can't translate. The reader should leave with a sense that
   fifteen-year-old minds work in ways the page does not unpack. Not an
   explanation, one unexplained competence (for instance, how she reads the
   viaduct plan).

2. **[must-fix] The "nine in ten" is self-graded, and nobody notices.** The
   log is Tamsin's own notebook, scored by Tamsin ("Tick", "Miss"). The court
   treats it as evidence of a mind. That is the comic centre, the vanity to
   puncture, and the bible plays it straight. Let one person (Gaz, the firm's
   lawyer, or the judge) ask who marked it. Tamsin has no good answer. A
   prediction she scored as a tick may have been Mum's own choice that merely
   resembled it.

3. **[must-fix] The law's logic is a non sequitur, and the story has to see
   it.** "Predictable, therefore couldn't have chosen otherwise" is the
   world's folly. Tamsin's error is meant to be that very equation, but the
   ending corrects her cleanly: she files "could have chosen otherwise" and
   the court agrees. That resolves too much. Keep the law absurd and
   unrepaired: the judge's ruling should be right for the wrong reason, or
   leave the question open whether the 3 June prediction proves
   responsibility (she foresaw it) or the reverse (it was foreseeable).

4. **[should] The last "Miss" is a feel-good epiphany.** "Tamsin logs it as a
   miss and is glad" is the style guide's banned "single-paragraph epiphany"
   in a nice coat. The bible's own line, that she doesn't know whether she'll
   be surprised again, is better. Suggest that the final surprise is
   something Tamsin cannot classify: she writes neither "Tick" nor "Miss",
   because she can't tell if Mum chose it or merely drifted. Ledger row
   "Ending" stays explicit through the ruling; only the last beat is
   left unscored.

5. **[should] Let the log be the form, not just a tic.** The brackets-and-ticks
   device is the right instinct. Push it: one entry should be a prediction
   about a person Tamsin has no data on (the judge), marked "no basis", and
   one a prediction about herself. A fragment such as the 3 June entry
   quoted verbatim (under the 150-word limit) does more than narration;
   consider giving the court letter and the ruling the same dry, quoted
   treatment, with no narrator gloss.

6. **[could] Irony budget.** Priya's rule-bound certainty is the funniest
   thing here (a sixteen-year-old boss of her old teachers) and has one
   line in the bible. Give her one scene where she is confidently, checkably
   wrong. It keeps the treated from being flatteringly right about everything
   except love.

**Ledger check.** Every row can be made explicit as written without
explaining anything alien away, because the story's alien element is the
institution's reasoning, not a mind. The risk runs the other way: the ledger
is so complete that the world's confusion has no place to live. Keep rows 3
and 4 explicit and leave the law's soundness unexplained.
