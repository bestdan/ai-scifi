# Synthesis: run-03

Four premises, one per shortlisted option in `02-audit.md`. Every premise
keeps the brief's seed as written: the children are treated in utero or at
birth, they are far smarter and faster-learning than the untreated adults
around them, and the story shows that gap on the page. Each premise steers
off the audit's attractors: no water or shoreline setting, no explainer
institution at the core, no elder whose slow knowledge wins the argument, no
benevolent deception that the elder knew about all along, and no Ines.

## A. The Witness (Majority × The Witness)

The treatment speeds the brain's learning but not the slower maturing of
judgement and impulse, so a treated child thinks like a brilliant adult at
nine and still goes through adolescence at thirteen to sixteen. Two laws
follow. Any treated official under eighteen makes consequential calls with
an untreated adult in the room, because teenagers take worse risks when no
adult is watching, even one who understands nothing. And a board can put a
teenage official on "adolescence leave" when her judgement is in doubt.
Nadia, fourteen, chairs the city's power-grid board through a week-long heat
wave. Her assigned witness is her father, a line worker who can't follow a
word of her load models. When a rival board member moves to put her on
leave at the height of the crisis, the board asks the one person the law
trusts on that question: her father. He knows she hasn't slept and has just
had a blow-up with her best friend; she can see, three moves ahead, that he
is about to lie and say she's fine. She must choose whether to let him
perjure himself to keep her in the chair, or to take herself off the crisis
she alone can solve.

- **Plain pitch:** Kids born with a treatment that makes them far smarter
  than their parents run the city's power grid by fourteen, but they still
  go through ordinary teenage moods, so the law puts an ordinary adult in
  the room to steady them. In a heat wave, a girl who outthinks everyone
  has to decide whether to let her father lie that she's fine, or admit
  she's not and hand the crisis to someone worse.
- **Seed:** holds. She outthinks the board and her father; his only standing
  is having been fourteen.
- **Strengths:** the strongest mechanism in the run (real research on
  adolescent risk); a ticking clock; the gap is visible in every scene.
- **Risks:** infrastructure jargon; the father could become the wise elder
  who knows best, which the audit names as the drift to avoid.
- **Load:** one novum (the treatment's split clocks), with the witness and
  leave laws as its consequences; no twist.
- **Estimated minimum:** 3,000 words, about 4,000 with the crisis plot.

## B. The Mouth (Elder Sister × The Mouth)

People learn from those they see as elders: older, higher status, listened
to by others. That bias doesn't switch off because a child is right. So in a
hill town of pepper and tea terraces, the plans of treated children reach
the adults through hired grown-ups who speak them as their own, called
"mouths". The law has gone further than the habit: the treated child is now
the household's legal head, whatever his birth order. Joon, eleven, treated
at birth, has spent two years designing the cooperative's switch to a
blight-resistant crop. His sister Mi-rae, twenty, who raised him, is his
mouth; the farmers trust her, and her additions, the jokes and the "our
mother used to say", are part of why they comply. At the vote that decides
the switch, Joon wants to stand up and speak in his own voice. If he does,
the farmers may refuse a child and lose the season. If he doesn't, his
sister stays the public owner of his mind, and he stays a secret.

- **Plain pitch:** Children born smarter than any adult still can't get
  grown-ups to take their advice, so their ideas go out through hired
  adults who pass them off as their own. An eleven-year-old whose plan
  could save his village's harvest has to decide whether to speak for
  himself and risk being ignored, or keep hiding behind the sister who
  raised him.
- **Seed:** holds. Joon's plans are the town's best thinking; the adults
  can't learn from him directly.
- **Strengths:** a fresh institution grounded in real psychology; a warm
  sibling pair; the gap is in every exchange.
- **Risks:** the sister's skill as a mouth could read as "the adult's
  wisdom wins"; Korean kinship terms could become untranslated jargon.
- **Load:** one novum and its institution; the legal-head law is
  background. No twist.
- **Estimated minimum:** 3,000 words, about 4,000.

## C. Foreseeable

A treated child gets a whole childhood of data on one person, and people are
more predictable than they like to believe. By fourteen the treated can
predict their untreated parents' words and choices nine times in ten, and
the courts have noticed. In liability hearings they now ask treated
relatives for a "foreseeability statement": could this person have chosen
otherwise? Tamsin, fifteen, keeps a running log of her predictions about her
mother, a scaffolding foreman. When a scaffold her mother signed off on
collapses and injures a worker, Tamsin's statement could clear her: her
mother "foreseeably" couldn't have judged the load. Her mother asks Tamsin
not to file it. She wants to be blamed, because being excused means she
never chose anything. Then the court asks for Tamsin's log, and it shows
that Tamsin predicted the shortcut her mother would take, the week before.

- **Plain pitch:** Children born far smarter than their parents can predict
  them nine times in ten, so courts now ask them whether a parent could
  have done otherwise. A girl can clear her mother of a workplace accident
  by swearing her mother couldn't have known better, but her mother wants
  to be held responsible, and the girl's own notes show she saw it coming.
- **Seed:** holds strongly. The prediction gap is the novum.
- **Strengths:** a clean philosophical question with humane stakes; a
  natural formal device (the bracketed predictions); the twist grows from
  the novum.
- **Risks:** the legal procedure could become exposition; a courtroom
  register could lose the coming-of-age frame.
- **Load:** one novum and one twist (the log).
- **Estimated minimum:** 4,500 words.

## D. The Long Minority (Long Minority × Second Childhood)

The first treated cohort was drafted into adult work at twelve by
overwhelmed institutions. Now thirty and running the government, they keep
their own treated children legal minors until twenty, as protected time to
be wrong, and send their displaced untreated parents back to funded
residential school. Oriel, fifteen, petitions for an exemption to join a
rail-tunnel project she has already redesigned on paper. Her mother, a
surgeon at fourteen who won't talk about those years, is the ministry
official who will rule on the petition. To win, Oriel has to prove to her
mother that her mother's rushed childhood did her no harm, and Oriel's
grandmother, back at school at sixty-eight, has a residency logbook from
those years that says otherwise.

- **Plain pitch:** The first generation of super-smart kids was put to work
  at twelve, and now that they run the country they won't let their own
  children work until twenty. A fifteen-year-old who has already
  out-designed the engineers has to win her exemption from the official
  who decides it: her mother, who won't say what being rushed cost her.
- **Seed:** delays the handover by law. Acceptable only as an obstacle that
  Oriel visibly outthinks.
- **Strengths:** three generations at once; a strong mother-daughter
  conflict.
- **Risks:** the law softens the gap; two institutions to establish; the
  grandmother's logbook leans toward the elder-knowledge cluster.
- **Load:** two nova (the long minority and second childhood) and a reveal.
- **Estimated minimum:** 6,000 words.

## Decision

**C, Foreseeable**, revised. A consultant concurred (64%, a judgement call):
C's tension is the easiest to state, its core idea ("she can finish her
mother's sentences") explains itself at a kitchen table, and the gap is the
novum, so the seed holds on every page.

How it answers the panel's warnings:

- **Banks: a hearing room, no world to stand in.** The court gets a few
  pages at most. Most scenes are at home, on the street and on the work
  site. Tamsin, fifteen, already works: she is a junior structural checker
  at the city buildings office, the kind of job her treated generation is
  taking from people like her mother. That is the brief's handover
  direction. She is explicitly not on her mother's file.
- **Chiang and the consultant: the twist as a gotcha, and Tamsin's
  negligence.** The prediction that incriminates her is domestic, made at
  the dinner table ("she'll skip the second tie-in, because the crew's
  late"), not an inspection she failed. The log's hits and misses are
  planted at home first, so the court's request lands as dread.
- **Seed inversion.** The mother never out-predicts Tamsin. Her one power
  is to act against the forecast.
- **Tone.** The injured worker breaks a leg and recovers. No default
  darkness.
- **Comprehension (run-02's failure).** The legal idea is first explained
  in a scene, in plain words, before the court's short letter names it.
  "Foreseeability statement" never appears without plain words beside it.
- **Vinge's push** (higher accuracy, and the court wanting her unspent
  forecasts) is declined as the core, since it adds load; the unspent
  forecasts may appear in one line.

**Declined:** A (the father as wise elder risk, present-day setting), B
(runner-up; coined "mouth" plus Korean kin terms is run-02's slang risk, and
*Ender's Game* owns the device), D (delays the seed by law, two institutions,
6,000 words).
