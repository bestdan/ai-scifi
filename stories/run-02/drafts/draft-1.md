# Strike Note

## 16 September

The loam was on the board before six. Davi heard the strickle as he came down the stairs: the pivot squeaking on its iron post each time the arm came round, and under it the radio on the windowsill, playing the same forró it always played.

His mother didn't look round. Ceiça walked the board round the core with both hands flat on the arm, and the loam came off the edge of the profile in a long curl and dropped on her boots. The core stood waist high in the pit, brick and straw and loam baked hard over a basket of coke, and on it she was building the false bell, the clay shape of the bronze to come. It was for the Rosário church. The tower's high bell had cracked in July and had rung like a frying pan since, and the brotherhood wanted the new one hung for the feast.

"Clean profile," he said. "Hair fat at the shoulder."

It was, by two millimetres, maybe less. At camp in Belo Horizonte, on a slow afternoon, he had run her drawing through a model and it had given him every partial of the finished bell to the cent. He could have told her where the strike note would sit before there was any bronze to strike. His mother had never once pretended to follow his models. When he'd shown her the partials on the screen she had said that's nice, son, in the voice she kept for the priest's slides at Lent, and gone back to the board, and the bell had come out of the pit right anyway.

She walked the board round once more. The shoulder came true, and she stopped.

"You want it?"

He took the arm. The board was a plank of ipê cut to the curve of the bell and shod with a steel edge, heavier than it looked, and the loam wanted it moving at one speed, the loam's speed, which nobody had ever given him as a number. He walked it round. At the waist the edge caught and jumped and opened a tear the width of his palm, down to the straw.

"Bricked," he said. "Zero chance. Fully bricked."

Ceiça put her hand in the slurry bucket, packed the tear with two fingers, took the arm back and walked it round twice. The waist was smooth. She wiped her fingers on her thigh.

"Loam you can put back," she said. "Nobody puts metal back. Go and wash, your grandfather's ringing at twelve."

He was in the yard with the hose on his boots when the Rosário tower spoke over the roofs.

Low first: a death. Three: a man. Four high: of the Rosário brotherhood. One low: that is all. The shoemaker on Rua da Cadeia, then, who had been dying since June. The high strokes came out of the cracked bell with a buzz on them, like a lid on a pan.

Vô Zé, his grandfather, came in at a quarter past with rope fibre on his palms and sat down at the head of the table without washing them, because he was seventy-eight and had rung for the brotherhood since before the treatment had a name. Lia was already there. She was nine, his cousin, his aunt's girl, at their table every Sunday, and she had laid the spoons in a spiral.

"Did him slow," Vô Zé said. "He liked things slow." He took beans and then more beans. "On the low one. You hear the low one first, you've lost somebody before you've even counted. That's why. Pitch before number. And if you hear one low stroke dropped in the middle of a feast, somebody in that house is going. Not gone. Going."

"Pitch before number," Lia said, in his exact rasp.

"Four hundred deaths I've rung for the Rosário," he said.

"Four hundred and twelve." Lia didn't look up from her angu. "You said three hundred and ninety at Easter, and there've been twenty-two."

Vô Zé looked at the ceiling as if the number were written there in a hand he didn't trust.

Ceiça came in from the foundry and sat down with loam still in the creases of her knuckles, and asked nobody in particular whether the couve had been washed, and ate.

"Three weeks Sunday," Vô Zé said. "The fuss."

"The Entrega," Ceiça said.

"The fuss. I gave her the foundry in a kitchen. This kitchen." He tapped the oilcloth. "Shook her hand, she made coffee. Two thousand and thirty-eight. Nobody rang anything."

"You cried," Ceiça said.

"I had a cold."

"Has he been to the clinic?" Vô Zé asked her, as if Davi were out in the yard.

"Wednesday," Ceiça said.

"Seat's mine on the seventh," Davi said. "Obviously. Mãe stands down, I take the vote and the licence, done. Clean handover."

His mother ate her beans. Nobody said anything about the waist of the Rosário bell. It sat on the table between them with the salt.

"Lia," he said. "What do you think?"

"Advice is one real."

He found a coin. She put it under her plate.

"Take the seat," she said. "Zero chance otherwise. Otherwise it's bricked."

Vô Zé put his spoon down. "Where did she get bricked?"

"I've always said bricked," Davi said.

"Since Thursday," his mother said.

"Don't ask the open for advice, Dadá," said Vô Zé, who was the last person who called him that. "They give you your own back with a ribbon on it."

After lunch Davi stood in front of the brotherhood's calendar by the stove, the one with the Virgin of the Rosary in a blue mantle over the months, and counted. Sixteen to thirty was fourteen. Seven more to the feast. Twenty-one days.

## 19 September

The setting clinic was a room at the back of the health post, past the vaccine fridge and a poster about dengue. Someone in the next room was playing a cello, the same phrase over and over, and every time it reached the same high note it missed it, stopped, and went back. When he knocked, the cello stopped.

Rafaela Brum came through the connecting door with rosin on her sleeve. She was the town's setting counsellor and she sat on the câmara, and she was twenty-four, and her vowels were the wide slow vowels of the first treated kids in the old news clips. She pointed at a chair.

"Davi Ribeiro. Rosário feast. Bell founders." She had a folder. "Brabo. Sit."

Nobody had said brabo for good since he was about eight. The pack at school did her voice behind her back at the fountain, and he had done it too, and it hadn't been kind.

"I have to read you this," she said. "The law says the whole thing, aloud. Don't help."

She read it flat, at the speed of a woman reading out a bus timetable.

"'Lei da Entrega, 2052, Annex Three. Statement to be read in full to the heir before the agent known as firma is dispensed. One. Whereas the experience of the municipal councils in the period 2049 to 2052 has shown that members whose critical periods remain unclosed are liable to revise their positions in favour of the most recent speaker, to a degree incompatible with the continuity of public administration; and whereas the untreated parents of such persons, having been offered no occasion for the dignified surrender of their offices, have in many cases retained those offices beyond the point of competence and been circumvented, to their distress; the Congress establishes that a seat, licence or office with tenure shall pass on a fixed day, by rite, and only to an heir certified closed.'"

She turned the page.

"'Two. In the untreated brain a mesh of extracellular proteins, hereafter the nets, forms around certain neurons in childhood, and fixes the wiring for hearing, accent, movement and the use of the hands. The treatment administered in the first week of life prevents the nets from forming. It further maintains the adolescent sensitivity to the social surroundings, hereafter the pull, which in the untreated subsides in early adulthood. The mechanism of the second effect is less well understood than that of the first.'" She looked up. "That's the honest sentence. They fought about that one for a month. 'Three. Firma, administered as three tablets on three consecutive days, induces the formation of the nets and ends the pull. The nets harden over the six or seven days following the final tablet, hereafter the curing period, and the subject's state during that period is retained. The process is irreversible. Four. Subject to Annex Five, the heir may...'"

She stopped. She looked at page two, which was all subclauses, and at the clock over the door, and then she initialled the bottom of both pages and wrote *read in full* beside her initials.

"You know all that," she said.

"Since I was six."

"So. Things not on the sheet." She put the folder down. "I sat in the assembly for my father's pharmacy for five months in 'forty-nine. Open. We tried pledges. You sign on Monday that you'll vote for the reservoir whatever anyone says. Thursday someone talks to you in the bakery, and you sign a paper taking it back. Next week you take back the taking back. We had a folder of them this thick." She showed him with her finger and thumb. "That's why the sheet."

"I know about the weathervane years."

"You know the word. So. Your case." She counted on her fingers, and he thought she was quick and dry and a little bored with him, and liked her for it. "You take the first tablet by the twenty-eighth, the second on the twenty-ninth, the third on the thirtieth. A week to cure. I certify you on the sixth if you've cured, and on the seventh you take the seat. Your mother stands down and everybody says nice things about her. After, your hands learn like mine. Like anyone's."

"Like yours."

She tipped her head at the wall, where the cello was.

"I started at twenty. Set. Four years now." She didn't say how it was going. He had heard how it was going.

"And if I don't."

"The rite's once a year, feast day. You can take next year's. Until then your mother holds the licence past your feast, and the town has a word for a parent who does that. You know the word."

"Is there a way back? After."

"None yet." She said it carefully, as if each word cost something. "There's a lab in Campinas that opens the nets again in rats, with an enzyme. The rats forget the maze. Everything the nets were holding goes. So, none yet."

"Right."

"One more thing, and then you can go. Whoever you stand next to in the curing week, that's who you sound like. For life. Not slang. The vowels, the tune. I set beside my boyfriend." She opened her hands. "Listen to me. Brabo."

"Where's he now?"

"Recife. Six years."

"Did you choose him? To set beside."

She thought about it, which he hadn't expected.

"I was sixteen and open," she said. "I chose whatever he'd said last." She picked up the folder. "Go home and think. Not in the square. At home."

He sat on the steps of the health post in the sun with the notebook he kept for the bells, and it was obvious, brabo obvious. The hands were the trade. Set now, he'd take a seat and a licence with hands that couldn't strickle a waist. Stay open one year and the hands learned at a child's speed. The town would talk. Let it. He wrote in the notebook in pencil, in the one hand that had never been anyone's but his:

High and low, turn and turn: the vigil. The doors stay open all night.

Behind him, through the wall, the cello started the phrase again and missed the note.

## 22 September

The pack was at the fountain at lunch, eleven of them from the state school, sitting on the rim with their feet in the dry basin, eating pão de queijo out of one paper bag, and the talk was already going when he got there, round and over itself, nobody finishing a sentence because somebody else had already finished it better: the bus, the overnight bus not the day bus, a hostel in Savassi, somebody's aunt in Funcionários with a floor, a robotics lab that took the open for summer work, Christmas, why Christmas, because everyone's home at Christmas and the city's empty and the labs are hungry, money split eleven ways was nothing, that's ceiling, that's actually ceiling, and he heard himself say ceiling, and laughed, and they all laughed, and it was done. Belo Horizonte at Christmas, all of them, the open, for as long as it was good. He was in.

A boy from the year below did Rafaela's vowels, and somebody else did "Brabo, sit," and it went round the rim, and he did it too.

One of the untreated boys from his year sat on the far side of the basin with his own bag, following about one word in three. When he laughed it was late, and he laughed anyway.

Walking off down Rua Direita afterwards with the crumbs on his shirt, Davi tried to find the place where it had been decided. Nobody had proposed it. Nobody had said yes. He could have reproduced every sentence, and none of the sentences was the one where it happened.

His mother had sent him for meat. He went to Seu Tonho's because it was nearer.

Seu Tonho, the butcher, was sixty-one, untreated, and the town's one agarrado. His licence hung over the counter in a gold frame with a sprig of rue tucked behind it against the evil eye, and in the glass case below it the good cuts, the picanha and the cupim, had been there long enough to go dark at the edges. The mince tray was nearly empty.

"Ribeiro," Tonho said. "Rosário boy. This feast?"

"Maybe next."

Tonho laughed, a short bark, and wrapped a kilo of mince without being asked. "The boy waits, the mother's agarrada. Ask me." He pushed the parcel across. "They buy my mince. Mince they'll buy off an agarrado. Not the picanha. The picanha they buy from the supermarket, from a girl of twenty-two who's set and has never cut a cow."

"Why don't you hand it on?"

"To who?" He wiped his hands. "My Kátia never set. She's a cata-vento. Goes with the wind. Belém in March. Before that Porto Alegre. You can't give a licence to the open, and she's open, so I hold it, and every feast I'm still holding it, and they look." His phone lay on the block beside the scale, face up. "She left me a voice message in August."

"What did she say?"

"I haven't played it." He picked up the cleaver and put it down again. "Then I'd know what she sounds like now."

Walking home with the mince, Davi thought about the cutter. A model on a cutter, his model, would tune truer than any hand alive, his mother's included; there were lathes in São Paulo that did exactly that and sold the bells cheaper. He said so out loud, to Rua do Carmo. A dog on a doorstep watched him go by. Nobody answered.

## 26 September

The bell had been poured on the twentieth, at four in the morning, when the bronze in the crucible came up the colour Ceiça called cor de manga and she said now, and it went down the runner into the mould with a sound like frying, and the whole yard smelled of hot sand and burnt straw. They'd dug it out on Monday and broken the loam off it and wire-brushed it, and now it sat mouth-up on the tuning lathe, ninety kilos of new bronze with the casting skin still on the outside, dull, and the inside bright where she'd skimmed it.

She tapped it at the lip with the rubber mallet and held her head next to it and listened, and then she struck the fork and held that next to it, and listened again. A bell rings five notes at once, the hum under everything and the strike note on top and three more between, and it's tuned when they stand where they should to each other. This one was meant to sit a clean fifth over the old low bell in the Rosário tower, so the two of them could talk.

"Hum's sharp," she said.

"Eight cents sharp," Davi said. He had the app open. "Take it from up the waist. High."

"Uai. Then do it." She turned the forró down and stood back from the lathe. "You can only take away," she said. "Where. That's the whole trade."

He knew where. He could have drawn it for her to the millimetre: up inside the waist, a long shallow cut, and the hum would come down and the strike note would barely move. He set the cutter. The lathe turned the bell slowly round on its table, and he brought the tool in, and the tool chattered, and his hand, trying to hold the chatter, went down instead of up. The cutter bit low, near the lip. A curl of bronze sprang off it, bright as a fingernail, and hit the floor.

He took the tool out. She struck the bell.

The strike note had fallen. Not far. Fifteen cents. Far enough that anyone who had stood under a tower for fifty years would hear it, and for good, because nobody puts metal back.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Hm."

"In São Paulo a machine would've cut it where I meant. My model on a cutter would've..."

"A machine bell's a machine bell." She bent and picked up the curl of bronze and put it in the scrap tin, where it rang. "They pay us for the hand. That's the licence." She nodded at the wall over the bench, at the framed certificate with the federal crest and SINO DE MÃO across the top and her name under it, Conceição Aparecida Ribeiro, founder. "The churches pay for my hand. When it's your name there, they'll pay for yours."

"We'll recast."

"Recast is three weeks, and the feast doesn't move. It goes up Thursday. It goes up flat." She ran her thumb round the lip where he'd cut. "The brotherhood paid in March. Their feast, their bell, and a bell on the feast beats a bell in November."

"Vô Zé'll hear it."

"Your grandfather's heard worse than that out of that tower." She set the mallet down on the bench and lined it up with the edge. "It's a good bell, uai. It's a bit low. So are lots of people."

He stood with his hands on the lathe table. The bronze under his palms was still faintly warm from the cut.

"Mãe. What should I do?"

She didn't answer at once. She wiped the cutter with a rag, and folded the rag, and put it on the shelf.

"When I was thirty," she said, "I bought an accordion off a man in Lavras, red one, mother-of-pearl buttons, and I sat on the back step with it every night for a year, after the pour, after the dishes, and my fingers wouldn't. They just wouldn't, not the left hand, the bass side. I thought they were bad fingers, that's all, I'd got bad fingers for the accordion and good ones for the cutter. Your grandfather had me on the cutter at nine. Nine to fourteen, forty-some bells, and I don't remember learning it. I remember knowing it." She looked at the bell. "I never knew it was a price."

He waited.

"Eight bells a year," she said, "if the churches keep ordering. You stay open a year, that's eight bells with a child's hands, and a child's hands learn a cut in a bell or two. You set on the twenty-eighth and your hands learn like mine learned the accordion, slow, and maybe in ten years you're where I am now, if you stay at it, if you don't go off to the city with that lot. I don't need the seat. Let them call me agarrada. A year."

"You'd do that." He looked at the bell, not at her. "Tonho's picanha goes grey. They'll say you won't let go."

"They'll say. And then they'll want a bell, and they'll come to me for it, because there's three of us left in Minas."

"And Christmas." He hadn't meant to say it.

"What about Christmas?"

"Nothing. The pack. Belo Horizonte, maybe. A few weeks."

She looked at him, and then at his hands on the lathe table.

"Uai," she said. "Then that's a bell you won't make."

The lathe had stopped. The bell sat on it, low.

He didn't say anything else. She turned the radio back up, and the forró came out of it as it always did, and she went to look at the coke.

## 27 September

Evening at the fountain and the pack had it all planned by the time the light went: the bus was booked, the aunt was confirmed, two of the labs had answered and one hadn't, and the one that hadn't would, obviously, and they'd take shifts, and somebody had found a list of every open lab in the city, sorted by how much they paid, and it was ceiling, the whole thing was ceiling. He was talking as fast as any of them and it was easy, easy as falling. Somebody asked about his feast and somebody else answered for him, he's staying open, obviously, Ribeiro's in, and he said obviously, and somebody did Rafaela, and he laughed.

He got home at eleven. The foundry light was on. She was at the board.

## 28 September

He came down at six. She had the loam on the trough and the radio going, and she didn't look round, and he didn't say anything, and she moved over and gave him the end of the arm.

It was a practice core, an old one, broken loam over brick, nothing to cast. He walked the board round. She walked beside him with her hand an inch off his on the arm, not touching it, and when the edge began to chatter she said, "Slow, slow, let the loam tell you, uai, it's not going anywhere," and he slowed, and the curl came off the profile long and even, and dropped on his boots. They did it until ten. She didn't talk much. She hummed the bass of the forró under her breath and he caught himself humming it too, which he hadn't known he knew.

At half past ten he was in the chair in the setting clinic, and Rafaela had a paper cup of water and a white packet with three tablets in a strip, and the cello was quiet.

He took the notebook out of his back pocket and opened it to the page.

High and low, turn and turn: the vigil. The doors stay open all night.

He looked at it for a while. It was in his own pencil, in his own hand, and he could remember sitting on the steps and how certain it had been, and it was certain still, it was a good note, there was nothing wrong with it. It was only that the feast doesn't move, and a bell goes up when it goes up, and you hang what you've got and you ring it, and the ones who pay are paying for the hand and not the drawing, and a hand learns what it's standing beside, and he'd always known that, he'd known it since he was small, it was just a thing you knew, like the loam.

He pressed the first tablet out of the strip. It was small and white and chalky on his tongue and tasted of nothing.

"Tomorrow, same time," Rafaela said. "Then the thirtieth. Then a week." She was looking at him. "Where are you going to cure?"

"At the board," he said.

She wrote something on the form and didn't say anything else, and he folded the notebook and put it in his pocket.

When he got back to the foundry she was at the moulding bench with her back to the gate. He stood beside her. She looked at the packet in his shirt pocket, where the foil of the strip showed through, and went on smoothing the loam with the flat of the trowel.

"I'm not taking the year," he said.

She smoothed the loam out to the edge, and lifted the trowel, and looked along the edge.

"Then you'll stand here all week," she said, "where I can see your hands," and moved over.

He took the second tablet at half past ten the next day and the third the day after, and went straight back to the board each time. On the evening of the thirtieth the pack came past the foundry gate on the way to the fountain and whistled for him, and somebody shouted that the bus had gone up eight reais and it was still ceiling. He lifted a hand with the loam on it. They waited a minute. Then they went off up the hill, all talking at once, and he went back to the board.

## 2 October

The pivot squeaked. He could hear the squeak now as two notes, not one, the iron on iron as the arm came round and then a smaller one when it settled, and he found he'd been listening to it for an hour.

There was loam under his nails, packed so deep it wouldn't come out with the brush, and loam drying grey on the backs of his hands where it cracked when he made a fist. He'd been at the practice core since six. His mother was across the yard at the moulding bench with the next order, a bell for a chapel up past Tiradentes, and every so often she looked over and said nothing, which meant it was going all right.

The board came round. The edge caught at the waist, and he felt it before it jumped, and eased, and it didn't jump.

Vô Zé was on the bench by the door with a coffee, in his feast shirt already though the feast was five days off, because he liked to break a shirt in. He'd been watching for a while.

"You talk like your mother," he said. "Last month you talked like that camp. You don't hear it. None of you ever hear it."

"Zero chance. Sound like me. Always have. Uai, I don't," Davi said, "I never did, and you know I don't, Vô, you've known me since I was small, I've always talked like this, the same as I always talked at that table with the beans."

Vô Zé drank his coffee.

"Dadá," he said, kindly, and went back to watching the board.

The arm came round. The loam came off the profile in a long curl and dropped on his boots, and he let it lie there, and walked the board round again, slow, the way you go when you're not going anywhere.

On the sixth Rafaela put him under the lamp in the clinic and looked at the scan for a long time, and asked him to read a sentence off a card, and listened to him read it, to the long vowels at the ends of the words, and her mouth went small, and she signed the form.

"Brabo," she said, and nothing else.

## 7 October

The Congado came up Rua Direita at ten with the drums going, the Moçambique in front in their white with the gungas on their ankles, all those tin rattles chattering at once in step, and behind them the crowned kings and queens of Congo under their parasols, the old queen in blue with her rosary wound round her wrist, and the whole Irmandade after, the Ribeiros in it, Ceiça in her white skirt dancing with the women, steady and not fast, the way she'd danced it every October since she could walk, and Davi beside her with the drum strap cutting into his shoulder, and when they got to the square in front of the Rosário church, Vô Zé was up in the tower already and he rang them in.

High first, three and three and three: a feast. Except that it came down off the new bell fifteen cents low, and the square heard it.

He saw them hear it. He saw the old women by the church steps lift their heads, and the man selling canjica stop with the ladle up, and a girl from the brotherhood turn to her mother, and he knew what they were hearing before anybody said it, because it was what he'd have heard himself, if he hadn't made it: a low stroke in the middle of a feast. Somebody in that house is going. Not gone. Going.

"Whose house?"

"The Ribeiros cast it."

"Ceiça, then. It'll be the seat. She's mourning the seat."

"Or Zé. He's seventy-eight."

"Zé's ringing it, uai."

"That's how they do it. They ring their own."

And it went off down the valley. The chapel at Pedra Branca had heard it, and its ringer was eighty if he was a day and had learned the code the same year Vô Zé had, and he did what you do, he passed it on, and he passed it on plain, with the low stroke where he'd heard it, on his own low bell, so there wasn't any mistaking it:

High three: a feast. One low inside it: someone in the house is going. High three.

Then São Sebastião across the river had it, and then the next chapel past that, fainter, and the next, until it was only a thing on the wind, someone in the house is going, carried tower to tower down the whole valley, about a bell that was only flat.

By noon three women had brought Ceiça a pot of canjica each and squeezed her hands, and she thanked every one of them and didn't explain, and put the pots on the step.

"Why don't you tell them?" he said.

"Somebody is going, uai." She lifted the lid off the nearest pot and smelled it. "Me, out of that chair. This one's got cinnamon in."

The pack came through the square in the afternoon in a knot, eating out of one bag, and called to him, and he went over. They were talking about the bus, he thought, or the aunt, or something that had happened since Tuesday; there was a word they kept saying that he had never heard. He followed one word in three. When they laughed he laughed late. One of them did Rafaela's brabo at him, and from outside it he could hear that it was a good imitation and not a kind one. Then they went on round the fountain, still talking, and he went back across the square to his mother.

The Entrega was after the Congado, on the plank stage in front of the church, with the câmara at a table with a green cloth over it and Rafaela at the end of the table with the forms. There were four heirs that year. Davi was the third. When his name was called he went up, and his mother came up the other steps with the framed licence under her arm and the brass vote token with the foundry's mark on it in her other hand, and gave him both, and said the junior's words straight off, plainly, as if she was ordering bread: "I hand you the trade. From today I'm your apprentice. Teach me."

He took them. The frame was heavier than it looked. The token was warm from her hand.

Across the square, in the door of his shop with his apron on, Seu Tonho watched, and held his phone, and didn't play anything.

## 8 October

At six the loam was on the trough and the radio was on, and Davi was at the board, and the next order was on the core, the chapel bell for up past Tiradentes, and his name was on the wall over the bench now, with hers moved down the frame to where it said apprentice in the new hand of the clerk.

She came across the yard with her coffee and stood beside him and watched him walk the arm round once, and twice, and on the third time round she put her cup on the edge of the pit and put her hand over his on the board, her loam-cracked knuckles on his, and slowed it, slower than he'd have thought it could go, until the curl came off the edge of the profile long and whole and dropped onto his boots.

Up the hill the Rosário started on the six o'clock, the new bell a shade low in it, and the others answered it down the valley, and he counted.
