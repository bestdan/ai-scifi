# Story bible: The Setting Day (working title)

## Premise

The treatment works by keeping the brain's critical periods open, so the
treated never settle into fixed adults. They learn anything in weeks, and
their tastes, accents and loyalties drift with whoever they stand near.
Adulthood has become a dose you choose, and it sets the brain like plaster.
Towns have built a rite around it: in the year a treated heir turns
sixteen, on the town's feast day, the untreated parent steps down, hands
over the household's council vote and the trade licence, and becomes the
child's junior. The seat goes only to an heir who has set. A boy is three
weeks from his rite in his mother's trade, bell founding, where he can
model every bell and his hands still can't make one. Only an open brain
trains the hands at a child's speed. If he sets on the day, he takes a seat
he can't fill, with hands that will learn at an adult's pace from then on.
If he delays, the town reads it as his mother refusing to step down. His
mother has been set all her life without choosing it, and when he asks her
which to do, her answer is that she never knew it was a price.

*(Stage 3 revisions: no friend subplot; the protagonist is a boy; the
narration drifts lightly; the mechanism is stated once.)*

## The novum

- **What it is:** a treatment given in the first week of life, a single
  course of injections, routine in Brazil's public health system since
  2033. Nearly every child born since then is treated; the few untreated
  children are from families who refused it, and they are a small, watched
  minority at school. The treatment does two things, and the counsellors
  are honest that they understand the first better than the second:
  1. **The nets never form.** In an ordinary brain a mesh of proteins (the
     counsellor calls it "the nets") grows around certain neurons in
     childhood and locks the wiring for hearing, accent, movement and the
     hands. The treated never grow it, so they keep a small child's speed
     at learning a language, an ear or a skill of the hands.
  2. **The pull stays on.** Every adolescent tunes hard to the people
     around them, then the tuning eases in the early twenties. In the
     treated it never eases. Tastes, loyalties and opinions keep taking the
     shape of whoever is nearest.

  Together, and with a brain that never stops building, the treated by
  school age think faster and further than any untreated adult they know.
- **The dose:** *firma*, three tablets over three days. It grows the nets
  all at once and ends the pull. The nets cure over about a week; whatever
  the brain is doing in that week is what it keeps. A heir counts as set,
  for the law, when the counsellor certifies the cure complete.
- **What it costs:**
  - **Open:** you learn everything, including whatever is nearest. The open
    drift, and **they don't feel themselves drift.** Each new slang, taste
    or conviction feels like their own, and has always been. Others see it;
    they don't.
  - **Set:** you keep what you have and what you are. From then on you
    learn new skills at an ordinary adult's pace: slower, not never. A set
    treated adult still reasons far faster than any untreated one.
- **Its rules and their edge cases:**
  - **No way back, yet.** There is no reopening dose. A lab in Campinas
    reopens the nets in rats with an enzyme, and the rats lose what the
    nets were holding. Rafaela says "none yet" and means it.
  - Setting erases nothing; it fixes. Accent, prosody, the hands' level
    and attachments stay as they stand in the curing week.
  - **Who you stand near in the curing week is who you sound like for
    life.** A set person's vowels and cadence date them, along with a word
    or two of their year.
  - Motor skill learned while open stays after setting, at the level
    reached. So the weeks before the dose are worth drilling.
  - The open can vote in elections, study and work, but by law they can't
    hold an office or licence with tenure: a council seat, a trade licence,
    a judgeship.
  - Untreated people set the ordinary way, in childhood, without knowing.
  - Nobody is forced to set. A few never do, and move from city to city;
    they are called *cata-ventos* (weathervanes).
- **Why the hands haven't learned yet:** practice is limited by pours, not
  by the brain. The Fundição Ribeiro casts about eight bells a year; each
  needs weeks of mould-work, and tuning is a few irreversible cuts per
  bell. Davi has spent his open years on school, theory and camps, and
  worked at the board on weekends and holidays. Ceiça learned between 9
  and 14 on about forty bells, when her father had more orders. One more
  open year buys Davi about eight bells at a child's speed. After setting,
  matching her is perhaps ten years of work, if he ever does.
- **Why not a machine:** machine-tuned bells exist and are cheaper, cast
  in São Paulo. Rosário's foundry holds a heritage licence (*sino de mão*,
  a hand-made bell) that exists only because the founder's own hand casts
  and tunes. Churches pay for the hand. The licence names the founder, and
  the founder must be someone with tenure, so someone set.

## The consequence ladder

1. **First order:** treated children outstrip their parents in every
   subject by twelve, and pick up anything (a language, an instrument, a
   trade's theory) in weeks.
2. **Second order:** the first treated cohort came of age around 2049 and
   took seats while still open. Councils of open members reversed
   themselves week to week, persuaded by whoever spoke last. They tried
   binding pledges: a pledge signed on Monday was renounced on Thursday,
   and the renunciation was renounced the next week. These were the *anos
   de cata-vento* (weathervane years, 2049–2052). Meanwhile untreated
   parents clung to posts their children had outgrown, and were bypassed.
   The 2052 Lei da Entrega (handover law) fixed both problems: a seat
   passes on a fixed day, with dignity, and only to someone who has set.
3. **Third order:** coming of age has become a choice of when to stop
   changing, and of whom to be standing beside when you do. A mind that
   drifts cannot be sure the choice is its own. Trades of the hands ask
   the heir to give up the one thing that would let him learn them
   quickly. The untreated look back at their own childhoods and see a door
   that closed while they weren't watching.

## The society

- **Setting:** Rosário das Pedras, an invented hill town of about 9,000 in
  the south of Minas Gerais, Brazil, in September and October 2057.
  Baroque churches, red dirt, a granite quarry, coffee on the slopes. Like
  São João del-Rei, the town has *sinos que falam*, bells that ring set
  patterns telling the town who has died, who is marrying, what feast it
  is. Rosário's own code is local and partly invented for the story. The
  patron feast is Nossa Senhora do Rosário, on 7 October, kept by the
  Irmandade do Rosário dos Pretos (the Black brotherhood of the Rosary)
  with Congado drums and dancing in the square, as it has been for two
  centuries. The Entrega is held on the feast day, after the Congado.
- **Institutions:**
  - The *câmara* (town council). Since the 2052 law, each licensed
    household business holds one vote in the town's trade assembly, and
    the vote passes at the Entrega with the licence.
  - The setting clinic, a room at the health post. A state counsellor must
    read every heir the official consent sheet (written in 2052, and it
    sounds like it) before *firma* is dispensed, and must certify the cure.
  - The Fundição Ribeiro, a family bell foundry since 1931 and one of three
    left in Minas, casting bells by the old loam method. The Ribeiros are a
    Black family of the Irmandade; Vô Zé rings the Rosário tower.
- **Economy:** what's scarce is set judgement and trained hands. Theory is
  cheap; any open fourteen-year-old can design a bell's profile in an
  afternoon. What's worthless is advice: the open generate it endlessly,
  and nobody trusts it.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:** untreated parents raise
  treated children who are smarter than them by ten. Since the Entrega,
  the stepped-down parent is formally the heir's junior (*aprendiz*) in the
  trade. In fact the parent usually keeps teaching. The household's
  senior is whoever stood up at the rite, not whoever is older.
  Grandparents who handed over before 2052 did it informally, with no
  rite, and some of them resent that they had no day.
- **Death:** unchanged. The bells toll it.
- **Taboos:** to imitate a set person's year to their face; to *pregar*
  someone in the weeks before their setting (the word means both to nail
  and to preach: to fix them in your shape while they cure); to be
  *agarrado* (a clinger), a parent still holding the licence after the
  heir's feast has passed.
- **An ordinary day:** the foundry opens at six. The open kids from the
  state school drift through the square at lunch in a pack, all speaking
  the same new slang that will be gone by Christmas. Set treated adults in
  their twenties run the clinic and the council; they are quick and dry,
  and their vowels date them. Untreated elders keep the shops and the
  bells.

## Minds

- **The open (Davi, the school pack):** see the structure of anything in
  minutes. When they are together, the pack arrives at decisions in a way
  nobody proposes and nobody can afterwards reconstruct, in a fast,
  overlapping talk adults can't follow. Davi takes part, and alone he
  can't say how it worked or translate it for his mother. **The story never
  explains it.** What the open can't do is hold still, or notice that they
  haven't.
- **The set treated (Rafaela):** quick and certain, slower to be moved,
  dry. They find the open exhausting and a little touching. What they
  can't easily do any more is learn a new hand or ear, and they don't like
  to say so.
- **The untreated (Ceiça, Vô Zé, Seu Tonho):** set in childhood without
  choosing it. Slower to reason than their children, and they know it.
  Their hands and ears hold what they learned young, which in Ceiça's case
  is the whole trade. What they can't think, until someone asks, is that
  their own fixedness was a price.

## Characters

- **Davi Ribeiro**, 16 (born 11 March 2041), treated, open.
  - **What he wants:** to take the seat on the day, so his mother steps
    down with honour, and to be able to make a bell with his own hands. And
    a decent lunch. He can't have both of the first two.
  - **What he's wrong about:** he thinks the choice is about hands and
    honour, and that he is the one making it. Each opinion he holds feels
    like his own; the reader can see where each came from.
  - **What the novum does to him in particular:** he can compute a bell's
    partials to the cent from a drawing. His hands tear the loam mould and
    chatter the tuning cutter. He just spent ten days at a robotics camp in
    Belo Horizonte and came home with the camp's slang, which he insists
    he's always used.
  - **The one thing that stays his:** the bell code. He has known it since
    he was four and decodes it in his sleep, and it is the one register in
    the narration that never changes colour with whoever he stands beside.
- **Conceição "Ceiça" Ribeiro**, 47 (born 2010), untreated, master founder,
  his mother.
  - **What she wants:** for the craft to live in her son's hands. She will
    take the town's contempt for a year if that is the cost.
  - **What she's wrong about:** that the craft is the thing she has to
    give. What Davi wants from her is what she is.
  - **What the novum does to her in particular:** she can't follow his
    models and doesn't pretend to. She learned the cutter from her father
    between nine and fourteen. At thirty she tried the accordion, was bad
    at it for a year, and gave up; she says her fingers wouldn't learn, and
    she has never connected that to the cutter.
  - **Something she loves outside the plot:** *forró* on the radio while
    she rams loam, and bronze when it is the right colour to pour, "cor de
    manga" (mango-coloured).
- **José "Vô Zé" Ribeiro**, 78, untreated, Ceiça's father, Davi's
  grandfather. He handed the foundry to Ceiça in 2038 with a handshake and
  no rite, and he rings the Rosário bells for the Irmandade. He thinks the
  Entrega is a fuss about something he did in a kitchen. He calls Davi by
  a name the boy's open friends have dropped.
- **Lia**, 9, Davi's cousin, treated and open, at the family table. She
  corrects Vô Zé's arithmetic and copies whoever spoke last.
- **Rafaela Brum**, 24 (born 2033), treated, set at 16 in 2049, the town's
  setting counsellor and a câmara member.
  - **What she wants:** to do her job honestly.
  - **What she's wrong about:** that her dryness hides what she lost.
  - **What the novum does to her in particular:** her vowels and cadence
    are stuck in 2049, with a word of the year ("brabo"), and the open
    kids mimic it behind her back. She set beside her first boyfriend and
    sounds like him; he left for Recife six years ago. She started the
    cello at 20, set, and is slowly getting less bad. You can hear her
    practising through the clinic wall.
- **Seu Tonho (Antônio Lage)**, 61, untreated, the butcher, the town's one
  *agarrado*. His daughter Kátia never set and drifts between cities. He
  still holds his licence, and fewer people buy his good cuts. He keeps a
  voice message from her he won't play. Sad and a little ridiculous.
- **The school pack:** a drifting crowd, not individuals. Seen once.

## Point of view and voice

- **Teller:** close third person on Davi.
- **Person and tense:** third, past.
- **Register:** plain, quick, precise about materials. **The narration
  drifts** with whoever is present: the camp's clipped city slang, Rafaela's
  2049 vowels and her "brabo", his mother's *mineiro* cadence ("uai",
  "trem", long sentences that end on the thing in her hands). Davi never
  notices the drift; the narration doesn't flag it, and the reader catches
  it. The drift is light and always tied to a person in the scene. The bell
  code never drifts. After the cure, the narration holds one register,
  hers. Portuguese words appear only where English has no equal, never
  glossed in apposition.
- **Why this teller:** a mind that drifts can only be shown from inside,
  and the fixing is something the reader hears happen, without being told
  whether it was chosen.
- **A sample paragraph in the voice:** "She had the loam on the board
  before he was down the stairs, and she didn't look round. He watched her
  hands go up the strickle and back, up and back, and he could have told
  her the curve was a hair fat at the shoulder, two millimetres, maybe
  less. He didn't. The radio was playing the same forró it always played
  and he caught himself humming the bass line, which he hadn't known he
  knew."

## Rules deliberately broken

- **"Exposition from a scientist who explains everything":** the consent
  sheet is read aloud once, because the law requires it. Rafaela reads it
  flat and abandons it halfway; Davi already knows it and is impatient.
  That is the scene the ledger names; nowhere else explains.
