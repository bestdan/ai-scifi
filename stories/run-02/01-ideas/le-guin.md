# Idea cards: le-guin

**Blind spots of this lens:** slow pacing; little appetite for mechanism or
hard science (the treatment itself is left as given); a moral seriousness that
can tip into instruction. All three cards lean on ritual and kinship rather
than on technology or plot velocity. Let Banks, Egan and Watts push back.

## Card 1: The Stepping-Down

- **Novum:** The handover has become a rite. In a treated child's sixteenth
  year, the parent formally steps down: gives up the household seat, the trade
  licence and the council vote to the child, and enters the junior grade as the
  child's apprentice-in-life.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kin terms invert at the rite; "elder" now means whoever
  stood up, not whoever is older.
- **How it works:** After a decade of untreated adults clinging to posts and
  being bypassed anyway, towns codified the transfer so it could be done with
  dignity on a set day. Stepped-down parents get a second adolescence: schooling,
  leisure, a new junior name.
- **First-order consequence:** Orderly succession; no one is pushed out.
- **Second-order consequence:** Trades that live in the hands, not the head,
  pass to children who know everything about them and can do almost none of it.
- **Third-order consequence:** A quiet shame runs both ways: "holdovers" who
  won't step down, and "hollow chairs" who sit seats they can't fill.
- **The story:** Ninth-day before his rite, a treated boy in a boatbuilding
  harbour town works beside his father, the best shipwright on the coast. The
  boy can model a hull in his head; his adze still tears the grain. The rite
  will make him the senior. He must choose: take the chair and the wood's
  judgment, or refuse it and make his father a holdover in everyone's eyes.
- **Why this lens:** How one becomes an adult, answered as a society-wide
  custom with a cost.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain* (the gifted supplant the
  ungifted); Clarke, *Childhood's End*. Neither ritualizes the reversal; the
  nearest match differs in kind.

## Card 2: The Keepers

- **Novum:** Treated children could finish schooling by nine. Law instead
  guarantees them the Long Play, slow, unhurried years to fourteen, and only
  untreated adults may run it, because only they can't be argued out of it.
- **Futurity:** F2, reaching F3: childhood itself becomes an institution the
  slower generation holds in trust.
- **How it works:** Early cohorts who were rushed burned out or went strange.
  The settlement: the untreated keep childhood. It is their last authority and
  their pension.
- **First-order consequence:** A generation of untreated adults finds its
  work: keeping, not teaching.
- **Second-order consequence:** The keepers are dying out. Treated adults
  will soon run the Long Play, and they will optimize it.
- **Third-order consequence:** Children grow up loved by people who can't keep
  up with them, and know it.
- **The story:** A treated girl of twelve, ready and bored, wants to petition
  out early. Her keeper is the last untreated woman in the valley, who knows
  she'll lose any argument. The girl can win. Winning ends the keeping for
  every child after her.
- **Why this lens:** Who raises the children, and who pays for the good
  society.
- **Suspected prior art:** Card's *Ender's Game* (exploited gifted children);
  Walter Tevis's *Mockingbird* slightly. No close match for slowness as an
  institution.

## Card 3: The Owing Year

- **Novum:** A treated child's final school year is spent teaching one
  untreated adult, usually a parent, something real. The child graduates only
  when the adult passes. Standing in the new society is counted in what you
  have carried to slower minds.
- **Futurity:** F2. Schooling, status and debt reorganize around the
  generation gap.
- **How it works:** The treated learned everything faster than anyone could
  test. What they couldn't learn was patience; the Owing Year is the test of
  that.
- **First-order consequence:** Untreated adults are relearning trades, reading
  and law at fifty.
- **Second-order consequence:** A parent can hold a child's adulthood hostage,
  by accident or not.
- **Third-order consequence:** Families bargain, cheat, and grow close in ways
  the planners never charted.
- **The story:** A girl must teach her mother, a ferry pilot who never learned
  to read well, enough written navigation law to pass. Halfway through she
  suspects her mother is failing on purpose, to keep her home one more year.
  She can expose it, or let the year run.
- **Why this lens:** The carrier bag: a story about holding and passing
  something on, not a fight.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress again; Keyes, *Flowers for Algernon* in
  reverse. Nothing with this institution that I know of.
