# Idea cards: rajaniemi

**Blind spots, stated up front:** this lens mistakes density for depth. These
cards each carry three or four coined terms, and a drafter at 4,000 to 7,000
words should let only one or two of them into the first scene. The lens also
overloads plots with reversals, and it underweights the plain emotional line
a coming-of-age story needs. Let the First Reader and Asimov push back on
opacity.

## Card 1: Setting

- **Novum:** The treatment works by keeping the brain's critical periods open: a child learns a fourth language as easily as a first. It never shuts them, so adulthood becomes a dose you choose, *kovo*, which closes them.
- **Futurity:** F3. Being a grown-up stops being an age and becomes a chemical decision.
- **How it works:** The treatment stops perineuronal nets from forming. *Kovo* is a nets-precursor that sets them, like plaster. After that the mind stays sharp but slow to change.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated teens, *notkeat* ("supple ones"), learn anything in weeks, but their accents, loyalties and tastes drift with whoever they stand near.
- **Second-order consequence:** Institutions won't give long tenure to the unset. A judge has to have set.
- **Third-order consequence:** Love becomes a question of timing. Whoever sets first is fixed on a partner who can still drift away.
- **The story:** Iiris, 17, in Turku, is apprenticed to her untreated grandfather's ferry trade, which she learned in a month. She loves a boy who's staying open. Setting now keeps her grandfather's dialect and her love. Staying open keeps her fast for the world that's coming.
- **Why this lens:** A rite built outward from real neurobiology, and an infrastructure word that implies a whole life-course.
- **Suspected prior art:** Lowry's *The Giver* (puberty pills); Westerfeld's *Uglies* (operation at sixteen). Both impose the change and both are dystopias. Here it's chosen and the cost runs both ways. Nearest match isn't close.

## Card 2: The Minority of the Dike-Reeve

- **Novum:** Law can't let twelve-year-olds hold office, so the treated heirs to posts get *regents*. Untreated adults hold the seal until the child comes of age, the way a king's minority worked.
- **Futurity:** F2. The handover is codified: a whole tier of government is caretaker government.
- **How it works:** The Dutch waterschap (water board) revives the old *voogdij* (guardianship) law. The regent's only power is to delay a signature. They're known by the slang *stoelwarmer* (seat-warmer).
- **First-order consequence:** Fifteen-year-olds draft the flood models, and fifty-year-olds countersign them.
- **Second-order consequence:** A regency is court politics. Regents can stall, bargain and cultivate their heirs.
- **Third-order consequence:** A regent's legacy is the heir they shaped. *Regentenstukken* (group portraits of regents) come back, showing the regent and the child together.
- **The story:** Fenna, 16, is heir to the dike-reeve's seat of a Zeeland board, and her regent is her old maths teacher. She can prove the storm-surge plan he's stalling is right. Then she finds out why he's stalling: he's buying time for a village her model calls acceptable loss. To overrule him she would have to rule early.
- **Why this lens:** A royal-minority intrigue played straight in a municipal water board, and Dutch words that already carry four centuries of *regent* history.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress's *Beggars in Spain* (law against the enhanced); Card's *Ender's Game* (children in command). Neither has a regency structure.

## Card 3: The Lagman

- **Novum:** Seats, and some kinds of trust, are reserved for the untreated. Some treated adults "wear lead": they fake being slow. A *lagman* is someone who reads the lag and catches them.
- **Futurity:** F2. The handover has made being untreated a protected status worth forging.
- **How it works:** Faking slowness leaks. A *lagman* watches for boredom tells, for answers that arrive too evenly, and for the absence of honest mistakes. The word comes from the Shetland and Norse *lawman*, the one who held the law.
- **First-order consequence:** The *auld-sort* (untreated) hold protected seats on councils and as jurors.
- **Second-order consequence:** Treated people pass as *auld-sort* to get those seats, or to be loved as equals.
- **Third-order consequence:** Being slow becomes something you can perform, and nobody is sure which of their elders are real.
- **The story:** Ronja, 14 and treated, apprentices to Lerwick's aging *lagman* as a fast pair of eyes. A case leads her to her own mother, who was in the early trial and has worn lead for fifteen years. She did it so that her daughter would have a mother instead of a peer. Ronja has to decide whether to report her.
- **Why this lens:** A detective story set inside a posthuman social category, with a coined term that carries a thousand years of law.
- **Suspected prior art:** Shiras's *Children of the Atom* (gifted children hiding). Here a parent hides from her child, which inverts it. Also passing narratives in general.
