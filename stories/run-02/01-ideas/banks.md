# Idea cards: banks

**Blind spots, stated up front.** This lens sprawls. It reaches for institutions, treaties and set pieces, and it can bury a short story under worldbuilding. Short stories aren't its natural form. Each card below is cut to one household and one institution, but stage 3 should check that the institution stays offstage and that Chiang or Asimov trims anything that reads like a briefing.

## Card 1: Minding

- **Novum:** Treated teenagers run an informal, near-universal practice of "minding": they quietly fix their untreated parents' work at night so the parents keep feeling competent. The parents know, and they run a counter-practice of their own.
- **Futurity:** F2. One generation out, with family authority reorganised around a gap both sides pretend isn't there.
- **How it works:** The kids share notes, rigged problems and patched drafts over a peer network. The parents' version is older and slower: they compare notes at the school gate.
- **First-order consequence:** Untreated adults stay in their jobs, and their work gets better.
- **Second-order consequence:** Every household runs two benevolent deceptions, each one protecting the other.
- **Third-order consequence:** A generation learns that being known for a long time beats being clever, and it's the one thing their parents still have over them.
- **The story:** Ines, 15, minds her father, who runs the county's flood barriers. One night she finds his corrections to *her* corrections, made in a hand she can read. She has been managed too. She has to decide whether to tell him she knows, which would end both games.
- **Why this lens:** Power that means well, and dirty work done out of love on both sides.
- **Suspected prior art:** Asimov, "The Evitable Conflict" (covert benevolent management). Kress, *Beggars in Spain*. Neither has the reciprocal version.

## Card 2: Reasons Given

- **Novum:** The "Slow Clause" requires every public decision made by a treated council to come with reasons an untreated citizen can follow. The real reasons are too long and too fast, so a profession of treated "givers" writes true-enough simplifications.
- **Futurity:** F2. The institutions have been handed over, and the law exists to keep the handover legitimate.
- **How it works:** A decision passes only once a panel of untreated readers, drawn by lot, signs that they understood it. Givers are judged on how often the readers sign.
- **First-order consequence:** The untreated keep a veto in practice, and keep their consent.
- **Second-order consequence:** Givers get good at writing reasons that pass, which isn't the same as writing the true ones.
- **Third-order consequence:** Untreated citizens trust government more than they have in a century, on the strength of a well-made story.
- **The story:** Tomas, 19, is a junior giver writing up a coastal-retreat order that relocates his own untreated mother's street. The true reason would frighten her. The passable one leaves out the part she would object to. Her name comes up on the reading panel.
- **Why this lens:** The benevolent hegemon's dirty work, and the view from below.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang, "Catching Crumbs from the Table" (humans interpreting metahuman science). That story is about knowledge. This one is about consent, and it has a liar in the middle of it.

## Card 3: The Hinge Generation

- **Novum:** The treated generation, now adults with the votes, bans the treatment for their own children. Their kids are born ordinary, and they're raised by geniuses and grandparents who are not.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kin is rearranged: the gap now runs downward, and grandparents and grandchildren become peers.
- **How it works:** The ban passes narrowly in the first election in which the treated are a majority. Their stated reason is that children deserve a childhood their parents can't see straight through.
- **First-order consequence:** A one-generation bulge of minds, with a normal generation on either side of it.
- **Second-order consequence:** Untreated grandparents become their grandchildren's natural allies and interpreters.
- **Third-order consequence:** Growing up means forgiving a parent for a choice that was both kind and self-serving. The treated never wanted to be outgrown the way they outgrew their own parents.
- **The story:** Maren, 16 and ordinary, wants to know why her brilliant mother voted for the ban. Her grandmother knows both reasons. Maren sits on a youth panel advising on whether to reopen the treatment for the next generation, so she is choosing for children who aren't born yet. That's the choice her mother made, and the one her grandmother made before that.
- **Why this lens:** The civilization solved intelligence, and solving it created the problem. The most powerful people in the story need a forgiveness they can't take.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars and Choosers* (enhanced parents engineer children beyond themselves), which runs in the opposite direction. *Childhood's End* is not close.
