# Idea cards: watts

**Blind spots, stated up front.** This lens runs bleak, and it will try to
make every cost the point. It turns people into case studies, packs ideas too
densely for a short story, and undervalues ritual, joy and meaning as real
forces. Each card below has a hopeful line built into it. Le Guin and Banks
should test whether that hope is earned or just bolted on.

## Card 1: The Open Window

- **Novum:** The treatment raises IQ by keeping childhood critical periods open, so the treated never "set." Becoming an adult, with fixed tastes, loyalties and an accent, is now something you have to choose. A second drug does it.
- **Futurity:** F3. Adulthood stops being something that happens to you.
- **How it works:** It knocks down Lynx1 and blocks the build-up of perineuronal nets, so the brain keeps juvenile plasticity (Morishita et al. 2010; Pizzorusso et al. 2002). Valproate briefly reopens a critical period in adults (Gervain et al. 2013).
- **First-order consequence:** Treated kids learn anything quickly, including the preferences of whoever they happen to be around.
- **Second-order consequence:** Institutions want stubborn people at the top, and only the untreated are stubborn.
- **Third-order consequence:** "Closing" becomes a coming-of-age rite. Families argue over when to close, the way they used to argue about marriage.
- **The story:** Ilse, 19, is taking over her father's cider orchard. She loves the orchard. Then she spends a month in the city and loves the city just as much. She has to decide whether to close now, and become someone in particular, or stay open and stay brilliant. Her father, who never had the choice, can't tell her which.
- **Why this lens:** It asks what a trait is for. Fixed identity turns out to be the price of a brain that has stopped learning.
- **Suspected prior art:** Egan's "Reasons to Be Cheerful" (a man chooses his own preferences after brain damage). That story has one man and no rite. This card has a generation and a rite.

## Card 2: Showing Hands

- **Novum:** The treated can out-model untreated adults without effort, so they grow up with a code of their own: when you persuade an untreated person, you name your move out loud while you make it.
- **Futurity:** F2. Politics and families reorganize around a handicap the stronger side chose for itself.
- **How it works:** The social brain likely evolved for manipulation (Byrne & Whiten 1988; Dunbar 1998). Children manipulate their parents for more investment, and parents evolved to discount it (Trivers 1974). Now the discounting fails. The code works as a Zahavian honest signal (Zahavi 1975).
- **First-order consequence:** The treated narrate their own persuasion, and it is still persuasive.
- **Second-order consequence:** Untreated elders start to suspect that the disclosure is itself a move one level up.
- **Third-order consequence:** Trust stops being a feeling. It becomes a procedure.
- **The story:** Marit was born eleven months before the rollout. She is the last untreated member of her town's council, and her treated brother is running against her. He shows his hands flawlessly and wins fairly. Her choice is whether to accuse him of the meta-move she can't prove, or concede and find out what she is to him now.
- **Why this lens:** Machiavellian intelligence, and a viewpoint character who is the slower mind.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain* (resentment between enhanced and unenhanced). Kress has no disclosure code and no sibling seat.

## Card 3: The Keeper

- **Novum:** Faster cortical learning brings faster interference. The treated master skills but lose their own past within a few years unless they rehearse it. Untreated people hold the family's memory, so some treated parents now leave one child untreated on purpose.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kinship gets reorganized around who remembers.
- **How it works:** The brain has two learning systems, a fast hippocampus and a slow cortex. The cortex is slow so that new learning doesn't overwrite old (McClelland, McNaughton & O'Reilly 1995; McCloskey & Cohen 1989). Forgetting is adaptive (Richards & Frankland 2017).
- **First-order consequence:** The treated don't remember being children.
- **Second-order consequence:** Grandparents become the archive of the treated generation's childhood.
- **Third-order consequence:** As those grandparents die, the treated choose a "keeper" among their own children, a slow child raised to remember for them.
- **The story:** Hanna, 14, is untreated by her parents' choice, and she resents it. Her grandmother, who holds her mother's childhood, is dying. Hanna can take those memories on by listening to her grandmother, which makes her the keeper her parents planned. Or she can refuse, and let her mother lose her childhood for good.
- **Why this lens:** The self is a story the brain tells itself, and here someone else has to tell it.
- **Suspected prior art:** Lowry, *The Giver* (a memory-keeper role). Lowry's keeper is assigned by society through mystical transfer. Hanna is a keeper by kinship and biology, and she can refuse.
