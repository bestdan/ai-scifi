# Idea cards: lem

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front:** it prefers essay to scene and
documents to people, so these cards lean on document forms that may starve
the characters. It distrusts earnestness, which pulls against a
coming-of-age story's warmth; Banks and Le Guin should push on that. Its
characters can be ciphers. And its instinct to leave things unexplained has
to be squared with the style guide's payoff ledger. Every card below names
what stays unexplained, and also what must be made explicit.

## Card 1: Minutes of the Saddle Creek Ditch Board

- **Novum:** The treated cohort takes over a sixty-year-old irrigation board
  and finds the one thing their intelligence can't take apart: an informal
  water-sharing custom that works, that no living member can explain, and that
  acts like a mind with nobody behind it.
- **Futurity:** F2. Village institutions are handed to the cohort at nineteen.
- **How it works:** The custom is a rota of who opens their headgate when,
  amended by handshake over decades. The young board replaces it with an
  optimized schedule. That schedule is better on every variable they model, and
  it fails in the first dry August.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated nineteen-year-olds run the boards their
  grandparents ran.
- **Second-order consequence:** Inherited arrangements get "fixed" faster than
  anyone learns what they were doing.
- **Third-order consequence:** The old become keepers of rules they can't
  justify, and the young have to learn to obey a black box.
- **The story:** Told through board minutes, with the secretary's marginal
  notes. She is seventeen and treated, and her grandmother is the outgoing
  chair. One farm loses its orchard. The secretary has to choose whether to
  move to bring back the rota with no rationale on record: "reason unknown;
  retain." Explicit on the page: what failed, and who paid. Never explained:
  why the rota worked.
- **Why this lens:** *The Invincible*'s mindless evolved order, and science as
  projection.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chesterton's fence, which is an essay rather than a
  story. No close SF match that I know of.

## Card 2: Corrections to "Songs of the Upper Larrow"

- **Novum:** A treated fifteen-year-old is asked to edit her untreated
  father's life work, a field guide to local birdsong dialects recorded over
  thirty years. She can prove his taxonomy wrong in an afternoon, but she
  can't replace his data, and her own taxonomy turns out to be a projection
  too.
- **Futurity:** F2. Within a single family, the child becomes the authority
  in the parent's field.
- **How it works:** His categories follow what his ear could tell apart. Her
  reanalysis of his recordings finds structure his ear missed, and then finds
  that her structure tracks her own methods rather than the birds.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated children outclass their parents in the
  parents' own hobbies and careers.
- **Second-order consequence:** Amateur scholarship gets "corrected" by the
  scholars' own children.
- **Third-order consequence:** A parent's pride in their child and their
  grief for their own work arrive in the same week.
- **The story:** Told as her editorial report, with excerpts from the guide.
  She has to choose whether to send the report, which is honest and
  devastating and would make her name, or to publish his guide as he wrote
  it, with her doubt in a footnote. Never understood: what the birds are
  actually saying.
- **Why this lens:** *A Perfect Vacuum*'s form, and *Solaris*'s taxonomy that
  learns nothing.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Flowers for Algernon* uses document form for
  intelligence, but here the intelligence belongs to the writer, not the
  subject. Not close.

## Card 3: The Norming

- **Novum:** The old tests can't measure the treated: they score at the
  ceiling. The only people able to write a valid test for them are treated
  themselves, so a twelve-year-old is commissioned to write the intelligence
  test her whole cohort will be measured by.
- **Futurity:** F2. Children build the instrument that measures children.
- **How it works:** Every test she drafts ends up measuring how much the
  test-taker resembles her. No neutral scale exists for a population nobody
  older can rank.
- **First-order consequence:** Institutions certify the cohort with
  instruments the cohort writes.
- **Second-order consequence:** Adult committees solemnly approve exams that
  none of them can take, which is comic.
- **Third-order consequence:** Nobody can say what the "60 points" are points
  of. The treatment works, and nobody knows at what.
- **The story:** She is twelve, and her infant brother's cohort is waiting on
  the renewal vote. She can tune the test so that her cohort reads as uniform
  and safe, which saves the rollout, or report honestly that they diverge
  along axes no single number holds, which may stall it. Made explicit: the
  vote and her choice. Never explained: what the treatment raised.
- **Why this lens:** Human vanity punctured: the measurer turns out to be the
  measure. And *His Master's Voice*: the instrument decodes only its maker.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang's "The Evolution of Human Science" (adults
  interpreting metahuman work) is near in theme but differs in kind. Kress's
  *Beggars in Spain* is not close.
