# Idea cards: asimov

**Blind spots of this lens, stated up front:** these cards are built as rule
systems with a fair-play puzzle at the edge. They are thin on interiority and
the body, and they lean toward problems that turn out to be solvable. Each
one risks turning its characters into carriers for the idea. Le Guin, Lem and
Rajaniemi should push back on all three.

## Card 1: The Assessor Rule

- **Novum:** A statute passed to protect treated children: nobody may grade, license or examine anyone who outscores them. Untreated teachers and examiners are being dismissed cohort by cohort, and the first treated cohort can't be licensed by anyone.
- **Futurity:** F2. A society in its first generation of reorganizing who may judge whom.
- **How it works:** "Outscores" means the national capacity instrument. Examiners must sit at or above the candidate's band, so the eldest treated have no eligible examiner.
- **First-order consequence:** Untreated teachers lose the right to mark work. Children examine children.
- **Second-order consequence:** A 24-year-old surgeon of the first cohort practises unlicensed, waiting for her juniors to come of age so they can examine her.
- **Third-order consequence:** The untreated stop being trusted as judges of anything, and stop trusting themselves.
- **The story:** Nia, 16, treated, a valleys town. Her untreated maths teacher, Mr. Pryce, is to be struck off under the Rule. At the hearing she has to argue a premise the drafters missed: *checking* an answer is far easier than *finding* it, so the old don't need to be cleverer to judge. Planted earlier: her grandmother verifying a crossword she couldn't solve. Winning means admitting in public that her own work is checkable by people she outscores.
- **Why this lens:** a rule whose hidden assumption breaks at a case its makers didn't consider, and a solution a reader can reach first.
- **Suspected prior art:** Chiang, "Catching Crumbs from the Table" (humans as interpreters of superhuman science). Different in kind: here the theme is verification as a legal role. Asimov, "The Feeling of Power" is a distant cousin.

## Card 2: Explain It Back

- **Novum:** Consent law already holds that a guardian may consent only to what they understand. When the children are sixty points brighter than their parents, that doctrine quietly caps every child's horizon at what their parents can grasp.
- **Futurity:** F2. The parent-child relation is renegotiated through law and teaching.
- **How it works:** A magistrate tests comprehension by having the guardian explain the decision back, unprompted. If the child coaches the answers, the consent is void.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated children spend years teaching their parents, honestly, because that is the only way forward.
- **Second-order consequence:** The untreated parents become the best-educated untreated generation in history, taught by their own children.
- **Third-order consequence:** Teaching becomes the defining skill of the treated cohort. Their whole idea of intelligence bends toward making things clear.
- **The story:** Ines, 14, wants to join the regional water authority's residential intake, where teenagers are taking over from retiring engineers. Her father Aurelio is a ferry mechanic, and he has three weeks to understand estuary hydrology well enough to tell a magistrate about it. She teaches him truthfully. He learns it, and in learning it he sees a risk she didn't: the job, not the science. Then he refuses, and his refusal is a valid one.
- **Why this lens:** an existing rule meets a case it never imagined. A clever parent does a clever thing.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain* (legal status of the enhanced). Its angle is economic and adversarial. Here it is consent and teaching.

## Card 3: Ties Go to the Younger

- **Novum:** The dose improved every year from 2034 to 2041, so authority went to the highest-dose cohort, under an Act whose boilerplate tiebreak gives precedence to the younger cohort. Then the dose plateaued. Every cohort since ties, so the Act hands every office to the newest adults, the 18-year-olds, year after year.
- **Futurity:** F2 edging F3. Seniority itself inverts and then rotates annually.
- **How it works:** The 2042 increment showed no measurable gain. The regulator froze the dose, and nobody re-read clause 9.
- **First-order consequence:** Each year a fresh class of 18-year-olds takes the harbour boards, the courts and the councils.
- **Second-order consequence:** Institutions run in one-year terms and have no memory. Last year's office-holders become the advisers.
- **Third-order consequence:** Advisers turn into a respected class: a seniority of the overtaken.
- **The story:** Djamila, 17, of a later cohort in Mindelo, will inherit the harbour authority on her birthday. She is the one who notices clause 9, and she can close it before she turns 18 or take her year. The reader can spot the tiebreak from its first, throwaway mention.
- **Why this lens:** psychohistory in miniature, a society-level edge case, and a fair-play clue.
- **Suspected prior art:** Kress, *Beggars in Spain* (Super-Sleepless overtaking the Sleepless). Anderson, *Brain Wave*. Neither has the plateau or the procedural inversion.
