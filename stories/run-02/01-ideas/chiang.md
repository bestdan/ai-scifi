# Idea cards: chiang

**Blind spots of this lens.** I run low on plot momentum, action and
antagonists. All three cards put quiet domestic stakes at their centre and
have no villain. They can come out too measured and too polished. Banks or
Watts should push on whether each one has enough engine for 4,000 words or
more.

## Card 1: Fourth Draft

- **Novum:** One generation out, treated adolescents legally have to
  certify that an untreated person has consented to any decision that
  person can't follow. To do that they write "plain accounts", explanations
  aimed at the reader's level. A teenager's coming-of-age exam is to write
  one for her own mother.
- **Futurity:** F2. Law and family are reorganising around a gap in
  comprehension that runs inside each household.
- **How it works:** A plain account passes if the untreated reader can
  restate it and an independent treated assessor finds nothing material
  left out. Those two tests pull against each other.
- **First-order consequence:** Treated teenagers become their families'
  explainers.
- **Second-order consequence:** Accounts get compressed until each
  simplification is a small, defensible lie.
- **Third-order consequence:** Untreated adults learn that what they are
  trusting is the explainer, not the explanation.
- **The story:** Yusra, 15, has to explain an experimental heart procedure
  to her mother, a bus mechanic. The story is told as four drafts of the
  account, with the life between them showing in the margins. Each draft is
  more honest and harder to follow. In the end she sees that her mother's
  trust is reasoned rather than ignorant. Yusra has to decide whether to
  hand over the true draft, which her mother can't use, or the usable one,
  which leaves something out.
- **Why this lens:** It asks whether you can rationally trust reasoning you
  can't follow. The form, successive drafts, acts the question out.
- **Suspected prior art:** *Flowers for Algernon*'s progress reports (the
  form is close, the direction reversed) and Chiang's "Understand". Neither
  is close in premise.

## Card 2: Countersign

- **Novum:** Once the first treated cohort reaches majority, untreated
  adults need a treated "steward" to co-sign decisions above a statutory
  complexity line: mortgages, surgery, wills. Stewards are usually the
  untreated adult's own children.
- **Futurity:** F3. Guardianship flips, so parenthood and legal adulthood
  stop lining up.
- **How it works:** A published complexity index scores every contract
  type. A steward may refuse to countersign only if they can show the
  decision is "materially uninformed", not merely unwise.
- **First-order consequence:** Children hold a veto over their parents.
- **Second-order consequence:** Unwise but informed choices become a legal
  category of their own, and a contested one.
- **Third-order consequence:** Families argue over whether a choice is
  wrong or just misunderstood, and the child is the one who has to know
  which.
- **The story:** Tomasz is 19, newly certified, and his father's steward.
  His father wants to sink his savings into keeping a failing cider orchard
  alive. Tomasz can see exactly why it will fail. His father understands
  that well enough and wants to do it anyway. Countersigning means letting
  him lose. The form is the docket: each section is a decision filed that
  year, with Tomasz's annotations.
- **Why this lens:** It is a free-will question. Is a choice yours if
  someone who understands it better has to sign it?
- **Suspected prior art:** Nancy Kress's *Beggars in Spain* (the engineered
  elite against the rest, told in economic terms) and Vinge's "Fast Times
  at Fairmont High". The flipped guardianship as a family's private legal
  bond doesn't appear in either.

## Card 3: The Unread Room

- **Novum:** In the second treated generation, treated parents understand
  their treated children almost completely. The children start seeking out
  their untreated grandparents for the one thing the grandparents can
  offer, which is being only partly understood.
- **Futurity:** F3. Kin roles reorganise around opacity: elders become
  custodians of not-knowing.
- **How it works:** No new technology is involved. Treated parents read
  tells, motives and lies almost at once. The gap between untreated
  grandparent and treated child is the only private space a child has left.
- **First-order consequence:** Grandparents' houses become places of
  refuge.
- **Second-order consequence:** Treated parents, who were themselves raised
  by people they outstripped, envy a childhood they now can't give.
- **Third-order consequence:** A self forms in what others can't see. The
  culture has to decide whether it values that.
- **The story:** Told by Agnieszka, an untreated grandmother, who is aware
  that she misreads her 13-year-old granddaughter and records her guesses
  anyway. The girl is working on something she won't show her parents. The
  grandmother has to decide whether to tell her daughter, the girl's
  mother, or keep a secret she doesn't understand.
- **Why this lens:** It asks whether selfhood needs to be partly unseen.
  The narration, unreliable on purpose, puts the reader where the
  grandmother is.
- **Suspected prior art:** Clarke's *Childhood's End* and Sturgeon's *More
  Than Human* are distant. I know of no close match for grandparents as
  sanctuaries of opacity.
