# First Reader report: draft 3 (Strike Note)

## Where I got lost

- **The opening paragraphs (16 Sep, board and core).** "The board", "the core", "the false bell", "shod with steel", "the shoulder", "the waist" all arrive in a rush. I could picture a woman scraping clay round a shape, but I couldn't tell what "fat at the shoulder" meant until she stopped. I got the gist and moved on.
- **"Bricked", "zero chance", "advice is one real", "ceiling", "agarrada", "cata-vento", "pregar", "brabo".** Mostly I worked these out from context. "Bricked" was clear after the tear. "Pregar" I only half understood on the first pass. I took it to mean standing next to someone on purpose so they sound like you, and I was never sure whether it was an insult or a crime.
- **The Rafaela reading (19 Sep, Annex Three).** The legal text is long. I understood the point: a treated person's mind stays open to whoever they're near, and the tablets close it. I was slightly lost on "the pull" versus "the nets" until she explained the curing week out loud afterwards.
- **The death-bell and feast code at the Sunday table.** I followed that low means death and high means feast. I lost track of which bell was which once the Virgin, the small one and the low one were all in play. The "pitch before number" idea I liked, but the numbers (400, 390, 412) went past me.
- **The 7 Oct misreading.** I got there, but I had to work for it. The Virgin sounds low, so the town hears a death-sign in the middle of a feast. Once I had that, it landed.
- **The vigil notation.** The pencil mark `high • · low • ...` with "the vigil. The doors stay open all night." I didn't know what it was for, and I didn't know why he went back to it at the clinic. I read it as something he wrote to mean "wait a year".

## Where I got bored

- **The Annex reading.** Long, and I skimmed the middle of the first clause. The scene picks up as soon as she starts talking in her own voice.
- **The 22 Sep and 27 Sep fountain scenes.** The first one has a good moment. The second I mostly knew already: he's being swept along, everyone says "obviously", he says it back. I was waiting for him to go home to the board.
- **The Seu Tonho scene.** I was waiting for it to connect. It did, at the end with the unplayed voice message, which stayed with me. But the stretch about the picanha and the supermarket felt like explanation.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **"Loam you can put back. Nobody puts metal back."** I liked it straight away and liked it more when it came back.
- **Lia sitting there with all the codes, correcting her grandfather's death count to four hundred and twelve.** I laughed. "Vô Zé looked at the ceiling as if the number were written there in a hand he didn't trust."
- **"Ceiça ate round the grit."** Small, and I believed these people entirely.
- **Tonho and the voice message he hasn't played: "Then I'd know what she sounds like now."** This was the best line in the story for me. It made the whole premise personal in one sentence.
- **The cello through the wall.** Missing the same note, stopping, going back. I kept hearing it and it did the work of the whole explanation about how adult learning goes.
- **"It's a good bell, uai. It's a bit low. So are lots of people."** Funny, and warm, and a little sad.
- **The accordion speech.** "I never knew it was a price." I was moved here. It was the first time I understood what she was offering him and what it would cost her.
- **"Then you'll stand here all week where I can see your hands," and moved over.** Lovely. No speech, just her making room.
- **The town hearing the flat bell and carrying it down the valley as a death-sign, with three women bringing canjica.** Surprising and funny. "Me, out of that chair. This one's got cinnamon in." I loved it.
- **Vô Zé calling him "Dadá, kindly" after he'd said he didn't sound like his mother.** I understood what had happened to him from that alone, and it was more convincing than the clinic scene.
- **The last paragraph, her hand over his, and him hearing the bell before he counts.** It got me. I didn't need to be told whether he'd chosen well.

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd keep reading past page 2

I cared most about Ceiça: her silence, her hands, and the accordion story. I cared next about Davi, because he fumbles in front of her and then slides along with his friends without noticing. I also cared a good deal about Seu Tonho for the few lines he had, and about Rafaela with her cello.

Yes, I'd have kept reading past page 2. The opening is dense, but by the time the tear opens in the loam and she packs it with two fingers, I was in. The family at the table pulled me the rest of the way. If I'd had to judge from the first page alone, it would have been closer.

## The question I'd still be thinking about the next day

Whether Davi really chose to stay, or whether the board and his mother's hand just happened to be what he was standing next to. He can't find the moment it was decided, at the fountain or at the board, and neither can I. If he had been standing somewhere else that week, would he have come out as someone else? And if so, was it ever his choice?
