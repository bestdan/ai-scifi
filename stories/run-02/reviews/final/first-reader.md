# First Reader report: "Strike Note"

## Where I got lost

- The opening, paragraphs 3 to 4 ("Two millimetres fat... Max. He'd run her drawing at camp..."). I didn't know who "Max" was. I took it for a person, then a setting on a machine. I also couldn't tell whose thoughts I was in when the "Clean profile" line turned into the model talk.
- The bell-code strokes (the dots under "low ••• · high •••• · low •"). The explanation after them ("Low first: a death. Three: a man...") was clear enough once I read it. But I couldn't have decoded any of them myself, and the grandfather's "pitch before number" rule arrived after I'd already tried and failed.
- The legal reading in the clinic scene (the long "Lei da Entrega" passage). I understood the gist: a treatment keeps children "open" and impressionable, and a tablet "sets" them. I got through the clauses slowly. The speaker mostly telling me things I was meant to already know made it feel like homework.
- "Pregar", "agarrada", "cata-vento", "brabo" and "bricked". I mostly got them from context. "Brabo" shifted meaning for me. At first it seemed to be an insult, then praise, then something close to a password.
- 22 September, the fountain paragraph. It's one long run of overlapping talk, and I couldn't tell who said what. I think that was on purpose, but I lost the thread of who was in it.
- 27 September, the short entry. "She was at the board." I couldn't tell what had changed between this and the previous scene. I only worked out afterwards that he'd decided something.
- The 28 September notebook paragraph ("Only the feast doesn't move, and a bell goes up when it goes up..."). It's a long rush of reasons, and I wasn't sure which side he was choosing until the tablet went into his mouth.

## Where I got bored

- The legal reading (19 September). I was waiting for Davi to react, and he mostly didn't.
- The first lunch scene, at the stretch of grandfather talk about the Virgin's buzz and what a low stroke in a feast means. I liked Lia's numbers, but the explaining went on a bit before anything happened.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- Lia correcting the grandfather's count: "Four hundred and twelve. You said three hundred and ninety, and there've been twenty-two." Also her spoons in a spiral, and her taking a coin for advice.
- Ceiça packing the torn loam with two fingers: "Loam you can put back. Nobody puts metal back."
- Seu Tonho and the voice message he hasn't played: "Then I'd know what she sounds like now." That one hit hardest. I thought about it for days.
- The mother's accordion story and "I never knew it was a price."
- "It's a good bell, uai. It's a bit low. So are lots of people."
- The mother moving over on the plank and giving him the end of the arm. Later, "Then you'll stand here all week, where I can see your hands."
- The false alarm at the feast. The whole valley passing on "someone in the house is going" about a bell that was only flat. Ceiça's answer, "Somebody is going, uai. Me, out of that chair," made me laugh and then made me sad.
- Seu Tonho watching with his phone and not playing anything.
- The ending: her hand over his, the Virgin sounding fifteen cents down, and "he heard it before he had counted a single stroke, and then he counted."

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared most about Ceiça, and then Davi. I'd also say Seu Tonho and Lia, in small doses. I did keep reading past page 2, but the opening pages were work. I stayed because of the mother and the bell on the table. The fountain scenes made me like the boy a little less, but I think that was meant.

## The question I'd still be thinking about

Was the choice his? He keeps looking for the sentence where it was decided, and never finds it. I also keep wondering whether Tonho will play the message.
