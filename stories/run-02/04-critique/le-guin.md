# Le Guin lens: stage 4 critique of the bible

Blind spots: I judge society, people and language. I am weak on the
mechanism's biology and the arithmetic (Egan and Watts), and I tend to want
more slowness and more instruction than a 4,000 to 7,000 word story can
afford. My Portuguese checks are from general knowledge, not a native ear.

1. [must-fix] **The town has one household in it.** The premise is a rite
   that reshapes kinship, yet no father, grandparent, sibling or younger
   child appears. Ceição learned the cutter "from her father": is he alive,
   and did he step down to her? Who cooks, who sits at the table, who is
   nine years old and watching Davi? Suggestion: give the foundry a second
   hand (an old untreated journeyman, Ceição's father or an uncle) who
   stepped down before the 2052 law and so has no Entrega to remember, and
   put a meal in scene 1 with a younger cousin or untreated small child
   who is not yet anything. Show one family eating, not a household of two.

2. [must-fix] **The seat arithmetic breaks the rite.** The law gives the
   seat "only to someone who has set," yet *firma* finishes the morning of
   the 7th and the nets cure until the 14th. At the Entrega Davi is not set.
   The ending also has him spend the curing week beside his mother and then
   be fixed in her voice, and the flat bell rings at the rite. Decide
   whether the course starts about a week early (tablets 1 to 3 October,
   cured by the 7th) and the week before the feast is the gathering scene.
   That is better story anyway: the week beside her is the most carrier-bag
   material in the bible and should be on the page, not summarised.

3. [must-fix] **Who pays is mostly told, not seen.** Seu Tonho appears
   "once" and his daughter, a *cata-vento*, is a phone-call fact. The people
   who pay are the untreated who set without choosing, the ones who refuse
   to set and wander, and the heirs who set and lose their hands. Give the
   daughter one line of presence (a voice message Tonho will not play,
   which Davi, drifting, hears himself answering in her accent). Also say
   what happens to untreated children: if they exist in the 2033 to 2057
   cohorts they are the unseen cost, and the bible is silent on them.

4. [should] **Invented words.** *Segurador* in ordinary Portuguese means
   an insurer or underwriter, which reads as a joke or an error; the sense
   you want ("one who clings") is nearer *agarrado* or *apegado*. *Pregar*
   is lovely: it means both "to nail" and "to preach," which implies a
   whole culture's suspicion of persuasion. Use that double meaning on the
   page. "Ceição" is an unlikely clipping of Conceição; "Ceiça" is the
   usual one. Check the bell code's "nine strokes for a man, seven for a
   woman" against the real Minas tradition and say it is local if invented.
   A baroque Rosário town would also have a Black brotherhood tradition
   (irmandade, Congado) around this very feast. Leaving it out is not
   respectful; give the bell tower a keeper from it.

5. [should] **Keep the dryness from becoming the lesson.** Ceição's
   "I never knew it was a price" and Rafaela's explanation of what she
   lost are both theme delivered by the people who live it. Let Ceição
   say something sideways about the accordion, not the price; let Rafaela's
   loss come through the cello, played badly in the next room. The ledger
   needs the explicit line only once, in scene 2, and it can be stated by
   Davi's interruption, not hers.

6. [could] **Give the drifting boy one thing that stays his.** The bell
   code he decodes in his sleep is the right anchor. Let it be the one
   thing in the narration that does not change colour with who stands
   beside him, and let the ending hold it: set in her voice, still hearing
   the bells as himself. That makes the fixing a gain and a loss, not only
   a loss, and spares the ending the hope-by-default shape.

Characters: Ceição (forró, mango-coloured bronze) and Rafaela (the cello)
are people. Davi is nearest to a function until he is shown wanting
something small and ordinary, like his dinner.
