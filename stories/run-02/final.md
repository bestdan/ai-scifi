# Strike Note

## 16 September

"Clean profile," Davi said from the stairs. "Hair fat at the shoulder, though."

His mother didn't look round. Ceiça had been at the board since before six, as she was every morning. The board was a plank of ipê cut to the curve of a bell, swinging on an iron post, and she walked it round with both hands flat on the arm. Its steel edge scraped the wet loam into the bell's shape, and the extra came off in a long curl and dropped on her boots. The pivot squeaked each time it came round. The radio on the windowsill was playing the same forró it always played.

Under the loam, in the pit, the core stood waist high, brick and straw baked hard over a basket of coke, and on it she was building the false bell, the clay shape of the bronze to come. It was for the Rosário church, whose big bell, the Virgin, had cracked in July and rung like a frying pan at every Mass since. The brotherhood wanted a new Virgin hung for the feast.

Two millimetres fat, up at the shoulder where the side turned in to the crown. Max. He'd run her drawing at camp in Belo Horizonte, ten days of robotics, one slow afternoon. Model gave every partial of the finished bell to the cent. Strike note, hum, the lot, before any bronze existed. His mother had never once pretended to follow his models. When he'd shown her the partials on the screen she had said that's nice, son, in the voice she kept for the priest's slides at Lent, and gone back to the board, and the bell had come out of the pit right anyway.

She walked the board round once more. The shoulder came true, and she stopped.

"You want it?"

He took the arm. Heavier than it looked. Sixteen years and he'd had his hands on it Saturdays and holidays, a bell or two a season, mostly with her across the yard at something else. The rest was school, camps, theory. Theory took an afternoon. The cutter, never. Metal doesn't go back. The loam wanted the board at one speed, the loam's speed, and nobody had ever given him that as a number. He walked it round. At the waist, halfway down the side, the edge caught and jumped and opened a tear the width of his palm, down to the straw.

"Bricked," he said. "Zero chance. Fully bricked."

Ceiça put her hand in the slurry bucket, packed the tear with two fingers, took the arm back and walked it round twice. The waist was smooth. She wiped her fingers on her thigh.

"Loam you can put back," she said. "Nobody puts metal back. Go and wash, your grandfather's ringing at twelve."

He was in the yard with the hose on his boots when the Rosário tower spoke over the roofs.

Low first: a death. Three: a man. Four high: of the Rosário brotherhood. One low: that is all. The shoemaker on Rua da Cadeia, then, who had been dying since June. The tower had three bells, a small high one, the low one, and the big high one, the Virgin. The high strokes came clean off the small bell; the Virgin had no part in a death.

Vô Zé, his grandfather, came in at a quarter past with rope fibre on his palms and sat down at the head of the table without washing them, because he was seventy-eight and had rung for the brotherhood since before the treatment had a name. Lia was already there. She was nine, his cousin, his aunt's girl, at their table every Sunday, and she had laid the spoons in a spiral. She had been up the tower with Vô Zé twice in the last week and had come down with the whole code, every death and feast and fire. It had taken his mother until she was twelve.

"Did him slow," Vô Zé said. "He liked things slow." He took beans and then more beans. "On the low one. You hear the low one first, you've lost somebody before you've even counted. Pitch before number."

"Pitch before number," Lia said, in his exact rasp.

"And a feast," Lia said, "is three on the small one, one on the Virgin, three on the small one."

"The Virgin's stroke," Vô Zé said. He put his spoon down to say it properly. "Now. The Virgin's been buzzing since July and nobody's read a thing into it. A crack's only a buzz, nobody reads a buzz. But a high bell that sits sour against the low one, that's a low, to an old ear. And one low stroke dropped in the middle of a feast means somebody in that house is going. Not gone. Going."

"Four hundred deaths you've rung for the Rosário," Lia said. "You said so at Easter."

"Four hundred."

"Four hundred and twelve. You said three hundred and ninety, and there've been twenty-two."

Vô Zé looked at the ceiling as if the number were written there in a hand he didn't trust.

Ceiça came in from the foundry and sat down with loam still in the creases of her knuckles, and asked nobody in particular whether the couve had been washed. Lia said it had, with total certainty. It hadn't. Ceiça ate round the grit.

"Three weeks Sunday," Vô Zé said. "The fuss."

"The Entrega," Ceiça said.

"The fuss. I gave her the foundry in a kitchen. This kitchen." He tapped the oilcloth. "Shook her hand, she made coffee. Two thousand and thirty-eight. Nobody rang anything."

"You cried," Ceiça said.

"I had a cold."

"Has he been to the clinic?" Vô Zé asked her, as if Davi were out in the yard.

"Wednesday," Ceiça said.

"Seat's mine on the seventh," Davi said. "Obviously. Mãe stands down, I take the vote and the licence, done. Clean handover."

His mother ate her beans. Nobody said anything about the waist of the Rosário bell. It sat on the table between them with the salt.

"Lia," he said. "What do you think?"

"Advice is one real."

He found a coin. She put it under her plate.

"Take the seat," she said. "Zero chance otherwise. Otherwise it's bricked."

Vô Zé put his spoon down. "Where did she get bricked?"

"I've always said bricked," Davi said.

"Since Thursday," his mother said.

"Don't ask the open for advice, Dadá," said Vô Zé, who was the last person who called him that. "They give you your own back with a ribbon on it." He wiped his bowl with bread. "Not their fault. Nobody asked any of you. A week old and a needle in the leg, and here's the world."

After lunch Davi stood in front of the brotherhood's calendar by the stove, the one with the Virgin of the Rosary in a blue mantle over the months, and counted. Sixteen to thirty was fourteen. Seven more to the feast. Twenty-one days.

## 19 September

The setting clinic was a room at the back of the health post. Past the vaccine fridge. Past a poster about dengue. In the next room someone was playing a cello, the same phrase over and over, and every time it reached the same high note it missed, stopped, and went back. He knocked. The cello stopped.

Rafaela Brum came through the connecting door with rosin on her sleeve. Town setting counsellor. Câmara member. Twenty-four. Her vowels were the wide slow vowels of the first treated kids in the old news clips. She pointed at a chair.

"Davi Ribeiro. Rosário feast. Bell founders." She had a folder. "Brabo. Sit."

Nobody had said brabo for good since he was about eight. The pack at school did her voice behind her back at the fountain. He had done it too. It hadn't been kind.

"I have to read you this," she said. "The law says the whole thing, aloud. Don't help."

She read it flat, at the speed of a woman reading out a bus timetable.

"'Lei da Entrega, 2052, Annex Three. Statement to be read in full to the heir before the agent known as firma is dispensed. One. Whereas council members whose critical periods remain unclosed have shown, from 2049 to 2052, that they revise their positions in favour of the most recent speaker; and whereas their untreated parents, offered no dignified way to surrender their offices, have kept them past the point of competence and been worked around, to their distress; a seat, licence or office with tenure shall pass on a fixed day, by rite, and only to an heir certified closed.'"

She turned the page.

"'Two. In the untreated brain a mesh of proteins, hereafter the nets, forms around certain neurons in childhood and fixes the wiring for hearing, accent, movement and the use of the hands. The treatment given in the first week of life prevents the nets from forming. It also keeps the adolescent sensitivity to the people nearby, hereafter the pull, which in the untreated fades in early adulthood. The mechanism of the second effect is less well understood than that of the first.'" She looked up. "That's the honest sentence. They fought about it for a month. 'Three. Firma, three tablets on three consecutive days, forms the nets and ends the pull. The nets harden over the days after the last tablet, hereafter the curing period, and the subject's state during that period is retained. The process is irreversible.'"

She looked at page two, which was all subclauses, and at the clock, and initialled the bottom of both pages and wrote *read in full* beside her initials.

"You know all that," she said.

"Since I was six."

"So. Things not on the sheet." She put the folder down. "I sat in the assembly for my father's pharmacy for five months in 'forty-nine. Open. We tried pledges. You sign on Monday that you'll vote for the reservoir whatever anyone says. Thursday someone talks to you in the bakery, and you sign a paper taking it back. Next week you take back the taking back. We had a folder of them this thick." She showed him with her finger and thumb. "That's why the sheet."

"I know about the weathervane years."

"You know the word. So. Your case." She counted on her fingers. Quick, dry, a little bored with him. He liked her for it. "First tablet by the twenty-eighth, second on the twenty-ninth, third on the thirtieth. The tablets push the cure faster than nature would. That's their trick. About six days after the last one, so the sixth is the earliest I can sign. On the seventh you take the seat. Your mother stands down and everybody says nice things about her. After, your hands learn like mine. Like anyone's."

"Like yours."

She tipped her head at the wall, where the cello was.

"I started at twenty. Set. Four years now." She didn't say how it was going. He had heard how it was going.

"And if I don't."

"The rite's once a year, feast day. You can take next year's. Until then your mother holds the licence past your feast, and the town has a word for a parent who does that. You know the word."

"Is there a way back? After."

"None yet." She said it carefully, as if each word cost something. "There's a lab in Campinas that opens the nets again in rats, with an enzyme. The rats forget the maze. Everything the nets were holding goes. So, none yet."

"Right."

"One more thing, and then you can go. Whoever you stand next to in the curing week, that's who you sound like. For life. Not slang. The vowels, the tune. People know it, too. Standing next to someone that week on purpose, to fix them in your shape, there's a word for that. Pregar." She opened her hands. "I set at sixteen, beside my boyfriend. Listen to me. Brabo."

"Where's he now?"

"Recife. Six years."

"Did you choose him? To set beside."

She thought about it, which he hadn't expected.

"I was sixteen and open," she said. "I chose whatever he'd said last." She picked up the folder. "Go home and think. Not in the square. At home."

He sat on the steps of the health post in the sun with the notebook he kept for the bells. Obvious. Brabo obvious. The hands were the trade, and his couldn't strickle a waist. Set now: seat, licence, and hands that learned like Rafaela's, four years and still missing the note. Or no tablets. Stay open a year, and his hands would learn his mother's trade at a child's speed, as hers had learned it at nine: a year of bells, eight of them, with her next to him for every one. And all that year, past his feast, the town would call her agarrada, the mother who wouldn't let go of the licence, and watch her hold on to it in the square. Let it. A year. He wrote in the notebook in pencil, in the one hand that had never been anyone's but his:

High and low, turn and turn: the vigil. Nothing's settled yet; the doors stay open all night, and the house waits.

Behind him, through the wall, the cello started the phrase again and missed the note.

## 22 September

Fountain at lunch, eleven of them on the rim, feet in the dry basin, pão de queijo out of one bag, talk already going round and over itself, nobody finishing a sentence because someone else had finished it better: overnight bus, a hostel in Savassi, somebody's aunt with a floor, a lab that took the open for summer work, Christmas, that's ceiling, that's actually ceiling, and he heard himself say ceiling, and laughed, and they all laughed, and it was done. Belo Horizonte at Christmas, all of them. He was in.

A boy from the year below did Rafaela's vowels. Somebody did "Brabo, sit." It went round the rim. He did it too.

One of the untreated boys from his year sat on the far side of the basin with his own bag, following about one word in three. When he laughed it was late, and he laughed anyway.

Walking off down Rua Direita afterwards with the crumbs on his shirt, Davi tried to find the place where it had been decided. Nobody had proposed it. Nobody had said yes. He could have reproduced every sentence, and none of the sentences was the one where it happened.

His mother had sent him for meat. He went to Seu Tonho's because it was nearer.

Seu Tonho, the butcher, was sixty-one, untreated, and the town's one agarrado. His licence hung over the counter in a gold frame with a sprig of rue tucked behind it against the evil eye, and in the glass case below it the good cuts, the picanha and the cupim, had been there long enough to go dark at the edges. The mince tray was nearly empty.

"Ribeiro," Tonho said. "Rosário boy. This feast?"

"Maybe next."

Tonho laughed, a short bark, and wrapped a kilo of mince without being asked. "The boy waits, the mother's agarrada. Ask me." He pushed the parcel across. "Mince they'll buy off an agarrado. Not the picanha."

"Why don't you hand it on?"

"To who?" He wiped his hands. "My Kátia never set. She's a cata-vento. Goes with the wind. Belém in March. Before that Porto Alegre. You can't give a licence to the open, and she's open, so I hold it, and every feast I'm still holding it, and they look." His phone lay on the block beside the scale, face up. "She left me a voice message in August."

"What did she say?"

"I haven't played it." He picked up the cleaver and put it down again. "Then I'd know what she sounds like now."

Walking home with the mince, Davi thought about the cutter. A model on a cutter, his model, would tune truer than any hand alive, his mother's included; there were lathes in São Paulo that did exactly that and sold the bells cheaper. He said so out loud, to Rua do Carmo. A dog on a doorstep watched him go by. Nobody answered.

## 26 September

The bell had been poured on the twentieth, at four in the morning, when the bronze in the crucible came up the colour Ceiça called cor de manga and she said now, and it went down the runner into the mould with a sound like frying, and the whole yard smelled of hot sand and burnt straw. They'd dug it out on Monday and broken the loam off it and wire-brushed it, and now it sat mouth-up on the tuning lathe, ninety kilos of new bronze with the casting skin still on the outside, dull, and the inside bright where she'd skimmed it.

She tapped it at the lip with the rubber mallet and held her head next to it and listened, and then she struck the fork and held that next to it, and listened again. A bell rings five notes at once, the hum under everything and the strike note on top and three more between, and it's tuned when they stand where they should to each other. This one was meant to sit a clean fifth over the old low bell in the Rosário tower, so the two of them could talk.

"Hum's sharp," she said.

"Eight cents sharp," Davi said. He had the app open. "Take it from up the waist. High."

She held out the cutter to him, handle first.

"Your feast," she said. "The heir makes the last cut on the bell for his own feast. Your grandfather made mine." She turned the forró down and stood back from the lathe. "You can only take away," she said. "Where. That's the whole trade."

It was the first cut he had ever made in metal. He knew where. He could have drawn it for her to the millimetre: up inside the waist, a long shallow cut, and the hum would come down and the strike note would barely move. He set the cutter. The lathe turned the bell slowly round on its table, and he brought the tool in, and the tool chattered, and his hand, trying to hold the chatter, went down instead of up. The cutter bit low, near the lip. A curl of bronze sprang off it, bright as a fingernail, and hit the floor.

He took the tool out. She struck the bell.

The strike note had fallen. Not far. Fifteen cents. Far enough that anyone who had stood under a tower for fifty years would hear it, and for good, because nobody puts metal back.

"I'm sorry," he said.

"Hm."

"In São Paulo a machine would've cut it where I meant. My model on a cutter would've..."

"A machine bell's a machine bell." She bent and picked up the curl of bronze and put it in the scrap tin, where it rang. "They pay us for the hand. That's the licence." She nodded at the wall over the bench, at the framed certificate with the federal crest and SINO DE MÃO across the top and her name under it, Conceição Aparecida Ribeiro, founder. "The churches pay for my hand. When it's your name there, they'll pay for yours."

"We'll recast."

"Recast is three weeks, and the feast doesn't move. It goes up Thursday with the clapper tied, same as every new bell, and nobody hears it till the feast. It goes up flat." She ran her thumb round the lip where he'd cut. "The brotherhood paid in March. Their feast, their bell, and a bell on the feast beats a bell in November. You hang what you've got."

"Vô Zé'll hear it."

"Your grandfather's heard worse than that out of that tower." She set the mallet down on the bench and lined it up with the edge. "It's a good bell, uai. It's a bit low. So are lots of people."

He stood with his hands on the lathe table. The bronze under his palms was still faintly warm from the cut.

"Mãe. What should I do?"

She didn't answer at once. She wiped the cutter with a rag, and folded the rag, and put it on the shelf.

"When I was thirty," she said, "I bought an accordion off a man in Lavras, red one, mother-of-pearl buttons, and I sat on the back step with it every night for a year, after the pour, after the dishes, and my fingers wouldn't. They just wouldn't, not the left hand, the bass side. I thought they were bad fingers, that's all, I'd got bad fingers for the accordion and good ones for the cutter. Your grandfather had me on the cutter at nine. Nine to fourteen, forty-some bells, and I don't remember learning it. I remember knowing it." She looked at the bell. "I never knew it was a price."

He waited.

"Eight bells a year," she said, "if the churches keep ordering. You don't take the tablets, you stay open a year, that's eight bells with a child's hands and me beside you every day, not across the yard, and open hands with somebody beside them every day learn a cut in a bell or two. You set on the twenty-eighth and your hands learn like mine learned the accordion, slow, and maybe in ten years you're where I am now, if you stay at it. I don't need the seat. Let them call me agarrada. A year."

Ten years. He thought of the cello through the clinic wall, four years in and still going back for the same note. That was his cutter, then. That was the sound of it.

"You'd do that." He looked at the bell, not at her. "Tonho's picanha goes grey. They'll say you won't let go."

"They'll say. And then they'll want a bell, and they'll come to me for it, because there's three of us left in Minas."

"And Christmas." He hadn't meant to say it.

"What about Christmas?"

"Nothing. The pack. If I stay open, Belo Horizonte, maybe. A few weeks."

She looked at him, and then at his hands on the lathe table.

"Uai," she said. "Then that's a bell you won't make."

The lathe had stopped. The bell sat on it, low.

He didn't say anything else. She turned the radio back up, and the forró came out of it as it always did, and she went to look at the coke. She didn't touch the hum. It stayed eight cents sharp, because the last cut on a feast bell was his, and he had made it.

## 28 September

He came down at six, and she had the loam on the trough and the radio going and didn't look round, and he didn't say anything, and she moved over on the plank and gave him the end of the arm.

## 2 October

The pivot squeaked, and he could hear the squeak now as two notes, not one, the iron on iron as the arm came round and then a smaller one when it settled, and he found he'd been listening to it for an hour.

There was loam under his nails, packed so deep it wouldn't come out with the brush, and loam drying grey on the backs of his hands where it cracked when he made a fist, and he'd been at the practice core since six, and his mother was across the yard at the moulding bench with the next order, a bell for a chapel up past Tiradentes, and every so often she looked over and said nothing, which meant it was going all right. The board came round. The edge caught at the waist, and he felt it before it jumped, and eased, and it didn't jump.

Vô Zé was on the bench by the door with a coffee, in his feast shirt already though the feast was five days off, because he liked to break a shirt in, and he'd been watching for a while.

"You talk like your mother," he said. "Last month you talked like that camp. You don't hear it. None of you ever hear it."

"Zero chance," Davi said. "Uai, I don't, Vô, I talk like I talk, and you can say it to her if you want, I've got the waist coming round on this board."

Vô Zé drank his coffee.

"Dadá," he said, kindly, and went back to watching the board.

The arm came round, and the loam came off the profile in a long curl and dropped on his boots, and he let it lie there and walked the board round again, slow, because it wasn't going anywhere and neither was the loam.

On the sixth Rafaela put him under the lamp in the clinic and looked at the scan for a long time, and asked him to read a sentence off a card, and listened to him read it, to the long vowels at the ends of the words, and her mouth went small, and she signed the form.

"Brabo," she said, and nothing else.

He walked home the long way, by the fountain, which was empty, and tried to find the place where it had been decided, the steps or the board or the chair with the paper cup, the night at the fountain or the morning with her hand an inch off his, and he couldn't, any more than at the fountain the week before. He could have said every sentence of it over again, and none of the sentences was the one where it happened.

## 7 October

The Congado came up Rua Direita at ten with the drums going, the Moçambique in front in their white with the gungas on their ankles, all those tin rattles chattering at once in step.

Behind them came the crowned kings and queens of Congo, and the whole Irmandade after. Ceiça danced with the women in her white skirt, steady and not fast. Davi walked beside her with the drum strap cutting into his shoulder.

When they got to the square in front of the Rosário church, Vô Zé was up in the tower already, with the new Virgin's clapper untied at last, and he rang them in.

## 8 October

At six the loam was on the trough and the radio was on, and Davi was at the board, and the next order was on the core, the chapel bell for up past Tiradentes, and his name was on the wall over the bench now, with hers moved down the frame to where it said apprentice in the new hand of the clerk.

She came across the yard with her coffee and stood at the board beside him, his apprentice, the first morning of it, and watched him walk the arm round once, and twice, and on the third time round she put her cup on the edge of the pit and put her hand over his on the board, her loam-cracked knuckles on his, and slowed it, slower than he'd have thought it could go.

Up the hill the Rosário started on the six o'clock, and the others answered it down the valley, and in the middle of it the Virgin came in fifteen cents down, his cut, the one he had made with her standing back from the lathe, and he heard it before he had counted a single stroke, and then he counted.
