# Stage 3 critique: Cartographer

Blind spots: I judge novelty only. I can't say whether a premise is enjoyable
to inhabit, whether the science holds, or whether it fits the length. I did
no web search this pass; every attribution below is from memory, with
confidence given. "None I know of" is not "none exists". The bare seed is
charted and waived, so these are angle scores. Scores use the rubric formula,
keeping the audit's F, R, Y, E and S unless noted.

## A. The Setting Day

Nearest prior art: *Lear*, as before, and Egan's "Reasons to Be Cheerful"
(1997) plus Chiang's "Liking What You See" (2002) for elective
neuro-modification, all high. Nothing I know of makes adulthood a chosen dose
(low-med confidence it's empty). The two nova are one idea seen from two
sides, which helps. The new risk is the friend who sets early to spare her
the choice. That is the "Gift of the Magi" (O. Henry, 1905) shape: a hidden
sacrifice meant to remove a choice. The mother's "never knew it was a price"
is not a twist but a quiet consolation line, so it carries little novelty.
**Revised: N4, edge-of-map leaning uncharted. N4 F3 R4 Y5 E5 S4 → 34/40.**
Push: make the friend's early setting visibly wrong. It spares her nothing,
and he is the one who ends up unable to follow her.

## B. The Regent

Nearest prior art: Lansing, *Priests and Programmers* (1991), and Chesterton's
fence, both high. The twist, that the old rule hides a real reason neither
party names, is the standard fence reveal. Avalanche forecasting in fiction is
open as far as I know (Høeg's *Smilla's Sense of Snow*, 1992, is only
adjacent, med). Moving off water cleared the audit's cluster 2 but kept the
shape: a local safety board, a village at risk, an old rule. That is cluster
6 (elder knowledge intelligence can't reach), with the twist as the only
defence against consolation. Regency of a treated heir is the real
novelty. **Revised: N3, edge-of-map. N3 F2 R4 Y5 E4 S5 → 31/40.** Push: the
rule protects the regent, not the village.

## C. Wearing Lead

Nearest prior art: "Harrison Bergeron" (1961), high; *Good Bye Lenin!*
(2003), high, for a loving fiction kept up for a family member;
*Matilda* (Dahl, 1988), med, for a bright child misread by adults; "The Gift of
the Magi" again for the mirrored sacrifice. Dropping the reserved seats was
the right move and leaves a smaller, more original core: opacity as a gift,
cluster 9. But the ending (child takes up the parent's lead) is the Magi
reciprocation, the same as D. Continuity: a treated mother 15 years into
parenting needs a treatment cohort older than the brief's "one generation
out", and a treated father living beside her would see through her.
**Revised: N3, edge-of-map. N3 F3 R3 Y5 E5 S5 → 32/40.**
Push: the father has known all along, and the family is three people pretending
to different degrees.

## D. Plain Account

Nearest prior art: Chiang, "Catching Crumbs from the Table" (2000), as
theme; Grimm, "The Elves and the Shoemaker", high, for the night-time secret
helpers; Magi again. This sits in cluster 1, the audit's strongest model
attractor, and its minding half is cluster 4's hidden-kindness shape. The
reveal (his corrections are right) is the best beat, but a cold reader
recognises the father's secret kindness within a few pages. **Revised: N3,
edge-of-map nearer charted. N3 F2 R4 Y5 E4 S4 → 30/40.** Push: the assessor
finds the account complete only because of the father's corrections, and
must decide whether to fail the girl.

## Attractors across the shortlist

- **Three of four (A, C, D) turn on a hidden loving sacrifice that the child
  later matches.** That is one reveal in three coats. Whichever wins should
  not be written as the Magi mirror.
- All four protagonists are teenage girls at a tell-or-don't-tell choice
  (audit cluster 4). Flag for stage 4: consider a boy, or a choice that is
  not about disclosure.
- B and D keep the safety-board or explainer-institution shape (clusters 1, 6).
- A's mechanism is cluster 7, but the audit called it a good one.
- Fix names and settings from the world, not the audit's Ines, Tomasz or
  North Atlantic defaults.

## Ranking (novelty alone)

1. **A.** The only premise whose central object, a chosen adulthood, has no
   close neighbour I know of.
2. **B.** Regency of a treated heir is new, but the twist is charted.
3. **C.** The core is small and fresh, but the ending sits on Magi, Bergeron
   and Matilda.
4. **D.** Strongest attractor and the most predictable reveal.

A should win on novelty because it's the one premise where the nearest work I
can name is Lear, and Lear differs in kind: an abdication run on a calendar,
a body that can't take the seat, and a cost to the child as much as the
parent. Its one weakness is the friend subplot, which imports the
Magi shape from C and D. Cut it, or make it fail. Without it, A is the only
premise that doesn't end on a loving deception.
