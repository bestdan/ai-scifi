# Stage 3 critique: Chiang lens

Blind spots, stated: I'm low on action and momentum, I can over-prize the
measured and the polished, and I avoid antagonists. Premise A in particular
needs Banks to say whether it moves.

## A. The Setting Day

**Question.** Whether you may choose to become a fixed person, and what you
owe the one who would have kept changing. That is "Anxiety Is the Dizziness
of Freedom" territory, with the choice made concrete.
**Feeling.** Yes, and best in the hands: she can model the bell and cannot
cast it, and only the unset brain trains the hand. The concept makes the grief.
**Form.** A countdown. Three weeks, one scene per remaining stretch, ending
on the day without narrating the choice's aftermath.
**Likely failure.** Two nova arriving as a briefing, and the 60-IQ link
snapping. Decouple them: say "learns anything" and drop the number's
mechanism. Cut the friend; his early setting is a second story and it
steals the mother's line, "I never knew it was a price", which is the real
climax. Keep that and spend the words there.

## B. The Regent

**Question.** What a sample is worth against an inference, and whether
a rule whose reason is lost deserves obedience.
**Feeling.** Weaker. It runs beside the idea: the argument is excellent, but
the stakes (a closure plan) sit in a document, not a person.
**Form.** Commission minutes, or the regent's dated stall notes. A
conventional close-third scene sequence wastes the question.
**Likely failure.** Three nova at 6,000 words, plus avalanche science that
must be exact. The twist ("both wrong, the rule protects something else") is
the neat answer; it risks consoling the untreated. Leave the rule's reason
only half-recovered and I would trust it more.

## C. Wearing Lead

**Question.** Whether being fully understood is a good, and whether a love
that is a performance is less real. The grandmother-as-refuge premise
becomes sharper here.
**Feeling.** Strongest of the four. The twist is the novum: the mother's lead
is what being read costs, turned into a gift. Nothing is attached.
**Form.** The girl narrating after the fact, with the early kitchen scenes
left misread and later ones re-seen. The reader should be slightly slow, like
the mother.
**Likely failure.** "Reads anyone completely" asserted, not argued. Show it
once, early, with the father, as a small domestic thing. Also the Bergeron
echo: avoid the word "handicap" and any weights. The 4,500 estimate is
tight for a twist plus two kitchen eras; plan 5,000.

## D. Plain Account

**Question.** Whether consent without understanding is consent, which is
the explainer cluster the audit already marks as the model's reflex.
**Feeling.** Runs beside the idea; the surgery is a pretext for the forms.
**Form.** Four drafts is good, and it is the only thing here the form
requires. It also invites the attractor.
**Likely failure.** Two nova plus a twist; "minding" strains belief, and the
father's corrections being right is the neat answer. Cut minding and keep the
drafts and the surgery, and it stops being this premise.

## Ranking

1. C, because the feeling is the concept.
2. A, because the hands carry the idea and the brief's handover is literal.
3. B, rigor without a body.
4. D, the attractor.

**Why C should win.** It is the one premise in which the novum is not a
setting but a relation: intelligence makes you legible, and the only
privacy left is someone else's slowness. The ending is a real fork
(say it, or wear lead yourself), and neither answer is tidy, since saying
it destroys the gift and wearing lead makes the daughter the performer.
The cost is stated in the premise: a child learns what her mother spent
fifteen years paying. It also keeps the cast to three, and under the tone
charter it is warm without being frictionless. Its one weakness is the brief's
handover; make that the daughter taking the mother's role, and say it plainly
in the closing scene. If the panel needs more movement, A is the safer second.
