# Stage 3 critique: Banks lens, run-02

Blind spots, stated: I favour set pieces and scale, and short stories aren't
my natural form. I will over-reward premises that could sprawl, so discount my
enthusiasm for A and B accordingly. I'm also weak on rigor (Chiang's ground)
and on novelty checks (the Cartographer's).

## A. The Setting Day

**Moral friction.** High, and it's the right kind. Nobody is a villain. The
town demands the setting because a drifting council is a real danger, and the
mother is a casualty of a rite she was never offered a choice in. The friend
who sets early "so that she won't have to choose" is a small, loving piece of
dirty work, and it's his.

**Dirty work.** The town, collectively, which signed off on a child's
irreversible decision by calendar. Good: no single signatory.

**Humour.** It lives in the setting itself. A bell foundry or stone yard gives
you a girl who can model every pour and can't hold the ladle. Add drifting
accents: a treated sixteen-year-old who has caught the vowels of whoever she
last stood near is comic for free. Use it.

**Scale and intimacy.** The scale is a town and a trade, which is modest but
felt, and the rite makes it concrete. One moment of vastness is missing: show
a treated crowd drifting together at a market.

**Enjoyable?** Yes. This is a world to visit. Risk: my own, the drift of
worldbuilding. Keep the town to three scenes.

## B. The Regent

**Moral friction.** Real, but it resolves too tidily. "Both partly wrong, and
the old rule protects something neither reason covers" is a fair twist and
also a consolation: the elders turn out to be right for reasons nobody can
state. The audit's Panglossian warning applies, and I agree with it.

**Dirty work.** Weak. The teacher stalls honestly, the girl argues honestly.
Nobody does harm for good reasons unless the avalanche kills someone, and then
the story turns into a lesson. Give the regent one concealed thing: he does
know why the rule is there, and chose silence.

**Humour.** Almost absent. Commission minutes could carry dry comedy, but
avalanche science sits heavy on the page.

**Scale and intimacy.** Mountain against two people is good, and the closure
plan needs one vivid slope. Three nova in a short story is also too many.

**Enjoyable?** Competent and austere. The least fun of the four to stand in.

## C. Wearing Lead

**Moral friction.** The best on the list. The mother's fifteen-year lie is a
benevolent hegemon's act in miniature: she decided what her daughter needed
and never asked. Telling her ends the gift, so honesty costs something here.
That is a real Banks question: is it right to help someone who never asked?

**Dirty work.** The mother, with no one to sign off but herself. Very good.

**Humour.** The kitchen. A woman pretending, for fifteen years, to be
puzzled by a fridge magnet is funny, and the daughter's first attempts at
wearing lead should be funnier still. Without that, this reads as sad.

**Scale and intimacy.** All intimacy, no scale. It needs one glimpse of the
transparent treated world outside the kitchen, such as a school or a
treated-only dinner party where nobody can lie.

**Enjoyable?** Warm, and well inside the tone charter. Harrison Bergeron is
the Cartographer's concern. The structure matters to me: reveal the lie
late, by reordering. Open with the father's clear sight, then the kitchen.

## D. Plain Account

**Moral friction.** Present, and doubled. Two loving deceptions colliding
at a consent form is neat but schematic, and the father's corrections being
right is more a gag than a wound.

**Dirty work.** Diffuse. The assessor is a faceless institution.

**Humour.** The consent-form genre could be funny, with drafts getting more
and more plain. It is the only premise where the humour has a built-in
structure. Even so, it is my own card, and I'd rather not defend it.

**Scale and intimacy.** A surgery is intimate, and no scale appears. The
audit's strongest attractor holds it down.

**Enjoyable?** Pleasant, and the least surprising.

## Ranking

1. **A**: moral friction in a communal act, built-in comedy, a literal
   coming of age.
2. **C**: the best moral engine, but it lacks scale.
3. **B**: rigorous, but it consoles and has no wit.
4. **D**: the attractor, and the neatest and thinnest.

## Why A should win

A is the only premise where the whole society is complicit in something it
believes is wise, and where the cost of that choice is physical. A girl who
can model a bell and can't pour one is the right size of joke for an F2
story: the novum shows up in her hands, not in a briefing. The rite gives
the handover the brief asked for as a day on a calendar, with living people
on both sides of it. The mother's "I never knew it was a price" is the
moral centre: a parent who was set without being asked, now asked to watch
her daughter pay it knowingly. Cut the friend subplot or fold it into the
girl's last choice, and state the plasticity-to-IQ link in one scene where
someone has a reason to explain it. If the panel prefers C, steal one
thing from it: let the mother, or the town, be doing a kind harm on purpose.
