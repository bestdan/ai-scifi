# Idea cards: chiang

**Blind spots, stated:** this lens runs low on action, conflict and plot
momentum, and it avoids antagonists. It tends to measure where it should
push, and to polish where it should risk. None of these cards has a
villain. Each needs Banks or Watts to find the pressure that would make it
move faster.

- **Assigned:** 1. The question made concrete · Technology as magic, seen from both sides · vampire bat × a medieval scriptorium
- **Obvious card:** The interpreter finds one side quietly cheating, and must choose between telling the being (and watching it sicken) or mistranslating to keep it well. Not written.

## Card 1: What It Costs to Keep

- **Novum:** The being feeds on good faith, and good faith only counts when it costs the keeper something. When one side grows so rich that its clauses cost it nothing, the being starts to fade, though no clause has been broken.
- **How it works:** The treaty is a blood-share, like a vampire bat roost. Each world supplies a thing the other dies without: one side's spore-stock, the other side's nitrogen lift. The being's colour tracks forgone temptation, not compliance. A gift you'd have made anyway registers as nothing.
- **First-order consequence:** Prosperity on one side is slowly killing the treaty's living body.
- **Second-order consequence:** Both sides start looking for ways to make keeping the promise expensive again: invented scarcities, self-imposed fasts, honour taxes.
- **Third-order consequence:** Generosity becomes a performance of hardship, and the poorer side starts to suspect the rich one's fasts are theatre.
- **The story:** The interpreter keeps a nightly ledger of what each side gave up that day. She wants the being to live, and she finds that the only true cure is for the rich side to be tempted for real. That means someone has to offer them a reason to break the treaty.
- **The question and the form:** Is a promise that costs nothing still a promise? The form is her ledger, entry by entry, so the reader reads the sums before the people do.
- **From the stimulus:** The bat's reciprocity: a donor who isn't hungry proves nothing.
- **Why this lens:** A thought experiment about obligation, with the feeling coming out of the arithmetic.
- **Suspected prior art:** None close. The nearest is reciprocal-altruism essay writing, not fiction.

## Card 2: The Gloss

- **Novum:** The treaty exists in two texts, one per species, and they never meant quite the same thing. The being's body is only the overlap of the two readings, and it is exactly as large as their agreement.
- **How it works:** Both civilizations recopy their text by hand each season, as an act of assent, the way a scriptorium copies scripture. A copying drift that narrows the overlap shows up as a cough. Where the two readings genuinely agree, it grows flesh.
- **First-order consequence:** Translation, which used to be diplomacy, is now medicine.
- **Second-order consequence:** The fastest cure is for one side simply to adopt the other's reading. The being would bloom, and one meaning would be erased.
- **Third-order consequence:** Each culture has to ask whether it would rather be understood or be itself.
- **The story:** The human interpreter sits with the being in the margin between the texts. She wants to save it without letting either side swallow the other, so she hunts for readings both could mean honestly. That is slow, and the being is coughing now.
- **The question and the form:** Can two parties keep a promise they understand differently? The form is a glossed page: the treaty clause at the centre, her marginalia around it, and the being's voice in a third hand.
- **From the stimulus:** The scriptorium: copying as devotion, and the error as mutation.
- **Why this lens:** The form enacts the idea, and a real history (dual-language treaties) taken literally.
- **Suspected prior art:** The Treaty of Waitangi as history. In fiction, nothing close that I know of.

## Card 3: Small Clauses

- **Novum:** The being perceives nothing but promises. It cannot see, hear or know the interpreter at all unless she makes it a promise, so keeping it company means becoming a third, very minor party to the treaty.
- **How it works:** Its only sense organ is obligation. Each promise she makes ("I'll come back on the fourth day", "I'll tell you the truth about the weather") makes her a little more visible to it, and binds her a little more. One civilization grew the being on purpose as an enforcement instrument, and calls her promises a protocol exploit. The other believes it was born from their oath, and calls them a rite.
- **First-order consequence:** Friendship with the being is literally contractual.
- **Second-order consequence:** Her small kept promises put colour in it that the great powers' clauses can't. Both sides now want her to stay forever.
- **Third-order consequence:** Leaving would be a breach that wounds it, so her own freedom becomes treaty matter.
- **The story:** Told as a numbered list of every promise she made it, across eleven years, with the ones she broke marked. She wants a friend. She learns a friend is someone you've bound yourself to, and then she has to decide whether to make the one promise that can't be taken back.
- **The question and the form:** Is friendship a promise, and what do we owe a mind we can only reach by binding ourselves to it? The form is the numbered list of promises.
- **From the stimulus:** Bats remember who fed them, and reciprocity is how they recognise one another.
- **Why this lens:** What we owe the minds we make, with the magic-or-engineering split held on the being itself.
- **Suspected prior art:** "The Lifecycle of Software Objects" is the nearest in theme. I name it only as the nearest in theme: the device and the form both differ.
