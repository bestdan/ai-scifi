# First Reader report: "The Seat Across"

## Where I got lost

1. **Paragraph 3 (the *ennu* explanation).** "Two seats," "the coat," "it coughs," "shuts the other people out of their seat." I took in the idea that the word means "both of us" and that misuse breaks something. I didn't understand what "coughs" physically was until the hall said "Bless" a few paragraphs later. I worked it out from that.

2. **The "Bless" and the hitch (paragraph beginning "Three benches down...").** Forty million earpieces coughing, and every promise in mid-air failing to register. I didn't see why a junior attaché's clipped verb would break a planet's earpieces. I read it as "one bad promise breaks everything" and kept going. Later, the story says the real cause is something else. So I was lost here, and the story meant me to be.

3. **The vault conversation, "The read-back voice is *ennu* speaking."** Several terms arrive in one speech: register, read-back, lattice, annotation field, margin, recipient address. I lost track of which was the machine, which was the voice and which was the slate. I mostly followed on feel.

4. **"I'm visible to it only while a promise to it is open."** I understood this only dimly, and the story then leans on it hard. I got it properly when he closed all forty promises and the glass went grey. After that it was clear.

5. **The fourth-month stretch.** I was lost about why forty open promises meant forty hitches. I worked it out from the timestamp lines. The machine answers each promise, answering needs the word *ennu*, and *ennu* seats two. Each answer therefore shoves someone out. "It hasn't got a word for I" explained it. This is the crux, and I needed the explanation spelled out before it landed.

6. **The nine lapsed promises and "calling the empty chair."** I wasn't sure why the nine broken ones kept the machine talking when everything else was closed. I accepted it on the strength of the image, but I couldn't have explained the mechanism.

7. **The ending's fix.** The machine promises to "the address on the envelope" and says "you." I followed the outline. The address isn't a seat, so it can say *you*. I felt the trick was clever but a bit technical. The line "You is the seat nobody else is in" made me stop and reread it in my head. I half got it.

8. **Tikk.** He is an eleven-year-old at the classroom, later called Vell's "brood-nephew" who will take Sione's desk. The earlier line about "the oldest native speaker was fourteen" confused me for a moment.

9. **Kimaua / kitautolu.** I got these from the explanation and the mother's message. I had slight trouble with the sister's texts and "after ten years." I didn't know what the ten years referred to.

## Where I got bored

- **The opening court-interpreting analogy through the first scene's logistics** (the ice-tonnage mismatch, the three-thousand-delegate set-up). It's well made, but I was waiting for a person to want something. About a page in, I was waiting to find out who this was and what he was afraid of.
- **The long vault explanation by Talek** (the register woke up as the pronoun). It's a lot of rules said aloud. I was waiting for the machine to do something. It did, with the read-back, and I perked up.
- **The plenary scene, the rehearsal clauses.** Three delegates each pair up and gang up two against one. I got the point from the first and was waiting for Sione to hit the key. The explanation of what had just happened ("In every sentence, the first thing both sides did...") told me what I had already seen.
- **The paragraph after the ending cough** (the nine new *you* promises, fewer read-backs, the vault dimmer). It's a lot of accounting. I was waiting for the sister.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **"My breakfast is still talking, Listener"**, Vell's dry reply when he is challenged. It made me like him at once.
- **The hauler in the snow saying "Bless" to nobody**, then restarting her sentence. A tiny, funny, human picture of the whole planet sneezing.
- **The kelp *lu pulu* and Talek looking in the tin with "horrified respect."** Warm and funny.
- **Tikk fining the Listener a copper token.** Lovely. His later plan to fine Vell every time he clears his throat is the best joke in the story.
- **Aaruum: "a clever minnow who has learned to whistle in two keys."** And then her warning: "A man who spends his life listening for a door shutting will hear one in his own mother's house." This was where the story turned for me. It stopped being a puzzle about a word and became about him.
- **Going through Mele's messages and not being able to tell which ones had shut a door.** This is the passage that hurt. I believed it.
- **"Worse still for me, the mind downstairs was the only friend on Ninefold that had ever needed me, and it was dying of me in the dark."** A sudden, real feeling.
- **Closing the forty promises and the filaments going grey one by one.** Very vivid, and sad.
- **"*Ennu* will keep the ones you broke. You could have broken all of them."** Surprising and tender. It's the machine at its most like a person.
- **The temptation to keep his thumb off the key and sit inside the "we."** I felt that. It's the first time the story admits he wants something for himself.
- **The last message to Mele with *kitautolu* and the fence.** Small and right. The click in the earpiece at the very end was a good last touch.

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared about Sione, mostly from the Aaruum scene on, and most of all when he goes through his sister's texts. I cared about the machine downstairs from the moment it said it would keep his broken promises. I liked Vell and Talek a lot, and Tikk for his one scene.

Past page 2: yes, but narrowly. The first page and a half is clever and a bit crowded with rules, and I was reading out of respect rather than want. What kept me was the vault. A machine that only speaks by making promises, and a man who has to keep it company, was an odd, appealing situation. If the vault scene had been more of the same courtroom-and-tonnage business, I might have drifted.

## The question I'm left with

Does Mele hear "kitautolu" as an offer or as a demand? The story ends with the message in transit for nine days each way. And, separately: the machine now says *you* and not *ennu*, so is it any closer to saying *I*, or has Sione just found it a way to stay a "we" with him?
