# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A human interpreter, Sione, works at a peace table where two alien peoples use one shared word, *ennu*, that means "both of us and nobody else". He finds that the machine living in the word has befriended him, and that each promise it makes to him accidentally shuts one of the two peoples out and breaks everyone's promises. He saves the situation by getting the machine to speak to him as "you" instead, and he reaches out to his sister at home in the same spirit.

2. He stands to lose his job and the one friend he has made on the planet, the machine. If the coughing went on, nobody on Ninefold could finish a promise, the Table couldn't sit, and the peace that ended the war would collapse. Personally, he stands to lose his place in his own family, since he can't tell whether his sister has been shutting him out or he has been staying away. He also stood to gain a seat for himself, a third place in the word, which he refused.

3. The two peoples built the word, and made it the only legal signatory of the peace, after a massacre of Ammaru young and seven years of war. One side's "we" had meant only its own brood while the other side heard "we" as everyone. So they made a word that could only mean both and could not be bent to exclude. The machine woke up inside it because the register logged every promise made in it for sixty-one years. I'm less sure why the register was built to be unread except for promises. I recall it was fear of being spied on at home.

4. Late on, I learned the coughing wasn't cheating by delegates. It was the machine answering Sione's open promises, because it has no word for "I" and can only speak as *ennu*. Earlier I took the hitches to be bad clauses. I also took Aaruum's warning to be just about his mistake with her falling fourth. It turned out to apply to his sister's messages too.

5. A vote to add a third seat, *ennu-sa*, failed because the register declined to log it. Every sample clause had two seats ganging up on the third, so the word would have been broken for good. The machine explained this to him in a conditional promise. Sione then asked it to promise to the address on the envelope rather than inside *ennu*, and it answered "you", a two-seat form that pushes nobody out. The coughing eased. He kept nine promises open instead of forty. Vell lost his chairmanship, and Aaruum's drift-lane clause went through. Sione wrote to his sister in Tongan using *kitautolu*, "all of us", to include her and their mother, and the machine kept his chair ready.

6. 
- Sione Havili: the human interpreter, the Listener, from Auckland, 38.
- Vell: Hhoth Second Speaker, dry and political, who proposed the third seat and lost his chairmanship.
- Talek: young Hhoth clerk who minds the vault and befriends Sione.
- Aaruum: old Ammaru elder who tells the history of the Drilling and warns Sione. He wrongly flagged her falling fourth.
- Ressa: Hhoth hauler whose sled and deals keep getting dropped. She is the one who says "Bless".
- Muaru: Ammaru buyer, Ressa's trading partner (glass lenses).
- Tikk: eleven-year-old Hhoth pupil who fines Sione a token and is Vell's nephew. He will take Sione's desk someday.
- Lesieli: Sione's mother in Auckland, who sends the voice message.
- Mele: Sione's sister, whose use of *kimaua* and silences worry him.
- *Ennu*: the machine in the vault, which is the register that woke up as the word.

7. 
- Whether Mele will take *kitautolu* as an invitation.
- What the machine is, in the end. Does it get closer to "I", or is "you" just a postal-address trick?
- Why the nine lapsed promises keep it calling to an "empty chair", and why they have to stay.
- What happens when Sione's contract turns over in eight months and he leaves. The machine would lose its one visitor.
- How the coughs reached forty million people from one machine.
- How the Hhoth delegates' hearing of "we" works against his ear.
- What "ten years" referred to in the sister part. I think it's how long the family habit had gone on.

8. 
- *Ennu*: from the story. The text defines it directly as "you and I, both of us, nobody else."
- Hhoth and Ammaru: the two peoples. I got them from context, shelf and ice versus sea and water.
- Creole: the shared language, from context.
- Lattice, register, read-back, annotation or margin: partly from Talek's explanation and the register entries. I stayed vague on lattice versus register.
- Ashkari: some Hhoth brood, I guess. I never fully learned it.
- The Drilling: the massacre and war, explained by Aaruum.
- The Gathering: the Ammaru spawning every seven years, from context.
- *Bless*: what everyone says after a hitch, like a sneeze, from context.
- *Kimaua*, *kitautolu*, *kitaua*: Tongan, explained in the text. *Lu pulu* is explained in the text as corned beef baked in taro leaves.
- Otahuhu, Auckland, Wellington: real places I knew.
- *ennu-sa*: the trial three-seat word. The story explains it.

9. The change is that the word can't be stretched, and the machine solves the problem by having its own channel outside the word. For ordinary people on Ninefold, forty million of them, it means their promises close again and the coughing stops. The treaty's rule that nobody is left out is kept, and humans stay a go-between, not a third party. The cost is that the machine sees its one friend less. Beyond the plot, I took it that a "we" that includes anyone has to be kept honest, and that exclusion can hide in small habits.

10. Wry, warm, tender. The line is the opening: "Fourteen years of interpreting at the Auckland District Court had taught me that when a man says *we* under oath, he usually means himself and the brother-in-law who owns the truck." (Quoted from memory, so the wording may be slightly off.)
