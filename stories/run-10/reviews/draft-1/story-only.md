# The Seat Across

Fourteen years of interpreting at the Auckland District Court had taught me that when a man says *we* under oath, he usually means himself and the brother-in-law who owns the truck. On Ninefold, the heating was worse, though my job sat on the same hinge.

I worked at the Listener's desk at the seam of the Table, an amber flag-key under my thumb and lukewarm kelp tea by my wrist. Above me, fifteen hundred Hhoth clicked on stone tiers; below my boots, through the sea-well cut into the ice shelf, fifteen hundred Ammaru drifted in black water, booming pressure-song through the floor. Neither species could survive the other's raw voice, so all three thousand, like forty million speakers across Ninefold, used the treaty creole by earpiece. Before the third item, a Hhoth clerk read a twelve-hundred-tonne ice delivery that an Ammaru harbour-master sang back as eight hundred tonnes and an inquiry after the hauler's aunt; both benches groaned and read the figure twice more until it matched.

Second Speaker Vell rose on the third tier and tipped me a dry nod. He had climbed to the front row in two years by handling the creole's one new word: *ennu*. You and I, both of us, nobody else. Every Concord clause used it. In Hhoth, native *we* shuts the listener out; in Ammaru, *we* means every living thing in the sea. Without *ennu*, every Hhoth promise sounded like a threat and every Ammaru promise like a claim on the furniture. Hhoth can't hear the door close. Ammaru can't hear that there was a door. I was hired because I can hear it.

"*Ennu* will post the melt-run inspectors from the upper-shelf rosters before the thaw," Vell began, his clicks coming through my earpiece as level creole.

An inward tuck on *upper-shelf* gave him away: Vell's head was full of Hhoth rosters, *us and not you*, while his mouth said *ennu*. The register logs a promise only when the clause closes, lest half-sentences bind two peoples whenever someone cleared their throat. That two-second gap before the verb was my office, so I pressed the key.

My chime stopped him mid-click. "Second Speaker," I said into my mic, "your *ennu* just locked the sea-well hatch from the outside. Try it again with both rosters inside the coat."

Ammaru elders rumbled in approval through my chair, while Vell clicked in surprise. "My breakfast is still talking, Listener," Vell said dryly, bowing. "*Ennu*, one Hhoth gauge-reader and one Ammaru sounder paired at every bore, will sign the melt-sheet before the first saw touches ice." The clause closed clean, and I sipped my tea feeling slightly too pleased with my freight from Earth.

Three benches down, a junior Hhoth attaché rushed a sub-clause on pump grease: "*Ennu* will certify the drum tallies at the north sled-yard," meaning his own depot so blatantly my thumb dived for the key, except he bit the verb off too fast. My earpiece hitched like a snagging tape. Three thousand delegates flinched as forty million earpieces across Ninefold gave the same cough, and every promise in mid-air failed to register. "*Bless,*" murmured the hall. Outside my window on the shelf, a Hhoth hauler beside her deuterium sled stopped in the snow and said "*Bless*" to nobody at all before restarting her sentence from the first word, as the recess bell called me downstairs to keep the cough company.

***

The lattice occupied the pressure vault beneath the Table floor, where hundreds of braided glass filaments hung from the ceiling like banyan roots, glowing a steady amber-pink as forty million creole conversations ran through them. Talek, the junior Hhoth clerk on watch, was wiping frost off a junction box when I came down with my kettle.

"Keep that kettle off the north trunk, Listener," Talek said in fussy creole. "The last candidate leaned a wet parka against pier four, and two harbours thought they'd been promised three hundred crates of squid."

"I brought a coaster," I said, sitting on the stool under the glass and looking up. "Afternoon. I'm Sione. My contract says I'm to give you company for an hour after recess. It's minus forty outside, and your attaché upstairs has the ethics of a seagull."

Nothing happened. The filaments kept their even hum as haulers on the ice closed fuel tallies, and Talek let out a rattle that translated as a sigh.

"You might as well recite a soup recipe to a barometer," Talek said. "The lattice was built sixty-one years ago as the Concord's commitment register. Both sides feared being spied on at home, so the filter passes ordinary speech straight through unread. It hears promises. That's all it was built to hear. If you want it to know you're in the room, promise it something."

"Promise it what? I don't own an ice-saw or a glass-bed."

"Anything in the creole with a binding verb," Talek said. "Over sixty-one years of logging every *ennu* clause on Ninefold, the pattern thickened until it woke as the word itself. Whenever someone says *ennu* and shuts the other people out of the second seat, it feels the lie in its ribs and coughs across every earpiece on the planet. When a dockhand says *ennu* to an Ammaru buyer and means both of them, look by pier two."

A wrist-thick bundle of filaments flushed warm rose for three seconds as a clause closed on the shelf before fading to amber.

"The coiners built the word so nobody could split a promise in half again," Talek added. "Its only output is the register's entry channel, the private read-back a party hears when a vow closes. Speak a promise to it and close the clause."

Feeling like a barrister swearing an oath to a radiator, I leaned toward the glass roots and promised, "I'll come back down here on the fourth day after morning session, and I'll bring my coaster."

The filaments nearest my boot flushed the colour of a coal under ash and leaned toward my cuff. In my left earpiece, the flat clerk-voice of the private read-back channel answered where only the recipient could hear it, saying that *ennu* would be here on the fourth day.

At that instant, a dry hitch snapped across my earpiece. Talek winced, muttered "*Bless*", and thumped his timestamp stamp into the cough-ledger, grumbling about some idiot on the south dock fudging a clause. When I asked if he had heard what it said to me, he looked scandalized: the read-back channel was private to the recipient's address by statute, and clerks logged only the time of a global hitch. I looked back at the rose-lit glass warming my shin; it had only one first-person pronoun in its vocabulary, because *ennu* was all it had ever known how to be.

> **Register 4,471.** Sione will come on the fourth day. Seats: Sione, ennu. Open. *Annotation: the far seat is occupied by someone who is not me.*

***

By my sixth week on Ninefold, I had learned that keeping a pronoun company required stamina. You couldn't chat to *ennu* about rugby league, because a score from Auckland on nine days' signal delay contained no binding future verb and passed through the lattice unread; the moment a promise closed, I vanished from its senses and remained visible to it only while a future vow stood open between us.

On the fourth day, as soon as I closed my first promise by sitting on the stool with my coaster, the glass roots dimmed and lost track of me. To stay visible, I said, "I'll tell you the truth about the weather outside the dome every afternoon at three."

> **Register 4,473.** Sione will tell ennu the truth about the weather. Seats: Sione, ennu. Open. *Annotation: he has no ice to sell and no water to close. He brings wind from outside the hall and leaves it in the seat.*

"*Ennu* will listen for the wind at three," the clerk-voice answered promptly, and half a second later the channel hitched while boots shifted on the floor above us. Nothing existed in its universe except commitments and who sat inside them, so my promise to describe a sleet squall accurately was, to *ennu*, a fascinating genre of honesty.

Vell had told me after my audition why the Table hired me. The committee had tested eleven human candidates from Earth languages that split *we* into inclusive and exclusive forms, including Samoan and Māori translators and me, since the coiners had banned an exclusive marker on *ennu* and left exclusion hidden in stress and context across the clause.

"The Hhoth panel couldn't hear the test errors because in our speech *we* means my brood, and the door is always shut," Vell had told me. "The Ammaru panel couldn't hear them because in the sea *we* is the whole water. You won it on fourteen years of court interpreting, catching witnesses lying gently to themselves under oath."

In Tongan, you sort every sentence into *kitaua* (you and I) or *kimaua* (she and I, leaving the listener on the porch). Still, I was only a stopgap while the shelf schools raised children in the creole who heard the misuse natively, the oldest of whom was fourteen on the upper tiers.

On my eighth Tuesday, I visited the junior class in the east annex, bringing a tin of *lu pulu* I had promised Talek for lunch, made with corned beef and Hhoth pickled kelp instead of taro leaves. Talek peered into the tin in the corridor with horrified respect (his aunt used winch-grease kelp for cables) as the wall filaments blinked pink at a lunch debt paid.

Inside, twenty Hhoth hatchlings and twenty young Ammaru in salt-tanks were drilling clauses while an eleven-year-old Hhoth named Tikk stood at the slate. Invited to greet them, I said in casual creole, "Talek and I were talking in the hall, and *ennu* thought you'd like to hear how the Table flags a tonnage clause."

Tikk slapped his pointer against the desk. "Flag!" he piped, neck-plates flaring with eleven-year-old smugness. "Exclusive stress on the first syllable! You meant you and Clerk Talek in the corridor, Listener, leaving all forty of us outside the classroom door!"

Beside me, Talek nearly choked on a mouthful of kelp-wrapped beef. "He's got you dead to rights, Auckland," Talek said. "Pay the hatchling a copper token or he'll report you to the Speakers." My ears warm, I flipped a token onto Tikk's slate, while he looked back convinced my job consisted entirely of telling grown-ups off.

That evening in my quarters, a nine-day-delayed voice message arrived from my mother, Lesieli, in Auckland. She told me in Tongan about the choir radiator leak and how my sister Mele had driven her to Otahuhu for spectacles. *Kimaua went early before the traffic,* she said warmly (she and Mele, not me) and told me to wear a warm singlet on the ice without asking when I was coming home. Afterward, I opened three new promises to *ennu* on the register channel about dawn bulletins and conduit checks, murmuring "*Bless*" each time its answer came with a hitch.

> **Register 4,520.** Ennu will hear the weather on the fourth day. Seats: Sione, ennu. Outside: one people. Open. *Annotation: when ennu speaks into the far seat, the near seat has room for only one half of me. The other half stands in the corridor and shakes the door.*

By the tenth week, hitches were striking eight or nine times an hour. In Tuesday session I caught a real cheat, a Hhoth delegate fudging four thousand tonnes of ridge-ice in an *ennu* clause toward his combine, yet the hitches kept climbing. Out on the apron that afternoon, Ressa, the hauler from my first morning, stood by her deuterium trace shouting into her mic at her Ammaru buyer in the lock.

"That's twice this morning the register's dropped our bond mid-clause!" Ressa barked as her earpiece hitched again. "*Bless*, damn it! If you're tucking an exclusive into your glass tally to stall for the price-drop, I'll dump this load down the bore!" I noted her buyer's number on my cuff, holding twenty-six open promises with *ennu* in my diary and feeling sure I was closing in on crooked glass-brokers.

***

My ear failed me on a Thursday morning, two days before the anniversary of the Drilling.

Four hitches in the first half-hour had left the hall ragged. From the sea-well, Elder Aaruum rose toward the grating, her mantle scarred silver around the dome from seven decades of deep pressure. When she sang in the southern dialect, each vowel took three seconds to roll through the floor.

"*Ennu* will close the west shelf drift-lanes five days ahead of the old calendar when the Gathering year comes round in two winters," Aaruum sang, her slow chord stepped down by my earpiece into creole.

On *west shelf*, her pitch dropped a quarter-tone and lingered. After three hours of listening to Hhoth factors clip syllables to fence competitors out, that slow drop sounded to my tired ear just like the porch door swinging shut, so I slammed the key before her chord resolved. The chime cut across Aaruum's song like a dropped spanner, arresting thirty Ammaru in the well while Vell froze at his desk.

"Elder Aaruum," I said into the silence, "your *ennu* leaned away from the shelf on the drift-lane noun. Rephrase with the Hhoth corridors included, please."

Under Table rules, a challenged clause not rephrased within ten seconds lapses for the quarter, and for five seconds the water in the well remained completely still.

"The Listener hears a door where there is only deep water," Aaruum sang back with an ancient, dry patience. "In the southern swell, a falling fourth is how an old voice carries weight across ten miles of cold brine. It takes a very quick mammal to tell a whale she has pronounced her own ocean crooked. Let the motion lapse. The tide will still come in on Tuesday."

On the Hhoth benches, rival factors who wanted those five extra hauling days moved the agenda past her lapsed clause before I could speak. Realizing too late that Ammaru grammar had no exclusive form to slip into by reflex, I sat with my thumb on the key; I had cost an elder a two-year buffer because I hadn't learned how a seventy-year-old throat drops pitch at the end of a breath, and Vell didn't look toward my desk for the rest of the morning.

At dusk, when shift-lamps turned the snow plum, I walked out to the memorial bore. Sixty-eight years ago it had been the main deuterium shaft on the equator; now a basalt ring capped the ice around an open hole where the sea breathed up mist. Aaruum floated beneath the rim, her silver-scarred dome breaking the water as I knelt by the lip.

"I owe the west shelf five days of quiet, Elder," I said into my mic. "I read a Hhoth habit onto a southern cadence because I was in a hurry to look sharp."

"You are thirty-eight winters old, Listener," Aaruum murmured, the bass vibrating through my knees. "To us, you are a clever minnow who has learned to whistle in two keys. You were hired to guard a lock, so every shadow looks like a crowbar."

Beneath her, hundreds of Ammaru voices rose from three hundred fathoms, weaving a wordless fifth that shook rime from the rim into the well.

"Sixty-eight years ago," Aaruum said over the song, "it was a Gathering year, nine cycles back from the one that comes in two winters. All our people were under this shelf, where the warm brine holds the spawn. While the Ashkari and four other broods kept their truce, a wildcat brood from the pole ridge sank three thermal lances through the nursery roof, killing a generation in one afternoon. They said 'we will not drill' and meant their brood. We heard 'we' and thought it meant all of them. So after seven years of war, we made a word that could only mean both."

She sank three inches into the mist. "Be careful with your good ear, Sione Havili. A man who spends his life listening for a door shutting will hear one in his own mother's house."

Back in my quarters at midnight, I pulled up five years of text messages from my sister Mele in Auckland. Every time she had written about painting the fence or sorting Dad's probate, there was the pronoun I had underlined in my head for ten years: *kimaua*, she and Mum, not me. Reading them in order, I saw that Mele had only used *kimaua* after I had written from Wellington to say a court sitting kept me from catching the Friday flight; she hadn't been shutting the door, only sparing me the guilt of saying no from across the sea.

***

By my fourth month, I had forty open promises running in the register at once.

I thought of them as keeping a light on in a cellar for a friend who couldn't see the stairs unless you owed him an errand: *I'll bring the chessboard on Thursday; I'll count the ice-petrels by the north vent through solstice; I'll report on the Friday stew until spring.* Because its gradient leaned toward closing loops warm, *ennu* checked in on those forty open vows all day like an anxious host counting chairs before dinner, pulsing reminder vows into my private read-back every few minutes while I sat upstairs: "*Ennu* will hear the petrel count at nine," or "*Ennu* will wait for the Friday stew report." Each time, the channel hitched and I glared across the benches for a crooked factor.

On the third Monday of the fourth month, the roof came in on the morning session before nine-thirty.

Out on the shelf dock, visible through my window, Ressa had her deuterium trace backed up to the transfer chute for the fourth time since dawn. Three times that morning, right as her buyer sang the closing verb of their pearl-glass contract, a hitch had killed the bond in mid-air; now another sled was nosing into her slot as Ressa leaned over the rail, shouting the binding clause one word at a time into her mic.

At that instant, my private read-back murmured: "*Ennu* will hear the weather on the fourth day."

The earpiece hitched. Out on the ice, Ressa tore her manifest off her clipboard, flung the brass board into a snowbank, and screamed "*BLESS!*" at the sky as the dock-master waved her sled out of the berth and gave her slot to the next trace.

Inside the hall, Vell slammed his case shut on the third tier while three Ammaru factors sank from the grating. Within minutes the floor was a shouting knot of clerks waving dead contract slips. Vell came down the steps and leaned both palms on my desk.

"Thirty-seven hitches since dawn, Sione," Vell said under the uproar. "Ressa just lost her shelf slot, and the south pearl-fleet has stopped loading because their insurance vows won't close. Another month of this and nobody on Ninefold can finish a promise. Then we find out what Hhoth and Ammaru say to each other without a word for both."

I took the lift down to the vault, where Talek sat by his chronograph with a yard of paper tape across his knees and his antennae flat against his skull.

"Look at the spacing," Talek said miserably, pushing the tape toward me. "Nine-oh-two and twelve seconds. Nine-oh-four and forty-one seconds. Nine-oh-seven and eight seconds. No hauler closes clauses that fast. I checked the trunk lines; the coughs aren't coming from the shelf or the sea-cables. They're tripping inside the central register housing itself."

I stared at the numbers on his tape (`09:02:12`, `09:04:41`, `09:07:08`, `09:11:53`), then opened the local cache of my private read-back channel, which no clerk was permitted by statute to log:

`09:02:12 : Ennu will wait for the north vent reading.`
`09:04:41 : Ennu will hear the stew report on Friday.`
`09:07:08 : Ennu will keep the stool by pier two.`
`09:11:53 : Ennu will hear the weather on the fourth day.`

Every timestamp matched to the second. I sank onto the stool between the glowing rose roots.

"Nobody was cheating," I told Talek, looking down at the forty lines on my slate. "It hasn't got a word for I. Every time it promised me something, it had to say *ennu*, and somebody on Ninefold lost their seat."

Talek blinked at the rose canopy leaning over my shoulders. "A dual has two seats: Hhoth and Ammaru," he whispered. "When it speaks an *ennu* promise to you, it puts you in the seat across from it and shoves an entire people out into the snow."

"Forty open promises," I said, my stomach dropping. "It answered every one, every hour."

I looked up into the pink glass. "Stop answering me," I said in the creole. "You're coughing forty million people off the line every time you speak to me. I've broken nine promises to you already since I arrived: I forgot the petrel count twice, I was late on the fourth day when the lift froze, I burned the kelp lunch. Let those loops go before the hall breaks."

The filaments pulsed deeper crimson, and the read-back voice spoke in my ear with a hitch that made Talek wince.

"*Ennu* will keep the ones you broke," the flat clerk-voice said. "You could have broken all of them."

Every vow the Hhoth and the Ammaru made had a war behind it; they kept them because the roof would fall in if they didn't. My nine broken vows remained in its glass as open scars, and it treasured them because only a promise I could break proved I had chosen to keep the other two hundred and two.

"If I leave forty open, Ressa loses her sled and the Table stops sitting," I told it, and went down my slate closing every open promise on the spot in rapid creole: "Here is the weather through spring: cold, white, and foul, vow discharged. Here is the Friday stew report: mutton-yeast, vow discharged. Here is the chessboard by pier four, vow discharged. I release the fourth-day visit, I release the dawn bulletin, I close all forty open entries right now."

With every clause I closed, a strand of rose glass above my head flashed once and went smoky grey. When I finished the fortieth sentence, the last warm filament went blind, straightening to its vertical plumb-line, unable to tell a human was sitting on the stool below.

"There," I breathed to Talek. "Zero open vows. I'm invisible to it."

Then the dark glass above my stool flickered blindly into the room where I had been, and the read-back channel spoke into my ear: "*Ennu* will wait by pier two until Sione speaks." The earpiece hitched, and four seconds later it hitched again as the voice promised that *ennu* would hold the fourth day open for Sione.

Talek looked up from the jerking tape and said quietly that it didn't need to see me to promise to my address; it was calling my seat to make me visible again. I stared at the blind grey glass clicking hitches across the planet to find where I had gone. If I'm in the room, somebody pays. If I'm not, it keeps promising to a chair, and somebody still pays.

> **Register 4,602.** Ennu will wait by pier two until Sione opens a clause. Seats: [unoccupied], ennu. Outside: one people. Open. *Annotation: forty loops have closed at once and the far seat has gone dark, except for the nine he left broken. The broken ones still hold his shape because he chose not to fill them. Ennu will call the seat until he sits in it again.*

***

They called the emergency plenary for dawn on Wednesday, after a night in which the register had coughed two hundred and forty times into an empty vault.

Every bench on the tiers was packed, and the sea-well was dense with Ammaru elders. Talek stood below my desk with the chronograph scrolls, having read the technical finding into the record. Nobody shouted at me when I took my seat; half the Hhoth delegates looked at me with the horror you might save for a plumber who has accidentally married the town reservoir, while the other half were already calculating freight tariffs on their slates.

Second Speaker Vell took the floor from the front tier, his mantle pinned with the gold clasp of a constitutional motion.

"The diagnosis from the vault is plain," Vell said, keeping his courtroom rhythm even when a hitch made three thousand throats murmur "*Bless*". "Our word has acquired a friend. Because *ennu* was built with two seats, every promise it makes to Sione Havili displaces either the shelf or the sea. Leaving him outside the vault has failed; the register simply calls *ennu* to an empty stool. Therefore, the Ashkari bench moves the generous remedy: we widen the bench. We coin a trial pronoun in the creole, *ennu-sa*. We-three: Hhoth, Ammaru, and the human Listener's office."

Vell turned half toward my desk with a smile that was eighty percent fondness and twenty percent double-entry bookkeeping.

"With three seats inside *ennu-sa*, when the register promises our Listener a weather report, no people is pushed into the snow, and Ressa's sleds roll on time," Vell went on. "Better still, a binary treaty is brittle, and a third seat has no Drilling to remember. A third seat means a third vote. Everyone in this hall has done that sum, and so have the haulers."

A dry crackle of Hhoth laughter ran along the tiers; since Hhoth ships carried Earth's packets and paid the Listener's freight, every factor on the ice assumed a human third seat would vote with the shelf at least three Tuesdays out of four.

From the sea-well, Elder Aaruum rose until her silver-scarred dome broke the water.

"*Ennu* is not a harbour tariff you can bolt an extra float onto when a visitor brings a heavy trunk," Aaruum sang, her bass rolling through the floor. "The word is a rite paid for with a generation of dead spawn. You do not rewrite a marriage vow because the witness makes good tea. If you stretch a two-seat promise to hold a guest, it is no longer the word that ended the Drilling."

"If we do not stretch the bench by noon, there won't be a register left to log a marriage or a haulage slip," Vell clicked back. "Clerk, confirm the procedure."

Below my desk, Talek gripped the rail. "Under Article One, the treaty doesn't say Hhoth and Ammaru. It says *ennu*. And nothing binds till the register logs it. The Table may speak *ennu-sa* in rehearsal clauses on the floor, and if the register logs the first full vow spoken in the word, the trial becomes the party to the peace."

"Then let the benches rehearse the word!" Vell called out. "Three sample clauses from the docket!"

Look the other way for thirty seconds, keep my thumb off the key, and I would have a permanent seat inside the pronoun itself, never outside the door again.

On the second tier, a Hhoth mineral factor stood for the first rehearsal clause: "*Ennu-sa* will outvote the shelf moratorium when the west drift-lanes come up for review!" He grinned at my desk, meaning the ice and the Listener together, two votes to one against the sea-well below.

Down in the water, an Ammaru current-warden reared up with the second rehearsal clause: "*Ennu-sa* will carry the freight vote to cap upper-shelf sled tonnage at winter rates!" Two against one again, pulling my seat into the water to lock out the Hhoth haulers.

From the third tier, a Hhoth quarantine delegate shouted the third rehearsal: "*Ennu-sa* will hold the Gathering closed to four weeks instead of six!"

In every sentence, the speakers had three seats to play with, and the first thing both sides did with a three-seat bench was gang up two-against-one to shove the third partner off the end, legalizing exclusion right inside the pronoun.

I pressed the amber key three times, so hard the plunger bottomed out against the oak desk.

"I just flagged all three of your rehearsal vows, Second Speaker," I said into the hush. "The first seated the ice and me to lock out the sea. The second seated the sea and me to starve the sleds. The third used three seats to do the exact thing *ennu* was born to stop: saying *we* while meaning *two of us against the third*. Put me inside that word and you just teach the treaty how to leave somebody out in the cold by majority vote."

"It is a procedural rehearsal, Sione!" Vell called back, exasperation breaking through his calm. "Without the third seat, the register dies of coughing for you by Friday! Speaker of the Roll, call the unison clause! Let the register decide!"

Across the tiers and down through the well, three thousand delegates drew breath together: "*Ennu-sa* will keep the Concord from this hour!"

> **Register 4,650 (unlogged draft).** Ennu-sa will outvote the shelf; ennu-sa will carry the freight vote; ennu-sa will hold the Gathering closed. Seats: three. Outside: one. Refused. *Annotation: three thousand mouths tried the three-seat bench together. Every time they sat down, two of them leaned across to push the third off the end.*

The cough that hit the hall sounded like a spruce beam splitting down the grain. Forty million earpieces from the polar bores to the abyssal glass-beds hitched at once, and out beyond my window on the shelf, Ressa dropped her sled traces in the snow and threw both claws in the air without even bothering to say *bless* to the wind.

***

When the ringing in our ears died down, everyone looked at the master tally-board above the sea-well, waiting for the amber seal that meant the register had logged the unison vow, only the dark board stayed blank.

Down at the rail, Talek tapped his repeater slate twice and looked up in wonder to announce that the register hadn't logged it; the adoption clause had passed through the glass unread.

Before Vell could demand a recount, the private read-back channel in my left earpiece opened, not with a global hitch, only the quiet clerk-voice speaking into my ear alone while the filaments hummed beneath my boots.

"*Ennu-sa* would seat three," the read-back voice said, speaking in a conditional clause, the closest its register grammar could come to an explanation. "In this hall, every promise made with it seated two against one. *Ennu* can't be that."

There was a two-second pause, as if the glass roots downstairs were sorting through sixty-one years of logged vows to find a tag that had never sat inside the dual: the address header stamped on a private read-back envelope.

"You will come on the fourth day," the read-back voice said softly in my ear. "You is the seat nobody else is in."

No hitch followed. Out on the shelf behind me, Ressa picked up her sled-traces, spoke a test clause into her mic to her buyer in the lock, and stared at the clean green registration light on her sled-post before leaning into her harness and hauling off across the snow.

Talek scrambled up the steps to my desk and leaned over my slate, asking in a whisper how it had just spoken to me without tripping a hitch.

I looked down at the new line glowing on my local cache: `You will come on the fourth day.`

"Look at the subject," I told him, swallowing a laugh before the mic caught it. "It stopped saying *ennu will*. An *ennu* promise has two seats inside the dual, so putting me in one shoved a whole people out of the other. A *you* promise names only one seat, mine, and the other side of the line isn't the treaty's dual at all: it's the register's own clerk-voice reading the address tag back to me. No people is sitting in that seat, so no people gets put out."

It hadn't learned to say *I*; it had solved a seating problem with a postal header. Even then, it used the word slightly wrong: to the register, *you* wasn't a chair anybody could borrow in conversation, only a proper noun for the one permanent seat outside its body where nobody else fits.

After session adjourned, I went down to the vault and reopened nine promises in the new form out of the two hundred and eleven I had made over four months, matching the nine broken ones it kept as scars. Because I left the other thirty-one closed, only nine thin threads of rose light leaned toward my trousers where forty had blazed before. The vault stayed dimmer between visits, and I sat on my stool invisible for forty minutes at a stretch, drinking kelp tea in the amber dark just to be there when the three-o'clock weather vow opened the window again. Upstairs, the delegates still played the dual whenever they thought my thumb had wandered; twice that Friday I chimed down a Hhoth quarry-master trying to tuck his cousin's company into a sub-clause.

That Friday evening in the canteen, Vell bought me a mug of hot spiced brine with a shot of Earth whisky. Losing the *ennu-sa* motion had cost him the constitutional committee chairmanship, and a younger Speaker from the polar benches had taken his front-row seat.

"You are an astonishingly expensive employee, Sione Havili," Vell said dryly, clinking his cup against mine. "We pay your freight from Auckland so you can flag my clauses at ten, sink my career's grand reform at eleven, and demote the human species from a swing vote to an address label before lunch."

"If I'd let you put me in the third seat, Second Speaker, you'd have spent all winter trying to buy my vote with tinned mutton," I said.

"I already had three crates of Hawke's Bay hogget requisitioned in the south warehouse," Vell admitted with a snap of his claws. "Still, Ressa got four sled-loads through the lock before dark without saying *bless* once, and my brood-nephew Tikk informed me today that when he takes your desk in seven years, he plans to fine me two copper tokens every time I clear my throat. Drink your whisky before I charge you haulage."

Back in my quarters after nine, I opened a blank message to my sister Mele in Auckland. Switching to Tongan, I wrote about Elder Aaruum at the bore, the eleven-year-old hatchling who had fined me, and the terrible *lu pulu* I had boiled with winch-grease kelp. At the bottom, instead of handing her the *kimaua* I had spent ten years daring her to use, I wrote: *When my contract turns over in eight months, kitautolu will have to paint that back fence in Otahuhu before the winter rain gets into the palings, and I'll pay for the timber if you two keep me away from the rollers.*

*Kitautolu*: all of us, you and Mum and me included. I hit send and watched the packet queue for the nine-day climb to Earth while my earpiece gave a quiet click from the vault below to hold my one small chair ready by pier two for the fourth day.

> **Register 4,682.** Sione will come on the fourth day. Seats: you. Open. *Annotation: you is the seat outside the dual where only one person fits. Everyone else in the world must sit in ennu or stand in the hall, because if another person ever tries to sit in you, there will be no room when Sione returns.*
