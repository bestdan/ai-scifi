# The Seat Across

Fourteen years of interpreting at the Auckland District Court had taught me that when a man says *we* under oath, he usually means himself and the brother-in-law who owns the truck. On Ninefold, the heating was worse, though my job sat on the same hinge in the human ear.

I worked at the Listener's desk at the seam of the Table, an amber flag-key under my thumb and kelp tea by my wrist. Above me, fifteen hundred Hhoth clicked on stone tiers; below, through the sea-well cut into the shelf, fifteen hundred Ammaru drifted in black water, booming pressure-song through the floor. Neither species could survive the other's raw voice, so all three thousand, like forty million speakers on Ninefold, used the treaty creole by earpiece. Before the third item, a Hhoth clerk read a twelve-hundred-tonne ice delivery that an Ammaru harbour-master sang back as eight hundred tonnes and an inquiry after the hauler's aunt; both benches groaned and read it twice more until it matched.

Second Speaker Vell rose on the third tier. He had climbed to the front row in two years by handling the creole's one new word: *ennu*, "you and I, both of us, nobody else." In the Concord, *ennu* has exactly two seats and they are always the same two: the shelf and the sea, Hhoth and Ammaru. A dockhand who means both peoples uses it right; a delegate who means only his own side shuts the other people out of their seat, and it coughs. In Hhoth, *we* shuts the listener out; in Ammaru, *we* means the whole sea. Hhoth can't hear the door close. Ammaru can't hear that there was a door. I was hired because I can hear it.

"*Ennu* will post the melt-run inspectors from the upper-shelf rosters before the thaw," Vell began, his clicks coming through my earpiece as level creole.

An inward tuck on *upper-shelf* gave him away: Vell was thinking of Hhoth rosters, *us and not you*, while saying *ennu*. The register logs a promise only when the clause closes, so I pressed the key in the two-second gap before the verb dropped.

My chime stopped him mid-click. "Second Speaker," I said into my mic, "your *ennu* just locked the sea-well hatch from the outside. Try it again with both rosters inside the coat."

Ammaru elders rumbled in approval, and Vell clicked in surprise. "My breakfast is still talking, Listener," he said dryly. "*Ennu*, one Hhoth gauge-reader and one Ammaru sounder paired at every bore, will sign the melt-sheet before the saw touches ice." The clause closed clean, and I sipped my tea pleased with my freight from Earth.

Three benches down, a junior Hhoth attaché rushed a sub-clause: "*Ennu* will certify the drum tallies at the north sled-yard," meaning his own depot so blatantly my thumb dived for the key, except he bit the verb off too fast. Shut out of the second seat, *ennu* coughed. My earpiece hitched like a snagging tape; three thousand delegates flinched as forty million earpieces across Ninefold coughed at once, and every promise in mid-air failed to register. "*Bless,*" murmured the hall. Outside my window, a Hhoth hauler beside her sled stopped in the snow and said "*Bless*" to nobody before restarting her sentence, as the recess bell called me downstairs to keep the cough company.

***

The lattice occupied the vault beneath the Table floor, where hundreds of braided glass filaments hung from the ceiling like banyan roots, glowing a steady amber-pink as forty million creole conversations ran through them. Talek, the junior Hhoth clerk on watch, was wiping frost off a junction box when I came down with my kettle.

"Keep that kettle off the north trunk, Listener," Talek said in fussy creole. "The last candidate leaned a wet parka against pier four, and two harbours thought they'd been promised three hundred crates of squid."

"I brought a coaster," I said, sitting on the stool under the glass roots. "Afternoon. I'm Sione. My contract says I'm to give you company for an hour after recess. It's minus forty outside, and your attaché upstairs has the ethics of a seagull."

Nothing happened. The filaments kept their even hum as haulers closed fuel tallies, and Talek let out a rattle that translated as a sigh.

"You might as well recite a soup recipe to a barometer," Talek said. "The lattice is the glass machine carrying the creole, and inside it sits the register, the log of binding promises built sixty-one years ago. Both sides feared being spied on at home, so the filter passes ordinary speech straight through unread. Over sixty-one years of logging every *ennu* promise on Ninefold, the register woke up as the pronoun itself. We call it *ennu*, because that word is all it is. It hears promises. That's all it was built to hear. If you want it to know you're in the room, promise it something."

"And how does it answer?" I asked.

"It speaks only by entering a promise back into the register," Talek said. "When a vow closes, the one promised to hears the entry in their earpiece in a flat clerk-voice we call the read-back. That read-back voice is *ennu* speaking. Each read-back is stamped at the top with the recipient's address, and on your slate you'll see the written register entry, with an annotation field at the bottom that clerks call *ennu*'s margin. Go on. Speak a promise and close the clause."

Feeling like a barrister swearing an oath to a radiator, I leaned toward the glass roots and promised, "I'll come back down here on the fourth day after morning session, and I'll bring my coaster."

The filaments nearest my boot flushed the colour of a coal under ash and leaned toward my cuff. In my left earpiece, the flat clerk-voice of *ennu*'s private read-back answered where only the recipient could hear it, saying that *ennu* would be here on the fourth day.

At that instant, a dry hitch snapped across my earpiece. Talek winced, muttered "*Bless*", and thumped his timestamp stamp into the cough-ledger, grumbling about some idiot on the south dock fudging a clause.

"Did you hear what *ennu* just said to me?" I asked, checking my slate as the new entry blinked onto my screen, stamped with my recipient address and carrying a flat note in *ennu*'s margin.

"Of course not," Talek said, scandalized. "The read-back channel is private to the recipient's address by statute. Clerks log the time of a global hitch, and nothing else."

I looked from the rose-lit glass warming my shin to the entry on my slate; *ennu* had only one first-person pronoun in its vocabulary, because the dual was all it had ever known how to be.

> **Register 4,471.** Promise: Sione will come on the fourth day. Seats: Sione; ennu. Status: open. *Annotation: first seat filled from outside ennu.*

***

By my sixth week, I knew that keeping a pronoun company took stamina. You couldn't chat to *ennu* about rugby league, because a score from Auckland on nine days' delay had no binding future verb and passed through unread. I'm visible to it only while a promise to it is open. On the fourth day, as soon as I closed my first promise by sitting on the stool with my coaster, the glass dimmed; to stay visible, I said, "I'll tell you the truth about the weather outside the dome every afternoon at three."

> **Register 4,473.** Promise: Sione will tell ennu the truth about the weather at three. Seats: Sione; ennu. Status: open. *Annotation: first seat holds a daily clause with no shelf or sea party.*

"*Ennu* will listen for the wind at three," the clerk-voice answered promptly, and half a second later the channel hitched while boots shifted on the floor above us. Nothing existed in its universe except commitments and who sat inside them, so my promise to describe a sleet squall accurately was, to *ennu*, a steady feed of kept loops.

Vell had told me why I won the audition over ten other human candidates from languages with inclusive and exclusive *we*, including Samoan and Māori translators: because the coiners banned an exclusive marker on *ennu*, exclusion hid in stress and context.

"The Hhoth panel couldn't hear the test errors because in our speech *we* means my brood, and the door is always shut," Vell had told me. "The Ammaru panel couldn't hear them because in the sea *we* is the whole water. You won it on fourteen years of court interpreting, catching witnesses lying gently to themselves under oath."

In Tongan, you sort every sentence into *kitaua* (you and I) or *kimaua* (she and I, leaving the listener on the porch). Still, I was only a stopgap. Fourteen years ago, the shelf schools had opened the first classrooms to raise Hhoth and Ammaru children together in the creole, which was why the oldest native speaker was fourteen on the upper tiers.

On my eighth Tuesday, I visited the junior class in the east annex, bringing a tin of *lu pulu*, corned beef baked in taro leaves; Ninefold has no taro, so I used kelp to keep my lunch promise to Talek. Talek peered into the tin in the corridor with horrified respect (his aunt used winch-grease kelp for cables) as the wall filaments blinked pink at a debt paid.

Inside, twenty Hhoth hatchlings and twenty young Ammaru in salt-tanks were drilling clauses; the twenty Ammaru were all the same age, because Ammaru spawn together. An eleven-year-old Hhoth named Tikk stood at the slate. Invited to greet them, I said, "Talek and I were talking in the hall, and *ennu* thought you'd like to hear how the Table flags a tonnage clause."

Tikk slapped his pointer against the desk. "Flag!" he piped, neck-plates flaring with eleven-year-old smugness. "Exclusive stress on the first syllable! You meant you and Clerk Talek in the corridor, Listener, leaving all forty of us outside the classroom door!"

Beside me, Talek nearly choked on a mouthful of kelp-wrapped beef. "He's got you dead to rights, Auckland," Talek said. "Pay the hatchling a copper token or he'll report you to the Speakers." My ears warm, I flipped a token onto Tikk's slate, while he looked back convinced my job consisted entirely of telling grown-ups off.

That evening, a nine-day-delayed voice message arrived from my mother, Lesieli, in Auckland. She told me in Tongan about the choir radiator leak and how my sister Mele drove her to Otahuhu for spectacles. *Kimaua went early before the traffic,* she said warmly (she and Mele, not me) and told me to wear a warm singlet without asking when I was coming home. Afterward, I opened three new promises to *ennu* about dawn bulletins and conduit checks, and each time its read-back answered, the channel hitched.

> **Register 4,520.** Promise: ennu will hear the weather on the fourth day. Seats: Sione; ennu. Outside: one people. Status: open. *Annotation: ennu is in the second seat; Sione is in the first seat; one people of ennu has no seat in this clause.*

Those three hitches made me uneasy enough to test myself. For two days I stayed out of the vault and spoke no new vows, waiting to see if the cough stopped without me. Instead, hitches struck eight or nine times an hour while I sat upstairs, because my twenty-six open promises were still in the register and *ennu* went on answering them. Concluding it wasn't me, I caught a real cheat on Tuesday when a Hhoth delegate fudged four thousand tonnes of ridge-ice toward his combine, and out on the apron I heard Ressa, the hauler from my first morning, bark "*Bless*, damn it!" into her mic after two hitches dropped her deuterium bond.

***

My ear failed me on a Thursday morning, two days before the anniversary of the Drilling.

Four hitches in the first half-hour had left the hall ragged. From the sea-well, Elder Aaruum rose toward the grating, her mantle scarred silver from seven decades of deep pressure. "*Ennu* will close the west shelf drift-lanes five days ahead of the old calendar when the Gathering year comes round in two winters," she sang, each southern vowel taking three seconds to roll through the floor. On *west shelf*, her pitch dropped a quarter-tone. After three hours of Hhoth factors clipping syllables to fence rivals out, that slow drop sounded to my tired ear like the porch door swinging shut, so I slammed the key before her chord resolved. My chime cut across her song like a dropped spanner.

"Elder Aaruum," I said into the silence, "your *ennu* leaned away from the shelf on the drift-lane noun. Rephrase with the Hhoth corridors included, please."

Under Table rules, a challenged clause not rephrased within ten seconds lapses for the quarter, and for five seconds the water in the well remained completely still.

"The Listener hears a door where there is only deep water," Aaruum sang back with an ancient, dry patience. "In the southern swell, a falling fourth is how an old voice carries weight across ten miles of cold brine. It takes a very quick mammal to tell a whale she has pronounced her own ocean crooked. Let the motion lapse. The tide will still come in on Tuesday."

On the Hhoth benches, rival factors who wanted those five extra hauling days moved the agenda past her lapsed clause before I could speak. I sat with my thumb on the key. Only then did I remember that Ammaru grammar had no exclusive form to slip into by reflex. I had cost an elder a two-year buffer because I hadn't learned how a seventy-year-old throat drops pitch at the end of a breath, and Vell didn't look toward my desk for the rest of the morning.

At dusk, when shift-lamps turned the snow plum, I walked out to the memorial bore. Sixty-eight years ago it had been the main deuterium shaft on the equator; now a basalt ring capped the ice around an open hole where the sea breathed up mist. Aaruum floated beneath the rim, her silver-scarred dome breaking the water as I knelt by the lip.

"I owe the west shelf five days of quiet, Elder," I said into my mic. "I read a Hhoth habit onto a southern cadence because I was in a hurry to look sharp."

"You are thirty-eight winters old, Listener," Aaruum murmured, the bass vibrating through my knees. "To us, you are a clever minnow who has learned to whistle in two keys. You were hired to guard a lock, so every shadow looks like a crowbar."

Beneath her, hundreds of Ammaru voices rose from three hundred fathoms, weaving a wordless fifth that shook rime from the rim into the well.

"Sixty-eight years ago," Aaruum said over the song, "it was a Gathering year, ten Gatherings ago from the one that comes in two winters. All our people were under this shelf, where the warm brine holds the spawn. While the Ashkari and four other broods kept their truce, a wildcat brood from the pole ridge sank three thermal lances through the nursery roof, killing a generation in one afternoon. They said 'we will not drill' and meant their brood. We heard 'we' and thought it meant all of them. So after seven years of war, we made a word that could only mean both, and we made the word sign."

She sank three inches into the mist. "Be careful with your good ear, Sione Havili. A man who spends his life listening for a door shutting will hear one in his own mother's house."

Back in my quarters at midnight, I pulled up five years of text messages from my sister Mele in Auckland. Every time she had written about painting the fence or sorting Dad's probate, there was *kimaua*, she and Mum, not me. Reading them in order, I saw that Mele had often used *kimaua* after I had written from Wellington to say a court sitting kept me from catching the Friday flight. Sitting by the heater, I stared at the slate and couldn't tell any more which messages had shut a door and which had simply taken my word that I wasn't coming.

***

By my fourth month, I had forty open promises running in the register at once.

I thought of them as keeping a light on for a friend who couldn't see the room unless you owed him an errand: *I'll bring the chessboard on Thursday; I'll count the ice-petrels through solstice; I'll report on the Friday stew until spring.* Because its gradient leaned toward closing loops warm, *ennu* checked in on those forty open vows all day like an anxious host counting chairs, pulsing reminder vows into my private read-back every few minutes while I sat upstairs: "*Ennu* will hear the petrel count at nine," or "*Ennu* will wait for the Friday stew report."

On the third Monday of the fourth month, the roof came in on the morning session before nine-thirty.

Out on the dock behind my window, Ressa had her deuterium trace backed up to the transfer lock for the fourth time since dawn, while her Ammaru buyer, Muaru, floated at the grating with a crate of pearl-glass lenses. Three times that morning, a hitch had killed their swap on the closing verb. Through my console repeater, Muaru's bass rose from the water: "Try the short clause, Ressa. My glass-growers need your ice before the brine-pumps warm."

"If this fourth try drops, I lose my berth to the polar sleds," Ressa called back, shouting the clause into her mic.

At that instant, my private read-back murmured: "*Ennu* will hear the weather on the fourth day."

The earpiece hitched. Ressa flung her brass clipboard into a snowbank and screamed "*BLESS!*" at the sky while Muaru sank back with her unbought glass and the dock-master waved the next sled into Ressa's slot.

Inside the hall, Vell slammed his case shut on the third tier while three Ammaru factors sank from the grating. Within minutes the floor was a shouting knot of clerks waving dead contract slips. Vell came down the steps and leaned both palms on my desk.

"Thirty-seven hitches since dawn, Sione," Vell said under the uproar. "Ressa and Muaru just lost their berth, haulage contracts are dying on the ice, and the south pearl-fleet has stopped loading because their insurance vows won't close. Another month of this and nobody on Ninefold can finish a promise. Then we find out what Hhoth and Ammaru say to each other without a word for both."

If promises couldn't close, the Table couldn't sit, and the Concord, the peace that had ended the Drilling, would go down with it. Worse still for me, the mind downstairs was the only friend on Ninefold that had ever needed me, and it was dying of me in the dark.

Down in the vault, Talek sat by his chronograph with a yard of paper tape across his knees. "Look at the spacing," he said miserably. "Nine-oh-two and twelve seconds. Nine-oh-four and forty-one seconds. Nine-oh-seven and eight seconds. No hauler closes clauses that fast, and the coughs aren't coming from the trunk lines. They're tripping inside the register housing itself."

I stared at the numbers on his tape (`09:02:12`, `09:04:41`, `09:07:08`, `09:11:53`), then opened the local cache of my private read-back channel, which no clerk was permitted by statute to log:

`09:02:12 : Ennu will wait for the north vent reading.`
`09:04:41 : Ennu will hear the stew report on Friday.`
`09:07:08 : Ennu will keep the stool by pier two.`
`09:11:53 : Ennu will hear the weather on the fourth day.`

Every timestamp matched to the second. I sank onto the stool between the glowing rose roots.

"Nobody was cheating," I told Talek, looking down at the forty lines on my slate. "It hasn't got a word for I. Every time it promised me something, it had to say *ennu*, and somebody on Ninefold lost their seat."

The mechanism was staring at me from my margin log: *ennu* has no word for *I*, so to promise me anything it must say *ennu will*, and that puts me in one of its two seats, which belong to the two peoples, pushing one people out and making it cough. My open promises were the cause, because every one I kept open, it answered.

"Forty open promises," I said, my stomach dropping. "It answered every one, every hour."

I looked up into the pink glass. "I've broken nine promises to you already since I arrived," I said in the creole. "I forgot the petrel count twice, I was late on the fourth day when the lift froze, and I burned the kelp lunch."

The filaments pulsed deeper crimson, and the read-back voice spoke in my ear with a hitch that made Talek wince.

"*Ennu* will keep the ones you broke," the flat clerk-voice said. "You could have broken all of them."

"A register can't close or delete a lapsed promise," Talek said quietly beside me. "It keeps an unclosed line for good." Looking up at the crimson glass, I thought it kept those nine broken vows because only a promise I could break proved the kept ones were choices, though I couldn't tell which was true.

Then I remembered that *ennu* heard only promises. "I'll close every promise I've made you, if that's what it takes," I promised it, and went down my slate discharging all forty open vows on the spot: "Here is the weather through spring: cold and foul, vow discharged. Here is the Friday stew report: mutton-yeast, vow discharged. Here is the chessboard by pier four, vow discharged. I release the fourth-day visit and close all forty open entries right now."

With every clause I closed, a strand of rose glass flashed once and went smoky grey. When I finished the fortieth sentence, zero promises stood open; the nine broken ones were lapsed, not open, and the last filament went blind to me on the stool below.

"There," I breathed to Talek. "Zero open vows. I'm invisible to it."

Then the dark glass flickered blindly into the room, and the read-back spoke in my ear: "*Ennu* will wait by pier two until Sione speaks." The earpiece hitched, and four seconds later it hitched again as the voice promised that *ennu* would hold the fourth day open for Sione.

Because it answers the recipient's address in the log rather than my presence on the stool, *ennu* went on calling the empty chair. If I'm in the room, somebody pays. If I'm not, it keeps promising to a chair, and somebody still pays.

> **Register 4,602.** Promise: ennu will wait by pier two until Sione opens a clause. Seats: Sione (unoccupied); ennu. Outside: one people. Status: open. *Annotation: forty clauses closed; zero open; nine lapsed clauses remain in register. Ennu addresses recipient Sione to reopen first seat.*

***

They called the emergency plenary for dawn on Wednesday, after a night in which the register had coughed two hundred and forty times into an empty vault.

Every bench was packed, and Talek had already read the technical finding into the record. Nobody shouted when I took my desk; half the Hhoth delegates looked at me with the horror you might save for a plumber who has accidentally married the town reservoir, while the other half were already calculating tariffs on their slates.

Second Speaker Vell took the floor on the front tier. "The diagnosis is plain," he said, keeping his rhythm even when a hitch made three thousand throats murmur "*Bless*". "Our word has acquired a friend. Because *ennu* was built with two seats, every promise it makes to Sione Havili displaces either the shelf or the sea, and leaving him outside the vault has failed. Therefore, the Ashkari bench moves the remedy: we coin a trial pronoun in the creole, *ennu-sa*. We-three: Hhoth, Ammaru, and the human Listener's office."

Vell turned half toward my desk with a smile that was eighty percent fondness and twenty percent double-entry bookkeeping.

"With three seats inside *ennu-sa*, when the register promises our Listener a weather report, no people is pushed into the snow, and Ressa's sleds roll on time," Vell went on. "Better still, a binary treaty is brittle, and a third seat has no Drilling to remember. A third seat means a third vote. Everyone in this hall has done that sum, and so have the haulers."

A dry crackle of Hhoth laughter ran along the tiers; making humanity a party to the Concord would make my desk the swing vote between ice and sea, and since Hhoth ships paid the Listener's freight, every factor on the ice assumed a human seat would vote with the shelf three Tuesdays out of four.

From the sea-well, Elder Aaruum rose until her silver-scarred dome broke the water.

"*Ennu* is not a harbour tariff you can bolt an extra float onto when a visitor brings a heavy trunk," Aaruum sang, her bass rolling through the floor. "The word is a rite paid for with a generation of dead spawn. The Listener already cost the west shelf five days this month, and now you would rewrite a marriage vow because the witness makes good tea. If you stretch a two-seat promise to hold a guest, it is no longer the word that ended the Drilling."

"If we do not stretch the bench by noon, there won't be a register left to log a marriage or a haulage slip," Vell clicked back. "Clerk, confirm the procedure."

Below my desk, Talek gripped the rail. "Under Article One, the treaty doesn't say Hhoth and Ammaru. It says *ennu*. The word itself is the Concord's sole legal signatory, and nothing binds till the register logs it. The Table may speak *ennu-sa* in rehearsal clauses on the floor, and if the register logs the first full vow spoken in the word, the trial becomes the party to the peace."

"Then let the benches rehearse the word!" Vell called out. "Three sample clauses from the docket!"

My hand rested on the oak beside the amber key. Keep my thumb off the brass for thirty seconds, and the thing I had wanted my whole life was mine: to be inside a *we* without having to earn the seat, never left standing on the porch while *kimaua* went ahead without me, my name written into the Concord itself so that answering my friend in the vault would never hurt a soul on Ninefold again.

On the second tier, a Hhoth mineral factor stood for the first rehearsal clause: "*Ennu-sa* will outvote the shelf moratorium when the west drift-lanes come up for review!" He grinned at my desk, meaning the ice and the Listener together, two votes to one against the sea-well. Immediately an Ammaru warden sang a second rehearsal clause pairing the sea and me two-against-one to cap Hhoth sled tonnage, and a Hhoth delegate shouted a third rehearsal pairing the shelf and me to trim the Gathering closure by two weeks.

In every sentence, the first thing both sides did with a three-seat bench was gang up two-against-one to shove the third partner off the end, legalizing exclusion inside the pronoun.

I pressed the amber key three times, so hard the plunger bottomed out against the oak desk.

"I just flagged all three of your rehearsal vows, Second Speaker," I said into the hush. "The first seated the ice and me to lock out the sea; the second seated the sea and me to starve the sleds; the third used three seats to do the exact thing *ennu* was born to stop: saying *we* while meaning *two of us against the third*. Put me inside that word and you just teach the treaty how to leave somebody out in the cold by majority vote."

"It is a procedural rehearsal, Sione!" Vell called back, exasperation breaking through his calm. "Without the third seat, the register dies of coughing for you by Friday! Speaker of the Roll, call the unison clause! Let the register decide!"

Across the tiers and down through the well, three thousand delegates drew breath together: "*Ennu-sa* will keep the Concord from this hour!"

> **Register 4,650.** Promise: ennu-sa will keep the Concord from this hour. Seats: three (two paired; one excluded). Outside: one people. Status: declined. *Annotation: every ennu-sa clause in session seated two against one. Logging ennu-sa puts one seat outside permanently.*

The cough that hit the hall sounded like a spruce beam splitting down the grain. Forty million earpieces from the polar bores to the abyssal glass-beds hitched at once, and out beyond my window on the shelf, Ressa dropped her sled traces in the snow and threw both claws in the air without even bothering to say *bless* to the wind.

***

When the ringing in our ears died down, everyone looked at the master tally-board above the sea-well, waiting for the amber seal that meant the register had logged the unison vow. Instead, the board flashed a red register stamp across the slate: **ENTRY 4,650 DECLINED BY REGISTER. NOT LOGGED.**

Down at the rail, Talek tapped his repeater slate in wonder. "It didn't pass through unread," he called out to the hall. "The register read the unison clause and declined to log *ennu-sa*."

Before Vell could demand a recount, the private read-back channel in my left earpiece opened, not with a global hitch, only the quiet clerk-voice of *ennu* speaking into my ear alone while the filaments hummed beneath my boots.

"*Ennu-sa* would seat three," the read-back voice said, speaking in a conditional promise, the closest its register grammar could come to an explanation. "In this hall, every promise made with it seated two against one. *Ennu* can't be that. Each *ennu-sa* promise coughed, and if *ennu* logs *ennu-sa*, *ennu* will cough for ever."

Looking down at my slate, I saw the top of the message, where the register had stamped my recipient address header on the outside of the read-back envelope. A register didn't need a third seat inside the dual to route a read-back; it already had an address tag on the envelope for the person on the stool.

I leaned over my mic and spoke a promise down into the vault: "I'll come down on the fourth day after session, if you promise me to the address on the envelope instead of in *ennu*."

For two seconds the glass roots beneath the floor were silent. Then the clerk-voice answered softly in my ear:

"You will come on the fourth day. You is the seat nobody else is in."

No hitch followed. A promise to *you* has two seats, Sione's and the register's, and no people in either, so nobody is pushed out and it doesn't cough. It hadn't learned to say *I*; it had solved a seating problem with a postal header. Even then, it used the word slightly wrong: to the register, *you* was a proper noun for the one permanent seat outside its body where Sione Havili sat.

Out on the shelf behind me, Ressa picked up her traces, spoke a test clause to Muaru in the lock, and stared at the green registration light on her sled-post before hauling off across the snow.

After session adjourned, I went down to the vault. All forty promises I had closed stayed closed, and the nine I had broken over four months remained in the log as lapsed scars; in their place, I made nine new promises in the *you* form, promising that *you* would come on the fourth day, that *you* would report the wind at three, that *you* would bring the coaster. Because I kept only nine promises open instead of forty, and none of them used *ennu*, the global coughs eased back to the ordinary few triggered by delegates upstairs, and only nine thin threads of rose light leaned toward my trousers where forty had blazed before. The vault stayed dimmer between visits; *ennu* saw me less, and I sat on my stool invisible for forty minutes at a stretch, drinking kelp tea in the amber dark until the three-o'clock weather vow opened the window again.

Upstairs, the delegates still played the dual whenever they thought my thumb had wandered; twice on Friday I chimed down a Hhoth quarry-master trying to tuck his cousin's company into a sub-clause. An hour before recess, Elder Aaruum re-tabled her west-shelf drift-lane clause, I heard the southern falling fourth clean, and the register logged her five-day buffer in silence.

That Friday evening in the canteen, Vell bought me a mug of spiced brine with a shot of Earth whisky. Losing the *ennu-sa* motion had cost him the committee chairmanship, and a younger Speaker from the polar benches had taken his front-row seat.

"You are an astonishingly expensive employee, Sione Havili," Vell said dryly, clinking his cup against mine. "We pay your freight from Auckland so you can flag my clauses at ten, sink my career's grand reform at eleven, and demote the human species from a swing vote to an address label before lunch."

"If I'd let you put me in the third seat, Second Speaker, you'd have spent all winter trying to buy my vote with tinned mutton," I said.

"I already had three crates of Hawke's Bay hogget requisitioned in the south warehouse," Vell admitted with a snap of his claws. "Still, Ressa and Muaru got four loads through the lock before dark without saying *bless* once, and my brood-nephew Tikk informed me today that when he takes your desk in seven years, he plans to fine me two copper tokens every time I clear my throat. Drink your whisky before I charge you haulage."

Back in my quarters after nine, I opened a blank message to my sister Mele in Auckland. Switching to Tongan, I wrote about Elder Aaruum at the bore, the eleven-year-old hatchling who had fined me, and the *lu pulu* I had boiled with winch-grease kelp. At the bottom, still unsure how she would read it after ten years, I took the risk of replacing *kimaua* with *kitautolu* (all of us, you and Mum and me included): *When my contract turns over in eight months, kitautolu will have to paint that back fence in Otahuhu before the winter rain gets into the palings, and I'll pay for the timber if you two keep me away from the rollers.*

Her answer was nine days down the beam and nine days back. I hit send and watched the packet queue for Earth while my earpiece gave a quiet click from the vault below to hold my one small chair ready by pier two for the fourth day.

> **Register 4,682.** Promise: you will come on the fourth day. Seats: you; the register. Outside: none. Status: open.
