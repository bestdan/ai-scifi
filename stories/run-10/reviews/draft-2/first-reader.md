# First Reader report: "The Seat Across" (draft 2)

## Where I got lost

1. **Paragraph 2 (the Table, the Listener's desk, the sea-well).** A lot of geography and two species at once, plus "forty million speakers" and "the treaty creole by earpiece." I couldn't picture the room for a while. I sorted it out by the end of the section: Hhoth above on tiers, Ammaru below in water, an interpreter in the middle.

2. **Paragraph 3 (what *ennu* is).** "Two seats... the shelf and the sea... it coughs... Hhoth can't hear the door close. Ammaru can't hear that there was a door." I followed the gist (a word for "both of us" that breaks if you mean only your side). I did not follow how a word "coughs" or what it means that a promise is "logged only when the clause closes." I let it go and kept reading. The later scenes made it clear enough.

3. **The first-morning cough (paragraph beginning "Three benches down").** "Three thousand delegates flinched as forty million earpieces across Ninefold coughed at once, and every promise in mid-air failed to register." I wasn't sure what had physically happened, or why it was the junior attaché's fault. I took it that a cheat in the word makes a system-wide glitch. I worked it out from context only partly. The same scene opens with a lot of invented terms in a row.

4. **Talek's explanation of the lattice and register (the long speech starting "You might as well recite a soup recipe").** Too much at once: lattice, register, filter, read-back, margin, address. I lost track of which was the machine and which was the log. I understood the core, that the log woke up as the word and answers only promises. The rest I held on to loosely. This is where I was closest to putting the story down.

5. **"A promise to *you* has two seats..." and the ending logic.** I got the feeling of it, that the register found a way to address Sione without putting him in a seat. I could not have explained the mechanism. "Seats: you; the register. Outside: none" reads as a clever trick I half trust.

6. **The Tongan words.** *kitaua / kimaua* were explained on the page and I got them. Later *kitautolu* shows up as the swap in the last message. I read it as "all of us including you." I got that from the gloss, not on my own.

7. **The ending's last two lines.** Sione's message to Mele and the register's click "to hold my one small chair ready by pier two" ran together. I wasn't sure for a second whether the click was a promise being made to him, or something else. I worked it out from the final entry.

8. **The nine broken promises.** "*Ennu* will keep the ones you broke... You could have broken all of them." I felt this was meant to matter a lot. I wasn't sure what it was telling me. Sione's own guess ("a promise I could break proved the kept ones were choices") was more than I could follow. I left it unresolved.

9. **The hitch counts and register numbers** (4,471, 4,473, 4,520, 4,602, 4,650, 4,682). I skimmed these. They felt like paperwork between the scenes that I liked.

## Where I got bored

- **The register entries in block quotes in the early sections.** The first one was a nice surprise. The next two or three read like a form being filled out, and I was waiting for something to happen to a person.
- **The stretch after the first vault scene up to the classroom.** The "sixth week" section, with Vell explaining why Sione was hired and the Tongan grammar aside, felt like background piled up. I was waiting for the story to start pushing.
- **The long paragraph where Sione realises the mechanism** ("The mechanism was staring at me from my margin log..."). It explains what I had already worked out one paragraph earlier.
- **The three rehearsal clauses at the plenary.** I saw the point after the first one. The second and third were the same point again.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **The opening line:** "when a man says *we* under oath, he usually means himself and the brother-in-law who owns the truck." That got me at once. I trusted whoever was talking.
- **"Outside my window, a Hhoth hauler beside her sled stopped in the snow and said 'Bless' to nobody before restarting her sentence."** I liked that a whole planet says "bless" at a glitch, like a sneeze. It made the world feel lived in.
- **"If you want it to know you're in the room, promise it something."** Strange and good. It was also where I first felt for the lonely thing downstairs.
- **Sione "feeling like a barrister swearing an oath to a radiator."**
- **Tikk catching him out on the exclusive "we" in the classroom, and the copper token.** Funny, warm, and the first place I saw Sione as someone who gets things wrong in public and takes it.
- **Mele's texts** ("I couldn't tell any more which messages had shut a door and which had simply taken my word that I wasn't coming"). This was the first place the story stopped being about a puzzle and started being about him. The sister and mother in Auckland are what I was moved by most in the whole piece.
- **Aaruum: "A man who spends his life listening for a door shutting will hear one in his own mother's house."** It landed hard, and it joined the work to the family thread.
- **Sione being wrong about the falling fourth and costing the elder five days.** I was surprised he got to be wrong in a way that cost someone. It made the later part matter.
- **"It's dying of me in the dark."** Short, a little ridiculous, and it worked.
- **The register's line "You will come on the fourth day. You is the seat nobody else is in."** The bad grammar is the moving part. Of everything I read, this is what I'd remember.
- **Vell: "You are an astonishingly expensive employee," and the three crates of Hawke's Bay hogget.** Funny, and I was glad to see Vell again, still himself, after losing his chairmanship.
- **The last message to Mele,** swapping *kimaua* for *kitautolu*, and the offer to pay for the timber if they keep him away from the rollers. I was moved here, and a bit surprised that the ending was a text to his sister and not a plenary win.

## Who I cared about, and would I have kept reading past page 2

**Sione.** I cared about him from the first paragraph, mostly for his dry voice, and more once his sister and mother appeared. I cared a little about Ressa, the hauler, because she kept losing her berth to something that wasn't her fault, and a little about Talek and Vell as company. I cared about the glass thing downstairs, though less as a character and more as a lonely visitor I felt sorry for.

**Would I have kept reading past page 2?** Yes, but only because of the voice. The first page and a half hooked me. Page 2, with the invented words and the lattice speech, I got through on trust. If the voice had been less funny I'd have put it down in the vault scene. I'd say I was in, then nearly out, then back in at the classroom and the text messages.

## The question I'm left with

Why did the register decline to log *ennu-sa*, and was it protecting the treaty or protecting Sione? And does Sione's family now hear "all of us" the way he meant it, or does Mele read it as one more thing he promised? The nine days there and nine back leave that open, and it is the one I most want answered.
