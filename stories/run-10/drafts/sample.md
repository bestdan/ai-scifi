Fourteen years of interpreting at the Auckland District Court had taught me that when a man says *we* under oath, he usually means himself and the brother-in-law who owns the truck. On Ninefold, the heating was worse, though my job was the same.

I sat at the Listener's desk at the seam of the Table, an amber flag-key under my thumb and a mug of lukewarm kelp tea by my elbow. Above me, fifteen hundred Hhoth delegates clicked on frosted stone tiers; below my boots, through the sea-well cut into the equatorial ice shelf, fifteen hundred Ammaru drifted in black water, booming their half of the agenda up through the floor in slow pressure-song. Because neither species could survive hearing the other's voice raw, all three thousand of them, like the forty million haulers and glass-growers across Ninefold, spoke the treaty creole through an earpiece. Before the third item, a Hhoth clerk read out an ice delivery for twelve hundred tonnes and an Ammaru harbour-master sang it back as eight hundred tonnes and a compliment to the hauler's aunt. Both benches groaned and read the figure twice more; a click-count often stretched into a song-chord, so nobody closed a sentence with a number in it on the first try.

Then Second Speaker Vell rose on the third tier, snapped his claws over his warming-mantle, and tipped me a friendly nod. I liked Vell; he had the dry posture of a senior barrister who knew where every bad audit was buried, and he had climbed to the front row in two years on the strength of knowing how to use the creole's one new word: *ennu*. You and I, both of us, nobody else. Every clause of the Concord was written in it. In Hhoth, every native *we* shuts the listener out; in Ammaru, *we* means every living thing in the sea. Before the creole, every Hhoth promise sounded like a threat and every Ammaru promise like a claim on the furniture. Hhoth can't hear the door close. Ammaru can't hear that there was a door. I was hired because I can hear it.

"*Ennu* will post the melt-run inspectors from the upper-shelf rosters before the thaw," Vell began, his clicks coming through my earpiece as level creole.

There it was: a tiny inward tuck on *upper-shelf*, like a man pulling his coat-tails out of a puddle. Vell wasn't trying to cheat; after a morning arguing with surface engineers, his head was still full of Hhoth rosters—*us, not you*—while his mouth said *ennu*. The Concord's register logs a promise as binding only when the clause closes, since logging half-sentences would commit two civilizations to a dredging contract every time somebody cleared their throat. That two-second gap before the closing verb was my whole office, so I pressed the amber key before the verb could drop.

A flat brass chime rang out, stopping Vell mid-click as three thousand heads turned toward my desk.

"Second Speaker," I said into my mic, in the pleasant tone I used to save for magistrates who had misread an affidavit. "Your *ennu* just locked the sea-well door from the outside. Try it again with both inspection rosters inside the coat."

Down in the water, a dozen Ammaru elders let out an appreciative rumble that vibrated through my chair legs, while Vell blinked his inner eyelids, clicked once in genuine surprise, and gave me a crooked bow before restarting clean: "*Ennu*, one Hhoth gauge-reader and one Ammaru sounder paired at every bore, will sign the melt-sheet before the first saw touches ice."

The clause closed in silence; the register took the promise down without a hitch, and I drank my dead kelp tea feeling thoroughly worth my freight from Earth.

Three benches down, a junior Hhoth attaché jumped in with a sub-clause on pump grease, rushing to beat the recess. "*Ennu* will certify the drum tallies at the north sled-yard," he rattled off, meaning his own depot staff so blatantly that my thumb dived for the key, except he bit the closing verb off too fast.

In my left ear, the creole channel hitched, a dry catch like a tape snagging on a capstan. Across the hall, three thousand delegates flinched as forty million earpieces across Ninefold gave the same cough, and every promise hanging in mid-air from the ice-ports to the deep pearl-glass beds failed to register.

"*Bless,*" murmured three thousand voices in clicks and rising song at once.

Through the window behind my desk, out on the white shelf, a Hhoth hauler walking beside her deuterium sled stopped in the snow, touched her earpiece, and said "*Bless*" aloud to the empty ice and nobody at all, before leaning into her harness and starting her sentence over from the first word. In twenty minutes the recess bell would ring, and the other half of my job would begin: going downstairs to keep the cough company.
