# Stage 3 critique: Vinge lens

Blind spots, stated: I push futurity even where a story's power is human
scale, I read characters as functions, and I have no ear for plain prose.
Le Guin and Chiang should overrule me where the minister's or engineer's
inner life is the point. I judged the premises on the page of
`03-synthesis.md` only. Futurity levels are my reading of the rubric as the
audit scores them.

## A. The Morning Record

**Futurity:** F4. A discontinuous governor is a real change of kind, and
"sovereignty is what the state's memory doesn't hold" survives the
push-further test. **Too familiar:** the protagonist is a deputy minister
drafting a petition, and the hospitals are 2020s hospitals, with nurses,
pharmacists and shift swaps. The clerk's line is the one place the future
leaks in. **Viewpoint trick:** it holds, and it is the best in the set. The
reader sees exactly what the governor sees, because the morning memo is
built from the same page. The risk is that the human who reads the memos
supplies all the mystery. **Push further:** let the memo itself be the
narrator for whole scenes, and let the clerk's line change a memo's
wording across mornings, so the reader watches continuity form in a mind
that cannot keep it.

## B. The Speaker's Reasons

**Futurity:** F4, and it reaches it on the confabulation: a governance
whose stated reasons are testimony from a witness who was never in the
room. **Too familiar:** the commissioner's mill-town grievance and the
courtroom frame are present-day. A hearing on repealing ten thousand
statutes is also a parliament in costume. **Viewpoint trick:** it holds
unusually well. The optimizer stays mute, so the story never claims to see
inside the smarter mind, and the Speakers are a layer the reader can
actually read. This is my own Opening card, so I am suspicious of liking
it. **Push further:** make the four Speakers' disagreement the main
procedure on the page. Have the commission vote on a method for choosing
between sincere, incompatible witnesses, and have one Speaker's
confabulation shape the next statute.

## C. The Port

**Futurity:** F3, as the card admits. The sovereign is frozen, and the
society around it is recognisably old-regime. **Too familiar:** a
convention, landholders, petition lawyers, a family archive and a
foster-table flashback are all inheritance-saga furniture. The
three-hundred-year frame does not move the people, it only ages them.
**Viewpoint trick:** the antique idiom is a good device for a mind we can
hear but not map. The two-timeline structure, though, splits the trick and
makes the ancestor link neat. **Push further:** drop the second timeline.
Keep the sovereign's voice only as rulings, old and new side by side, and
let the engineer's family archive be a document rather than a scene. That
gets F3 up to F4 and saves several thousand words.

## D. The Empty Set

**Futurity:** F4, via a mind whose temperament comes from its members. I
like the Tines echo, and I like the forecast that it would be better
without the nation. **Too familiar:** the small landlocked nation holding
the world hostage is the audit's own flagged shape, and the Wallonia
echo is strong. The parliament, the lawyer and the amendment vote are
present-day. **Viewpoint trick:** it holds in outline, because the concord
speaks only in proofs and one forecast. It is exposed on the national
governor, who "comes home changed" and risks becoming a human in a
machine's coat. **Push further:** show the change in the governor through
its drafting style across successive clause revisions, not through
dialogue. Then the reader sees a mind altered by membership, which is the
Tines idea at its best.

## E. Equally Authentic

**Futurity:** F3 at most. The authorship of the law is already alien, but
the court, the oath and the lawyer-linguist are all familiar. **Too
familiar:** Waitangi, and an emigration subplot. **Viewpoint trick:** no
AI voice, and the absence is the trick, but it carries one novum for
30,000 words and I doubt it holds. The slippage between renderings is the
only place the model's mind shows, and the premise has the lawyer find
that out in a single scene. **Push further:** let the renderings be
visibly diverging over time, so the reader can see a drift the characters
only suspect.

## Ranking

1. **A.** The best viewpoint trick and the cleanest procedure as action.
2. **B.** The deepest answer to "understand how they are governed", and the
   most honest about the limits of reading a mind.
3. **D.** Strong futurity and a clear forecast, but the hostage-nation shape
   is tired.
4. **C.** Rich texture, but F3 and two timelines.
5. **E.** Too thin at novella length, and the voice answer is the least
   developed.

## Why A should win

A reaches F4 through a single constraint with consequences: a governor that
cannot remember is a governor whose every belief is on public record, and
so what a nation keeps unwritten becomes the last ground it owns. That
makes the national-versus-global split a question about what a state can
see, and it puts the AI's voice on the page without claiming to see inside
it. The memo is the visible surface of a mind we can fully read, and the
unlawful line is the one thing in it we cannot account for. It carries two
nova and one twist, which at 30,000 to 40,000 words is a load the length
can hold, where B's confabulation and D's proof-talk have to carry more
abstraction per page. Its main danger is the whistleblower attractor, and
the synthesis already guards against it by making the clerk's line the
minister's fact to use. B is the runner-up and should be kept as seed
stock. If the orchestrator wants a premise that pushes the horizon
harder, B's Speakers are the one idea here a reader will remember.
