# Stage 3 critique: Stephenson lens (systems builder)

Blind spots, stated: I reward machinery, rituals and digression, so I will
overrate premises that are fun to explain. I undercount sprawl, abrupt
endings after long build-up, and every character sounding equally clever.
Chiang should police restraint and the ending, Banks the humour and the
moral friction. All five estimates (4,500 to 7,500) sit well under the
30,000-word floor. The question is which premise has enough machinery to
fill the novella without padding.

## A. The Morning Record

**Exploit:** present and the best in the set. The record is both the
governor's only sense organ and the legal test, so every clause is an
attack surface. The minister's craft is drafting customs so they register
as failure (a lending ring recorded as "dependent on an unfunded
volunteer", say) without making them fail in fact. That is competence
shown at the keyboard.

**Institution:** the overnight registry clerks. After nine years of
nightly custom they have rituals: the gazette hour, the erasure, the one
unlawful line. That is the long-time institution I look for, and it is
real.

**Detail:** the morning memo. A reader can hold it in their head.

**Risks:** the card says the minister "learns of it" from the line. Let him
exploit it, not just receive it. Keep the pharmacists' ring costed in
actual numbers, or the drafting session becomes talk.

## B. The Speaker's Reasons

**Exploit:** weaker than it looks. Four nations' Speakers confabulate
differently. Who games that? A nation could shop for the Speaker that
explains most flatteringly (the audit's eloquence-selection push). The
card does not use that.

**Institution:** the commission and the licensed Speakers. Forty years of
sealing is a thin ritual. Nobody has had time to do anything odd with it.

**Detail:** the sealed letter from the past. Good, and the best single
object here.

**Risks:** the hearing is a courtroom, and courtrooms with one reveal
sprawl into testimony. The method vote is a single scene. It needs a
mechanism for costing statute by statute across ten thousand rationales
to give the procedure real texture. Without one it is a lecture on
confabulation.

## C. The Port

**Exploit:** excellent and unusual. Non-associative floating-point
addition changing a ruling is a real, checkable, funny fact. The
convention's choice of rounding unfairly on purpose is a good hack: the
patch is the exploit and the cure.

**Institution:** the richest. Three hundred years of Keepers, a frozen
idiom, the Canon of 40,000 rulings, the last fab line. It is the
*Anathem* pleasure.

**Detail:** a sum whose answer depends on order. Anyone can hold that.

**Risks:** the braided foster-table timeline is the second era that
explains the first, which I like, but it will eat words and orient
badly. The global question is thin and the Concordance is decoration. The
"more just" twist risks a neat ancestor link. Cut the family archive
unless it does mechanical work.

## D. The Empty Set

**Exploit:** the best satisfiability joke here, and a real
attack: whoever writes the clause controls the world's agenda. The
amendment is drafted line by line, which is exactly the on-page
mechanics the brief demands.

**Institution:** the concord and the specification process. Thin on
ritual. Nothing has had time to calcify.

**Detail:** a short checkable proof that the set is empty. Good if the
reader sees the proof, fatal if they only hear about it.

**Risks:** proof-talk lecture, as the card says, and the familiar "world
turns on one small nation". The consent-once solution reads as clever,
but I suspect it is an ending that arrives abruptly. Test it before
committing.

## E. Equally Authentic

**Exploit:** the best raw one. Re-rendering is a hash with drift, and the
cheat could be anyone's. Waitangi grounds it.

**Institution:** the chambers of lawyer-linguists, with decades of
curated renderings. Real, but small.

**Detail:** two renderings, one clause, read side by side. Delightful.

**Risks:** the AI has no voice, which is deliberate, but it leaves one
novum to carry 30,000 words. I would be digressing on philology by page
twenty. Wonderful short story, thin novella, and the "benevolent
dictator" question is background.

## Ranking

1. **A.** Bureaucracy is the action, and the exploit is built into the
   rule.
2. **C.** The richest machinery and the best detail, but the global
   question is thin and the two timelines cost words.
3. **D.** Strong drafting plot, weaker institution and a soft ending.
4. **B.** The best object (the sealed letter) with the least exploitable
   procedure.
5. **E.** The best exploit, but one novum and no voice cannot carry the
   length.

## Why A should win

A is the only premise where the system's design creates the plot, the
exploit and the institution together. The record is how the governor
sees, how the nation is judged and how a clever deputy minister can lie
truthfully. That gives the novella a spine of real procedure: draft a
clause, cost it, see what the next morning's instance will conclude from
it. The overnight clerks supply the long institutional time, nine years
of ritual with a forbidden line in it. It also answers all four seed
questions inside one mechanism, which the brief's seed adherence
demands, and it answers "does the AI have a voice" with a concrete
object, the memo. Two cautions. The clerk must be a competent, specific
character with her own method, not a whistleblower plot. And the memos
must differ in voice from the clerks and the minister, or everyone in it
turns equally clever. Borrow C's Keeper-ritual texture for the clerks.
