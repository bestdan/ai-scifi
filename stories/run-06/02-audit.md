# Audit: run-06

Cartographer, stage 2. Thirty cards from ten lenses, plus collisions.

**How to read this.** Confidence applies to the attribution: that the work
exists, that the author and year are right, and that the overlap is as
described. "I know of no fiction" is never the same as "there is none". I ran
WebSearch on every attribution I was unsure of, and on the four premises most
likely to have hidden prior art: the comprehension gate, retroactive consent,
an AI raising cycle in households, and one adviser serving both sides. None of
the four turned up a fiction match. That is weak evidence of absence, and
I mark those cards with medium confidence, not high. Yield is judged at the
midpoint of the brief's range, 35,000 words, written in parts.

**Ground the whole seed sits on.** The benevolent governing AI is charted
territory in its own right. It goes back through Asimov's "The Evitable
Conflict" (1950, high), Jack Williamson's "With Folded Hands" (1947, high),
D. F. Jones's *Colossus* (1966; film 1970, high), Banks's Culture Minds and
Neal Asher's Polity AIs (high), *Person of Interest* (TV, 2011–16, high), and
Rehoboam in *Westworld* season 3 (TV, 2020, high). Naomi Kritzer's "Cat
Pictures Please" (2015, high) is the benign small-scale version. So no card
earns novelty from "an AI governs". It earns it from the mechanism that makes
the governing legible, accepted or contested. Almost every card cites "The
Evitable Conflict" and *Infomocracy* (Malka Older, 2016, high) as its
nearest. That reflex is itself a finding (see Convergence), because the real
neighbours are often elsewhere: in political philosophy, in AI-safety
research, and in treaty law.

Score lines follow the rubric: N F R Y E S, with Novelty and Yield doubled,
out of 40. The **Why** line gives one clause per score in that order.

## asimov

### asimov/The Readback
Nearest prior art: Plato, *Laws* Book IV (c. 350 BC, nonfiction), confidence high. Plato argues that every law should carry a persuasive preamble, so that citizens obey from understanding rather than command alone.
Also near: Lon Fuller, *The Morality of Law* (1964, nonfiction; the parable of King Rex fails on clarity and promulgation), Asimov's "Franchise" (1955, short story; the inverse, a voter who understands nothing), real plain-language statutes.
Distance: real. I know of no fiction that makes comprehension the gate for a statute (medium confidence; search found none). The composition attack is the card's own: three laws that each pass, whose sum no jury ever saw. That is "salami-slicing" legislation, which happens in the real world, turned into the novum's built-in exploit.
Status: edge-of-map (one of six comprehension-gate cards; see Convergence)
Scores: N3 F3 R4 Y4 E4 S3 → weighted 28/40
Why: N, the gate is the panel's most crowded idea, and the composition twist is what lifts it · F, citizenship as comprehension is a basic-category shift · R, the slicing attack follows from the rule and is plausible · Y, the composition test against a working levy is a real choice the novum forces · E, "every brick, no building" fits present law uncomfortably well · S, the levy's beneficiaries and losers aren't anyone specific yet.
To push past the map: the composition test passes, and it turns out that no human-written legal code of the past would have survived it either. Ines's petition then indicts human government, not the AI.
