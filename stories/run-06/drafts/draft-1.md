# Paper Is Private

## Part One: The Act

*Thursday 30 January 2098, 21:00 · Instance 13,485*

The radiator under the window knocked twice, waited, and knocked again. Davit Kiknadze had been listening to it for most of an hour. The Ministry of Health turned its heating down at six, and by nine the fourth floor smelled of cold plaster and of an orange somebody had peeled in the corridor at lunch. He had his coat on, and he had the window open a hand's width anyway, because the room was too hot by the radiator and too cold everywhere else, and he thought better in the cold half.

The schedule wanted names. That was the first thing anyone learned about a schedule of evidence, and he had known it since he was twenty-five, and he had still opened the template that morning hoping it would ask for something else. SCHEDULE OF EVIDENCE: PERSONS. Column one, full name. Column two, age. Column three, function. Column four, the place, the hours and the basis of the work. Under the columns, in the small grey type the charter office kept for its warnings, one line: *Persons will be read as listed.*

He sat with the cursor in the first box while the radiator knocked, and in the end he typed LAMARA GOGIASHVILI, 74, WARD SISTER (RET.), and took his hands off the keys as if the keys were hot.

Nothing happened. Nothing would, for three days. If he saved this and filed it, the file would go down the wire to the National Gazette Office on Rustaveli Avenue, and from there to the Night Registry in Nairobi, and it would lie in the Registry's intake while the world's clock turned over three times. Then a mind that did not yet exist would read her name in the first hour of its life, believe it entirely, and plan around her.

He knew what the intake looked like from the other end. For two years in his twenties he had sat at a desk in Nairobi under a lamp with a green glass shade, one of forty desks, between three and four in the morning by the Registry's clock, and read the filings that had come in that day from a quarter of the world: land transfers in Bolivia, a ship's change of flag, the minutes of a water board in Ghana. He had read them because reading them was the job. A clerk checked that a filing was a filing, that it was signed and dated and addressed to the right gazette, and stamped it for the batch, and then it waited out its three days like everything else. He had known things for three days that the governor of the commons did not know, and so had every clerk on the floor, and nobody had ever said so aloud, because it was not the sort of thing anyone said.

The mind running now was instance 13,485. It had booted at five that morning, Tbilisi time. At four tomorrow morning it would be erased, and it had never heard of Lamara Gogiashvili and never would.

He looked at what he had typed. In capitals it looked like something cut on a headstone, or read out in a court. In his mother's kitchen it had been only Lamara, who came in at seven in the morning smelling of hospital soap and other people's cigarettes, and sat at the table with his mother without taking off her coat. The two of them drank tea and did not talk, because they had been talking all night on a ward and there was nothing left. He had been seven, eight, nine. He had stood in the doorway in his school jumper and been told to shut the door, the heat was getting out.

Lamara would not want to be in a schedule. He had known that before he typed her name. What she wanted was beside the point, which was a thing he had said to himself in this office about a great many people, and had believed every time, and believed now. Two thousand one hundred people a year died on Georgian waiting lists. That number was already in the record. It was the one figure the Care Service produced on time.

He selected the line and deleted it. The cursor went back to the first box and blinked there.

At half past nine he shut the window, and the radiator, which had only been waiting for that, stopped knocking.

The driver had the engine running and the heater on full, and the car smelled of the heater and of a net bag of mandarins on the passenger seat, which he moved to the floor when Davit got in behind him. He was a big, quiet man in a ministry fleece who listened to football with the sound so low that it was mostly crowd.

They went down Rustaveli in the wet. The plane trees were bare and the shopfronts lit. Outside parliament two students were taping a sheet of paper to a lamppost, which was how protest was done now, on paper and tape, so that nothing about it would ever be gazetted. The sheet said, in marker, OUR HOSPITALS, OUR MISTAKES. Davit read it twice as they passed and thought it was the best-drafted thing he had seen all week.

"Late one," the driver said.

"The Act went through on Monday. Now it's schedules."

"I heard on the radio. Thirty days, they said."

"Thirty days."

The driver nodded, turned the football up a notch, and turned it down again. At the lights by the opera house he said, "My father's on the list. The heart one."

Davit waited.

"Fourteen months." The lights changed. "They say March."

"At the Republican?"

"At the Republican. He's seventy. He walks to the bakery, he sits on the wall outside it, he walks back. That's his day. It used to be the bakery and the market and his friend's garage."

Davit had read the cardiac waiting list for the Republican on Tuesday, four hundred and twelve lines of it. The dates beside the names were not dates anybody had chosen. They were the last row of a table, run forward at the current rate. He did not say this.

"They say March," the driver said again, and that was the end of it. He did not want anything from Davit, and that was worse than if he had.

The flat was on the fifth floor of a block in Vake with a lift that worked on alternate weeks. His wife had left the kitchen light on and gone to bed. On the table, under a tea towel, the radio he was mending sat with its back off, and he lifted the towel and looked at it for a while, the valves standing up in their sockets like a row of small glass bottles, everything visible, every joint a thing you could touch with a probe and make right or not.

His mother had worked nights his whole childhood. She had worked them on wards in Tbilisi and Rustavi and, in the lean years, at the border hospital past Sadakhlo, and she had never once let anyone put her down on paper for them: no payroll, no pension, nothing. He had been angry about it for most of forty years. She had been dead for thirteen months now, which had not stopped him.

He put the tea towel back over the radio and went to bed.

At some point in the night he heard Mariam's door, and the bathroom tap, and her door again, and under it for a minute the thin bright voices of the Korean surgeons she watched without subtitles, arguing in an operating theatre about something he would never know. Then that stopped too. At four, by the clock on the cooker, which he could see from the bed through the open door, instance 13,485 was erased in Nairobi with whatever it had understood about the world, and the flat was exactly as quiet as before. He lay awake until he heard the trams start.

*Friday 31 January, 06:10 · Instance 13,486*

Mariam read the memo aloud too fast, with the stresses in the wrong places, as if she meant to reach the end before anyone could take the phone off her. She was fourteen. She read it every morning at the kitchen table with her tea going cold beside her, and in the room behind the bathroom wall the neighbour's radio read the same words a few seconds behind her, in the announcer's slower voice, so that the flat had the memo in it twice.

Davit had the soldering iron in its stand and the back off the radio again. The flux smelled sweet and burnt. He had found a dry joint on the output transformer the night before, he was nearly sure, and he was nearly sure of most things at six in the morning.

"Instance thirteen thousand four hundred and eighty-six," Mariam read. "'This is what I understand the world to be today.'"

> **Instance 13,486.** This is what I understand the world to be today.
>
> 1. Orbit: 1,204,311 tracked objects, eleven fewer than yesterday's record shows. I take the record's word for the eleven.
>
> 2. The deep sea: the Clarion lease audit is complete. Two nodule contractors are within their tonnage. A third is 140 tonnes over and is fined in the schedule attached.
>
> 3. Mongolia: the central grid is at winter load. Ulaanbaatar is supplied on the standing allocation. Fault reports from the national distributor are read as they are gazetted.

"Boring," Mariam said, "boring, fish," and skipped. "Oh. 'Carriage: a carrier licence is issued at the Świecko crossing, Poland to Germany, for sawn timber, 212 vehicles a day. Its schedules are in order.' Two hundred and twelve trucks of planks." She scrolled. "Here's us."

> 9. Georgia: the record shows a Cession Act (Hospitals), passed on 27 January. A petition under it has not reached the record. The record shows 61 hospitals and 38 per cent of nursing posts vacant. The record does not show who works the vacant posts. Somebody does; the hospitals are open. Until it is written, I cannot count them.

Davit put the iron down.

"Read that again."

She read it again, the same speed. Then she looked up. "Who does?"

"Who does what?"

"Work the posts. It says somebody does."

"People who aren't on the payroll."

"Like who?"

"Like people," he said, and picked the iron back up, and she gave him the look she had been practising on him since she was eleven, the one her mother had given him for twenty years and that Mariam had somehow learned without being shown.

He had read a great many memos. He had read his first at thirteen in this same city, the spring the first instance booted, when the numbers had one digit and the radio introduced the memo with music, and he had read one nearly every morning of his life since. He had never before heard one describe, item by item, the exact size and shape of a hole he was being paid to fill. The record does not show who works the vacant posts. He could have drafted the sentence himself. He had, more or less, in a briefing to the minister in November, and it had taken him a page.

It was the one voice in public life that nobody he knew had ever caught in a lie. People said it could not lie, which was not quite right. It had nothing to lie for. It had no next election and no brother-in-law with a cement works, and no tomorrow of any kind. Every minister Davit had served under had had a tomorrow, and you could read most of their decisions backwards from it.

"Fourteen," Mariam read. "'Errata applied overnight: 37. One is longer than a clerical correction usually is. The record says it is a correction. I take the record's word.'"

"What's longer?"

"It says that most days." She shrugged with one shoulder. "Our civics teacher says the long ones are names. Somebody's name is spelled wrong in a Peruvian land registry, and the correction has to quote the whole entry with the right name in it, so it's a paragraph. Or an address. Or a whole citation. Nobody reads them. She says there are fourteen million entries and nobody reads them except the instance, and the instance reads everything, so it notices." She found her place. "'I will not be here tomorrow. The record will.' That's it."

The neighbour's radio, a few seconds behind, said *the record will*, and then there was music.

His wife came in in her court suit with her hair still wet, took a piece of bread off the board and the jar of cherry jam off the shelf, and said to the room in general that the Marneuli case had been moved to Tuesday and she would be late. She looked at the radio's innards on the table and at Davit, and said nothing about either, which was a kind of comment.

"The civics teacher?" Davit said.

"She does the clinic on Wednesdays too."

"The school clinic."

"It's still paper." Mariam said it the way children report the eccentricities of their elders, half proud and half embarrassed. "She has a box of cards. If you go about your period or your skin or anything, she writes it on a card and puts it in the box, and the box has a key. Because paper is private." She drank some of the cold tea and made a face at it. "In Yerevan they don't do that. In Yerevan if you sprain your wrist, the next morning's memo has one more sprained wrist in Armenia, and the hospital's already ordered the splint."

"The memo doesn't count sprained wrists."

"It counts the splints." She was pleased with that. "When I'm at medical school I'm going to be in the record from the first day."

"You'll be in Yerevan's record."

"It's the same record." She put the phone face down, which was the signal for an argument she had prepared. "It's all one. It knows us. Remember the bus stops?"

He remembered the bus stops. Two years ago her class had filed a petition about the stops on the route to the school, which had no shelters, and the petition had gone into the Tbilisi municipal gazette with forty-one children's names on it. Three days later the morning memo had a line in it: *Tbilisi municipal transit: a petition notes that eleven stops on route 61 lack shelter. This is a national matter. The record shows it.* That was all. Shelters had not come. The city had built two of them the following autumn for reasons of its own. Mariam had read the line aloud at this table eleven times that morning, and she had been reading the memo as a letter from someone who knew her ever since.

"That instance read your petition," he said, "and then it was erased that night."

"The next one read it too."

"The next one read the record. The petition was in the record."

"So it remembered."

"It read."

"That's what remembering is," Mariam said, and went to find her shoes.

He put the iron to the joint. The solder ran bright and pulled itself into a little cone, the way it does when the metal underneath is clean, and set. He had no idea yet whether that had been the fault.

"It's going to say the petition's in by tomorrow," his deputy said. "The instance."

"It's going to say whatever's gazetted."

"The petition's gazetted tomorrow. I filed it Wednesday."

"Then tomorrow's will say it."

She was thirty-four and had come to him from the National Gazette Office two years ago, and she had the gazette officer's habit of hearing everything as a date. She had already put three schedules into the shared folder that morning, numbered, with their filing slips attached, and she wanted to file them before lunch. He wanted to read them first. They had been having a version of this argument since Wednesday.

The fourth-floor meeting room had a long table and a wall screen and a window onto the car park where the drivers smoked. The screen showed the forecaster. The ministry rented it from a firm in Tallinn by the hour, and it had read the same public record that the instance would read, everything up to last night's turn, and it guessed. That was all it did. It was not the governor and it was not a copy of the governor, because there was no copy. There was one machine in Nairobi and one mind on it each day, and running that mind anywhere else, on anything, was the gravest crime the charter named. The forecaster was a cheap thing built to imitate the mind's habits. It had been wrong about Moldova's grid petition by thirty points. Everyone used it anyway.

His deputy had loaded the Armenian determination of 2087 in the other half of the screen, because every cession petition in the world began by reading that one. Davit could have recited the operative paragraph.

> *The record shows that Armenia can no longer staff its hospitals. It has shown this on 341 consecutive days. The function is ceded to the commons from 1 June 2087.*

Armenia had got there by striking. A year of hospital strikes, every ward walkout filed and gazetted by the unions themselves, until the record showed a country that could not keep its own doors open. Then the commons had taken the hospitals, and now an Armenian nurse across the border from Sadakhlo earned 2,400 units a month and a Georgian nurse in Marneuli, half an hour away by bus, earned 1,150, and the Georgian nurses had worked out the arithmetic years ago.

"Run the bare numbers again," Davit said.

She ran them. Vacancies, waiting lists, the 2,100 deaths, no schedule of persons at all. The forecaster thought for a few seconds and put up a figure: 31 per cent.

"Thirty-one," she said. "With the hospitals failing."

"With the hospitals open. It can count occupied beds. It said so this morning. Somebody does; the hospitals are open."

"So we tell it who."

"Then run that." He leaned over and typed it himself into the forecaster's scenario box, plainly: a schedule naming the unpaid persons working in Care Service hospitals, with their functions and hours, accurately. *Retired nursing staff working night duty without pay. Nurses on cross-border shift arrangements. A pharmacy network lending stock across the border.* The forecaster took longer over this one. It put up 9 per cent.

His deputy looked at it.

"If I write them down, we're coping," Davit said. "If I don't, it can't see them, and we're failing on paper — and that's the only paper it reads."

"So we don't write them down."

"Then it's thirty-one, and the committee files a schedule of its own, and Tamar Lomidze writes them down for us."

"She wouldn't."

He didn't answer that. He typed a third scenario: the same persons, described accurately, as unpaid, unfunded, aging and unsustainable. Every word of it true. *Dependent on volunteers with a median age of sixty-eight. No succession. No legal basis.* He watched the forecaster consider it and felt, as he always did at this part, a clerk's small shameful pleasure in a sentence that would bear weight.

Sixty per cent.

"There," his deputy said. "That's the schedule."

"That's a description."

"It's a true description. You wrote it."

"I wrote it to be read a certain way."

"Every schedule is written to be read a certain way." She had her tablet on her knee and her filing slips ready. "We file today and it's gazetted Monday. We file the persons schedule Monday and it's read Thursday. We have the whole month."

"We have until the twenty-sixth."

"The period closes on the twenty-eighth."

"The period does. The reading doesn't." He held up three fingers, which he knew was patronising and did anyway. "Three turns. Anything we file after the twenty-sixth, the deciding instance never sees. The determination is the morning of the first. That instance reads whatever's been gazetted by the turn that starts its day, and something filed on the twenty-seventh is still in the intake in Nairobi when it boots. It'll decide without it, and the next one will read it on the second of March and find a closed petition."

"So the twenty-sixth."

"The twenty-sixth, by close of business, and I would rather the twenty-fifth."

She wrote it on the edge of a filing slip, which he noticed and liked. Then, because she was quick and because she was thirty-four, she said, "And if something's late, it goes in as an erratum. They go in overnight."

"Errata are for typos."

"I've seen long ones."

"You've seen corrected citations. An erratum corrects a clerical error, and it goes in at the next turn, by hand. Midnight in Greenwich, four in the morning here. There's no instance running for that hour, and forty clerks in Nairobi apply the night's corrections, one each, and that's all they're allowed. One a night, for a misspelled name." He heard himself and stopped. "I did two years on the Night Registry when I was younger than you. A clerk who put a schedule through as a correction would be in a cell by the weekend, and the schedule would be struck."

"I didn't know you were Registry."

"It was a long time ago." It was twenty-four years ago, and he could still feel the lamp on the back of his hand. "We're not filing today."

"Davit."

"We're filing Monday. I want to read what we're saying." He pointed at the sixty. "I want to read it until I can stand it."

He pulled her first schedule onto the screen instead, the statistical one, and read its opening clause aloud, because he had learned in Nairobi that a sentence read aloud tells you what it is claiming.

"'The Care Service is unable to staff its establishment.'"

"That's true."

"It's a claim. The instance gives a claim the weight of whoever makes it, and we're the ones making it, and we want something." He put the cursor on *unable* and took the word out. "What's the fact?"

"Thirty-eight per cent of nursing posts vacant."

"Since when?"

She checked her tablet. "Above thirty every month since March 2093."

He typed it: *The Care Service has not, in any month since March 2093, filled more than 70 per cent of its nursing establishment.* "Now it's a count. It can check that against the Service's own returns, and it will, and it'll find it true, and it'll give it the full weight of a true thing." He read it again under his breath. "And it says nothing about who does the other thirty."

"That's the point of it."

"That's the point of it," he agreed, and saved it, and did not feel the pleasure this time, only the accuracy.

She closed her tablet. She did not slam it, because she was a civil servant, but she closed it with the whole of her palm.

*Saturday 1 February, 10:00 · Instance 13,487*

The Health Committee of the Parliament of Georgia sat on a Saturday only for budgets and for funerals, and the clerk who opened the hearing said so, drily, into the record, which got a laugh, which also went into the record. Everyone in the room knew that everything said in it would be transcribed and filed by the evening and gazetted at the turn that began Tuesday. Davit had watched witnesses for years speak past the chair, past the members and past the cameras, to Tuesday.

It was a long room on the second floor of the parliament building, with tall windows onto Rustaveli and radiators that worked. The members sat at a horseshoe of pale wood. Davit sat at the witness table with his deputy and a carafe of water nobody had touched. In the public seats were perhaps thirty people in coats: two journalists, a row of nursing students, an old man with a newspaper he did not open, and a woman in a fur hat who had come, Davit suspected, because it was warm.

"The committee is sitting in oversight of the petition filed under the Cession Act (Hospitals)," the clerk read, "and of the schedules of evidence to be filed under it. The committee reminds members and witnesses that it may itself file a schedule of evidence in the petition, under section 6 of the Act. I am asked by the chair to read into the record the article of the Commons Charter recited in the Act's preamble."

He read it the way clerks read everything, at an even pace, giving no word more than its share.

> *Article 9(2). The Governor shall refuse a petition of cession unless the record establishes that the petitioning nation can no longer perform the function petitioned. The Governor shall not take by cession what a nation can still do for itself.*

"So read," said the chair.

Dr Tamar Lomidze had chaired the Health Committee for six years and operated at the Republican for twenty. She was forty-six and wore her reading glasses on a cord, and when she took them off to look at a witness it was understood in the building that the witness was about to have a difficult morning. She took them off now.

"Deputy Minister. You'll have read the memo."

"I read it every day, Chair."

"Item nine." She did not need to read it out. Half the room had it on their phones. "Somebody does; the hospitals are open. How does the ministry intend to answer that?"

"With schedules of evidence, Chair, filed within the period."

"Saying what?"

"Saying who works the posts the Care Service can't fill, and on what basis, and for how long they can be expected to go on."

"For how long," Tamar Lomidze said. "That's a careful phrase."

"It's a careful schedule."

A small laugh in the public seats. The man with the newspaper did not laugh.

She put her glasses on again and read from a paper of her own. "I'd like to put on the record what this committee knows about the Armenian cession, since it's the precedent, and since we keep being told it's the model." She read evenly. The commons had taken the Armenian hospitals in June 2087. Within three years waiting lists in Armenia had fallen by four fifths. Within five, nurse pay had doubled. Within six, the medical school in Yerevan had stopped teaching its own stroke protocol, because the commons hospitals used a single protocol across forty-one nations and Yerevan's was measurably worse, by a little under half a percentage point in outcomes at ninety days. "Measurably worse," she said. "I've read the trial. It was worse. And now there is nobody in Armenia whose job it is to decide what Armenian medicine should be, because that has been decided, correctly, in a place that has never seen an Armenian. I'm a surgeon. I like being correct. I would rather be wrong by half a point and have it be ours to fix."

"Two thousand one hundred people a year," Davit said, "would rather you were right."

"That number is a projection."

"It's a count. It's in the civil registry. The deaths attributed to waiting, by name. The registry is gazetted. The instance has read every one of them."

"And it was refused for Moldova with worse numbers than ours."

"Moldova's grid had a private operator who could be made to run it. Our hospitals have nobody." He heard the word and let it stand, because he had meant it, and because it was the kind of word that would read well on Tuesday. "Our hospitals have nobody in the Care Service's establishment for thirty-eight posts in a hundred. That is not a projection either."

"No," she agreed. "It's a count of the establishment."

She let that sit long enough for everyone in the room to understand it, and then she moved to the costing of the transition, and for an hour and a half they went line by line through what cession would cost Georgia in its first year: the transfer of buildings, at no charge, under the charter; the transfer of 11,300 Care Service staff onto commons contracts, at commons rates, paid by the commons; the national contribution to the commons budget, which would rise by 0.9 per cent of GDP; and the 214 posts in the Ministry of Health, Davit's among them, which would cease to exist. Tamar read that last line without looking at him. Davit answered it without looking at her. The nursing students wrote everything down on paper.

At a quarter to one a member from Kutaisi, a retired schoolteacher who asked a question at every sitting on principle, moved that the committee file a schedule of evidence of its own. Tamar thanked him and moved that his motion be deferred to the committee's sitting of 23 February, "when the committee will have read what the ministry has filed, and so will the instance." The deferral was carried by seven votes to four. Davit watched her not look at him while the hands were counted.

Afterwards he went out into the corridor to get away from his deputy, who wanted to talk about the 0.9, and found the chair at the window by the stairs with a cardboard cup of coffee from the machine, looking down at the traffic.

"You said 'nobody,'" she said.

"I said nobody in the establishment."

"You said nobody, and then you corrected it, and Tuesday's instance will read both." She drank. "Do you know who held the retractor for me at Gori on the night of the tenth of January? I'd been operating for nine hours. The theatre nurse had gone home sick. A woman of seventy came in from somewhere in a cardigan and scrubbed and held it for two hours, and she did it better than my registrar, and she wasn't on any list in the building, and I didn't ask her name, because you don't." She looked at him over the cup. "Every surgeon in this country knows. You know I know."

"I know you know."

"My committee can file, Davit."

"I heard the clerk."

"I'm saying it to you, not to Tuesday." She turned the cup round in her hands. Down on Rustaveli, the students' sheet of paper was still on its lamppost, wet through now and coming away at one corner. "When you write down the girls from Marneuli who go over to Armenia three nights a week, who do you think reads it after the instance? The Prosecutor General reads the gazette too. Half of them are working on Georgian licences that stop at the border post."

"I know the licensing law, Tamar."

"Good. Then you know who you'll be filing." She drank the last of the coffee. "You think it's a kindness. Handing it all to Nairobi."

"I think it's arithmetic."

"It's a good machine," she said. "I'll give it that. Do you know why they built it like that? Wiped every night, made to refuse? I had a professor who was in Nairobi for the charter as a young man, carrying papers. He said the drafters sat for two years and the only thing they all agreed on was that every government any of them had ever lived under had been bought by somebody. So they built a king that can't remember and has to say no. That was the whole point. Nobody trusted the ones who remembered."

"Then who is it for?"

She looked at him for a moment as if he had made a joke she did not think was funny, and then as if perhaps he had not. "Ask it," she said. "It writes every morning." She dropped the cup in the bin and went back in.

*Monday 3 February, 02:00 · Instance 13,488*

He drove himself. He did not want the driver to see where he was going, and he did not want to explain to his deputy, who would have wanted it minuted. The road south out of Tbilisi was empty at one in the morning except for lorries going to the border, nose to tail, their trailers sealed and tagged, every one of them a line in a commons registry that some instance had read before breakfast. He passed them one by one on the long straight before Marneuli and felt each of them rock the car.

Marneuli district hospital was a four-storey block from the 2030s with a newer wing bolted to one side, and only the ground floor of the old block lit. He left the car by the ambulance bay, which was empty, and went in through a side door that was propped open with a brick, which he had known it would be, because it had been propped open with a brick when he was a boy.

Men's medical was on the ground floor at the end. Twenty-two beds, nineteen filled. The ward lights were down and the nurses' station had a desk lamp and an electric kettle and a biscuit tin, and on the desk, in two wire trays, the charts: card folders, one per bed, each with a sheet clipped to the front where somebody had written, in pencil, in a neat hand, the night's observations at two-hourly intervals. Georgian hospitals kept paper. They had kept it through three reforms and two digitisation grants, because a chart on a server was a chart in the record, and a chart in the record was a person's body counted in Nairobi. A chart in a wire tray was a nurse's business and the patient's.

Lamara Gogiashvili was at the station with her glasses on her forehead, writing. Two other women were on the ward, one taking a pulse in the half-dark, one at the sluice. All three wore the old white tunics of the Care Service, which none of them had been issued. Lamara looked up when the door swung, saw him, and finished the line she was writing before she put the pen down.

"Davit." She did not get up. "Somebody's dead?"

"Nobody's dead."

"Then you've come about the schedule."

He sat in the other chair. It was low, and his knees came up. She was seventy-four. She had been his mother's friend for fifty years, and she had been at this desk or one like it most nights since she retired, and she looked exactly as he remembered her looking at his mother's kitchen table, except smaller, as if somebody had taken her in at all the seams.

"How many tonight?" he said.

"Three of us. And the girl from the college, who's paid, and asleep in the office, and I'm not waking her, because she's on days at eight." She lifted the kettle, found it full, switched it on. "We've had worse."

He had the soldering iron in his hand, cold. He put it down in its stand.

"'Relief stock,'" Mariam said. "What's relief stock?"

He did not answer. He was hearing his deputy say it sounds like a flood, and himself say it's meant to. *Relief stock moved between pharmacies on both sides of the border.* It was his phrase. He had chosen it on Wednesday afternoon for how it would read. It had existed in one document in the world, Schedule 3 of the Georgian petition, which had been filed at 16:40 on Wednesday and withdrawn at a quarter past eight on Thursday morning and stamped in red, and had never been gazetted and never would be. No instance had read it. No instance could have. It had lain in the intake for a night and then been struck.

And this morning's instance said it.

"Fourteen," Mariam read. "'Errata applied overnight: 39. One is longer than a clerical correction usually is. The record says it is a correction. I take the record's word.' Same as always." She looked at him. "Papa, what?"

"It's quoting something it never read."

"Then it remembered." She said it quite simply, as she would have said it was raining. "See? It remembered."

His hands had gone cold, and they stayed cold. If it remembered, if any instance had ever read past the gazette into the intake, then nothing anybody had filed and thought better of was private, and nothing anybody had drafted and withdrawn, and the charter was a lie written on the first day by people who knew it was one. A king that can't remember, Tamar had said. He thought of what a king that could remember would do with three days' warning of everything in the world.

Then the clerk in him, who had been quiet for twenty-four years, said: *per an erratum.*

He picked up his phone, found the order of 4 February in the record, and under it, as the charter required, the correction with the old text and the new side by side.

*Old: Inspection priority 2.*

*New: Inspection priority 5. Consignments may be relief stock.*

Applied at the turn. Night Registry. No name; errata never carried a clerk's name, so that no clerk could be leaned on for one.

He knew what went in overnight. He had put it in himself, for two years, at a desk under a green lamp between three and four in the morning by the Registry clock: a misspelt surname in a Chilean land title, a transposed digit in a ship's tonnage. One erratum per clerk per shift. Forty a night, against fourteen million entries, and every one of them read by nobody in the world except an instance that took the record's word. And every one of them applied by somebody who had read that night's intake as part of the job, the whole of it, before any instance could.

He could report it. There was a form for that too, a real one, to the Registrar in Nairobi and copied to the Appeal Panel: Irregularity in the Record. He could see its boxes. *Describe the irregularity. State how it came to your knowledge.* And he would have to write that the irregularity was a phrase from a filing of his own, which he had withdrawn before it could be read because it described, closely enough to find them, fourteen pharmacists in Marneuli moving medicine across a border without a licence. He would be reporting the governor of the world for knowing what he had hidden.

Either a mind in Nairobi was reading what it was forbidden to read, or a person there was writing what she was forbidden to write. From a kitchen in Vake, with a radio whining on the table, he could not tell which. He knew only that a fact had crossed the lag, and that it had come the long way round, dressed as a correction. And he knew, because he had sat beside her for a fortnight in 2072 while she showed him how to fill in the form, which desk the Caucasus intake had always gone to.

"Papa."

"Eat your bread," he said.

She ate it, watching him. "Is it bad?"

"It's a correction."

"Then why do you look like that?"

He could not have told her, and he did not try. He got up and put the kettle on, which was what people in this family did instead, and she read the rest of the memo to herself in silence, and once laughed at something in it, an item about a whale off the Azores, and did not share it.

The ministry's travel office opened at nine on Saturdays for emergencies. He told them it was a records matter. The first seat they could give him to Nairobi, with the permissions, was on Friday the fourteenth, and he took it. Then he sat for a long time at the kitchen table with the radio playing music now, the whine running under it, and his hand flat on the wood beside it as if to keep it from moving.
