# Outline: The Morning Record (working title)

- **Target length:** about 34,200 words in five parts. The brief's range is
  30,000 to 40,000.
- **Shape:** linear, over 30 January to 2 March 2098, with the governor's
  memos interleaved as documents. The form enacts the novum (Chiang): the
  reader sees each morning exactly what the instance sees, on the same
  page, so the reader's knowledge of the state is the state's knowledge of
  the nation. Each part opens on a memo excerpt, except Part 1, which opens
  on a human body at a desk. One scene in Part 5 is told entirely as a
  memo.
- **Opening question:** why would a minister be afraid to type an old
  woman's name into a form, and what will read it?
- **Final image:** the kitchen at 06:10 on 2 March. Davit opens a blank
  document under the radio he's been mending and types the first clause of
  an amnesty bill for the 400. Mariam's question about her grandmother is
  still unanswered in the room.
- **What the ending costs the protagonist:**
  - **Cession, the thing he set out to win,** is refused.
  - **His mother is written into the record,** which she refused all her
    life.
  - **Lamara's friendship** is gone.
  - **Eka and 399 others are prosecuted,** because of names he filed.
  - **He can't know** whether the one mercy came from his drafting or from
    an unlawful hand.

## Calendar (fair play)

Every input to the ending's arithmetic is planted before Part 5.

- **The gazette lag** is planted in 1.1 ("Nothing would, for three days")
  and stated in 1.3: filings are gazetted at the third turn, so Monday's
  filing is Thursday's reading.
- **The last useful day** is stated in 1.3 ("Anything we file after the
  twenty-sixth, the deciding instance never sees") and restated in 4.1.
  Tamar's 24 Feb filing is first read on 27 Feb, in memo 13,513, in 5.1.
- **Instance numbers:**
  - 30 Jan is 13,485, the evening of scene 1.1.
  - The memo on 31 Jan is 13,486.
  - 8 Feb is 13,494.
  - 1 Mar is 13,515, and 2 Mar is 13,516.

  Each memo header shows its number, so a reader can count.
- **Carriage means goods, not persons.** This is planted in 2.2 (the
  commons scanners read the freight and not the passengers), stated by
  Rauf's joke in 4.4, and paid off in 5.2.
- **The Sadakhlo erratum** is planted in 2.5 and explained in 3.4 as a
  standing order that later memos inherit. It is shown standing in 4.1's
  memo, and Akinyi names it again in her call in 5.3.
- **The late addendum:** filed 28 Feb, so it would be gazetted on 3 Mar.
  Only an erratum could carry it to 13,515. That is planted in 3.5's
  question and 4.4's held-back clause, and refused in 5.3.
- **The licensing rule:** a carrier's schedule names everyone who carries
  goods across. That is stated in 4.3, and it makes the runners' names, and
  so Eka's, necessary.
- **Unverifiable counts carry no weight.** Stated in 2.3's memo, which is
  why every schedule names people.

## Rules this outline watches

- **"An epiphany that resolves the emotional arc in a single paragraph":**
  the understanding (4.2) and the decision (4.5) are three scenes apart,
  and the resentment survives.
- **"The mysterious final image":** the ending is concrete (a clause in a
  bill). The seam's ambiguity rests on two specific readings that the page
  states in 5.3.
- **"The last line echoing the first":** typing a name at the start,
  typing a clause at the end. It is a rhyme, not a repeated sentence.
- **"The AI that learns to feel":** the memos never warm. Any apparent
  continuity across days is Akinyi's line, or the reader's projection.
