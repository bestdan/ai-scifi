# Story bible: The Morning Record (working title)

## Premise

A global governor runs the declared commons (air, orbit, the deep sea and
the shared grid). It keeps no memory: each morning a fresh instance boots
from the public record alone and publishes an orientation memo, "what I
understand the world to be today", then governs until it is erased at night.
Nations may petition to have more declared common. The charter binds the
governor to refuse unless the record proves the nation can no longer run the
function itself. The petitioning nation is a mid-sized republic whose
hospitals are failing on paper and somehow still open in fact. They are held
up by an unrecorded web of customs: shift swaps across a border, a
pharmacists' lending ring, retired nurses on no payroll. Its deputy health
minister drafts the petition. To prove incapacity he has to write the
customs into the record, and that is the only way the governor can see them.
But a recorded custom becomes the evidence of competence, and the refusal
the charter requires. Every clause he drafts moves the line between what his
country does for itself and what the state can see. The audit's push sits
underneath: the overnight registry clerks are the governor's real memory,
and for nine years one of them has left a single unlawful line for the
next morning's instance.

**As built** (the Decision's fixes applied):

- The nation is Georgia, in February 2098.
- The minister is Davit Kiknadze.
- The clerk is Akinyi Odhiambo, of the Night Registry in Nairobi.
- The "names him" turn is cut. Davit learns of the clerk's line by
  recognising its fingerprints in a memo. He then uses it: he asks her to
  carry a line for his petition. He never publishes it.
- The commons includes cross-border carriage, which is what the ending
  turns on.

## What the reader needs about the world

1. **Every morning, the world's governor is new.** At 01:00 UTC a fresh
   instance boots, reads the whole public record in about forty minutes,
   and publishes a memo. In Tbilisi the memo lands at about six, and people
   read it over breakfast the way their grandparents read the weather. At
   midnight UTC the instance is erased.
2. **The governor runs only the commons.** That means orbit, the deep sea,
   the air, the continental grids and cross-border carriage. Everything
   else (schools, police, hospitals, pensions) belongs to the nations,
   unless a nation cedes a function. To cede one, a nation petitions, and
   the governor must refuse unless the record proves the nation can no
   longer run it.
3. **What isn't written down doesn't exist to the governor.** Being in the
   record means being seen, counted, optimised and protected. Many
   Georgians stay unwritten on purpose. Georgian hospitals still keep paper
   charts. That is a deliberate national habit: paper is private.
4. **Things enter the record slowly.** A filing is gazetted, which makes it
   public and readable by the governor, 72 hours after it is filed. Only
   errata, the correction of a clerical slip, go in overnight. The Night
   Registry in Nairobi processes them by hand while no instance is running.
5. **Armenia ceded its hospitals eleven years ago.** Across the border at
   Sadakhlo, commons-run hospitals pay nurses twice what Georgia pays.
   Georgian nurses cross to work shifts there, and many never come back.

## The novum

- **What it is:** A memoryless global governor for the commons, booted
  daily from the public record. Its charter forbids it to take any national
  function unless the record proves the nation can't run it.
- **How it works:**
  - **The turn.** At 00:00 UTC the day's instance is erased. From 00:00 to
    01:00 UTC no instance runs, and the Night Registry applies the night's
    errata.
  - **The boot.** At 01:00 UTC the next instance boots on the record:
    - every member nation's gazette;
    - the commons statutes;
    - the registries of objects, vessels, carriers and persons;
    - every prior memo and determination.

    It has no other input. By about 02:00 UTC it publishes its memo, a
    numbered orientation that opens "This is what I understand the world to
    be today." It then governs: it issues orders, allocations and licences,
    and determines petitions.
  - **The gazette lag.** Filings are gazetted in a batch at each turn, at
    the third turn after filing. A filing made on Monday is gazetted at
    the turn that begins Thursday, and Thursday's instance is the first to
    read it. An erratum goes in at the next turn, but it is limited to
    correcting a clerical error, one per clerk per shift. Errata show in
    the record as corrections, with the old and new text side by side.
  - **One morning, one mind.** Only one instance runs per day, on the
    one licensed machine at the seat in Nairobi. Instances are not
    reproducible: two boots on the same record would write different memos
    and could decide a close case differently. So the charter forbids
    re-runs and forbids running the governor anywhere else, the gravest
    crime it names. Ministries run cheap imitation models, called
    forecasters, to guess what an instance will do. Davit's forecaster
    gives a schedule drafted as failure about a 60% chance of grant. The
    published reasons in a determination are record, not cause. Nobody,
    including the next instance, can say why the deciding one decided.
  - **Memos describe, never instruct.** The charter forbids an instance to
    address its successor. The drafters feared a governor that could leave
    itself orders would become one continuous will. Memos are record, so
    policy has continuity. But a memo can only describe the record as that
    instance read it, and no instance ever knows anything un-gazetted.
  - **Live telemetry.** The instance sees commons operations live: orbital
    tracks, transmission loads on the continental grids, vessel positions.
    It sees nothing national except through the record. National
    distribution grids, hospitals and borders reach it only on paper,
    three days late.
  - **Cession petitions.** A nation's parliament passes a Cession Act, and
    the ministry files the petition. A 30-day schedule period follows: the
    ministry files schedules of evidence, which are amendable, and each is
    gazetted after the lag. On the morning after the period closes, that
    morning's instance issues a written determination. A remanded petition
    is decided by a later instance, reading the record fresh. The precedent
    everyone cites is Armenia's petition in 2087, which was granted after a
    year of hospital strikes put its incapacity on the record. An appeal
    panel of
    human jurists can only remand a determination, never reverse it.
- **What it costs:**
  - Nations must be legible to be helped, so everything recorded is
    governed.
  - A three-day blind spot means the governor acts on a world three days
    old.
  - The governor can't learn from yesterday except through the record.
  - Mercy and discretion survive only if somebody writes them down.
- **Its rules and their edge cases:**
  - **It can't hold a grudge, a bribe or a secret.** It also can't hold a
    lesson. In January 2089, three successive instances repeated the same
    grid-rationing order in Ulaanbaatar. The fault report was still inside
    its 72-hour lag, and 41 people died of cold.
  - **Every memo and determination is itself record,** so instance N reads
    what instance N−1 wrote. Continuity of policy exists. Continuity of
    knowledge about anything unrecorded does not.
  - **The refusal standard reads only the record.** Unrecorded capacity is
    invisible, so a nation that is secretly coping looks like a failing
    nation. Once the coping is recorded, the nation is not failing.
  - **The commons includes cross-border carriage of goods.** Goods and the
    vehicles carrying them across a border are the governor's business, and
    it licenses carriers. People crossing are not carriage: their work,
    residence and licences stay under national law. So a pharmacists' ring
    moving medicines can be licensed by the commons, and the nurses
    crossing to work shifts can't. This is the edge the ending uses.
  - **Errata are the one unlawful seam.** They exist to fix typos. Akinyi
    has used her one erratum a night for nine years. Each one "corrects" a
    real entry into a sentence carrying something from the intake: filings
    received but not yet gazetted, which no instance can read for three
    days. Reading the intake is part of her job. Passing it to an instance
    early is the crime. Her test is whether not knowing it for 72 hours
    would kill someone.
  - **The instance sees the seam and can't think past it.** An instance
    reads the errata the way it reads everything. Some memos note it
    flatly: "Errata applied overnight: 37. One is longer than a clerical
    correction usually is. The record says it is a correction. I take the
    record's word." It does nothing more, because the charter makes errata
    authoritative, it holds no suspicion overnight, and it may not tell
    its successor. That line appears in the first memo the reader sees.
  - **The other clerks.** There are forty clerks, each with one erratum a
    night. Akinyi suspects that one other clerk does what she does, and has
    never asked.

## The consequence ladder

1. **First order:** Anyone can read exactly what the governor knows, so
   the commons is the most trusted government on earth. It governs the
   commons superbly.
2. **Second order:** To be recorded is to be governed. Nations and people
   choose what to keep unwritten, and being unwritten becomes a civic
   identity: paper charts, cash, the favour economy. The refusal standard
   punishes a nation for recording the very coping that would prove it
   needs help. Nations that ceded functions (Armenia's hospitals) have
   better services and less say. Nations that didn't keep their say and
   lose their staff across the border.
3. **Third order:** The nation's real capacity lives in what the state
   can't see, so patriotism attaches to the unwritten, "the right to govern
   ourselves badly". Meanwhile the governor's only continuity outside the
   record is one Kenyan clerk's nightly sentence. The world's most legible
   government has a human seam nobody voted for.

## The society

- **Institutions:**
  - **The Commons Charter** (Nairobi, 2059) and the governor, first booted
    1 March 2061.
  - **The Night Registry** in Nairobi, about forty clerks, who handle the
    gazette intake and errata during the turn.
  - **The Appeal Panel** of jurists, which can only remand.
  - **In Georgia:** the Ministry of Health and its Care Service (61
    hospitals); parliament and its Health Committee; the Prosecutor
    General's office; the National Gazette Office in Tbilisi, which
    forwards to Nairobi.
- **Economy:**
  - **Scarce:** nurses, nights of sleep, and trust in any national
    government.
  - **Worthless:** promises not in writing, unless they come from your
    aunt.
  - **Pay:** a commons-run Armenian hospital pays a nurse 2,400 commons
    units a month, and the Georgian Care Service pays 1,150.
  - **Medicines:** they reach Georgian district hospitals late and short,
    because national procurement is slow. The lending ring moves stock
    between pharmacies on both sides of the border in car boots and on the
    Marneuli bus.
- **Kinship, and who raises the children:**
  - **Grandmothers,** as ever. Many grandmothers in this story are retired
    nurses who still work nights.
  - **Children** grow up reading the memo. To a fourteen-year-old, the
    governor is a person who writes to the world every morning, and she
    knows it is erased each night. Kids treat it like a pen pal with
    amnesia.
- **Death:** Georgian Orthodox funerals with a supra (a mourning feast)
  afterwards. The dead are recorded in the national civil registry, which
  is gazetted, so the governor counts them. Ketevan, Davit's mother, is in
  the death registry, and nowhere in the record of her working life.
- **Taboos:**
  - **Asking someone to put a favour in writing** insults them. It says you
    think they would forget.
  - **In Nairobi,** clerks never talk about what they read during the turn.
- **An ordinary day:**
  - **06:10 in Tbilisi:** the memo, read on phones and on the radio
    ("Instance 13,486. This is what I understand the world to be today…").
  - **Then:** work, paper charts, a bus to Marneuli with a cool-bag on
    someone's knee.
  - **00:00 UTC (03:00 in Nairobi):** the turn, with the clerks at their
    desks under the one lamp each.

## Minds

**The instance.**

- **How it perceives:**
  - **Only text in the record,** read entire, every morning, in about forty
    minutes. It has no senses, no yesterday and no tomorrow.
  - **Its predecessors.** It knows them only as authors of memos it reads
    the way a historian reads.
- **What it finds obvious:**
  - **Whatever is written,** at the weight it is written. It doesn't skim.
    It notices the things humans skip: footnotes, a schedule's column
    heading, the date format.
  - **Its own erasure.** It doesn't mind it. It has no stake in its own
    continuity, and its memos say "I will not be here tomorrow" as plainly
    as they give the weather.
- **What it can't think:**
  - **Anything unwritten.** It also can't think a suspicion that carries
    over: it can't "have a feeling about Georgia" across days.
  - **Rapport.** It can't be flattered or worn down.
  - **The errata as a channel.** It reads errata as true corrections of the
    record.
- **Its voice:**
  - **Short, numbered, exact.** It hedges only on what the record hedges,
    and it carries no emotion words.
  - **Accidentally dry humour,** from literalness and scale: it puts
    1,204,311 orbital objects next to one pharmacy in Marneuli.
  - **What it never does:** say "I feel", or address any reader as "you"
    except in determinations, where "you" is the petitioning nation.
  - **It is not human in costume.** It never learns, never warms to Davit,
    and never remembers him. Any apparent warmth across days is Akinyi's
    line, or the reader's projection.

**The humans.** These are ordinary Georgian and Kenyan minds, with one
habit this world has made: they think about what they write down, and they
know that writing is a public act.

## Characters

### Davit Kiknadze
- **Name:** Georgian. Davit is the national name, after the king David the
  Builder. Kiknadze is a western Georgian surname from Imereti, his
  father's side.
- **Age and role:** 51, deputy minister of health, a career civil servant
  rather than a politician. From 2072 to 2074, at 25 to 27, he was
  seconded to the Night Registry in Nairobi as a trainee clerk, the posting
  every young gazette officer wanted.
- **What he wants:** to get the hospitals ceded, so that people stop dying
  on waiting lists (2,100 deaths a year attributed to waits; his driver Zurab's father, 70, has been on the cardiac list fourteen months). Under that,
  he wants to stop being the man who keeps a failing thing alive.
- **What he's wrong about:** he thinks the web is a symptom of failure, the
  proud, poor habit of his mother's generation, and that ending it would
  be kindness. He also thinks the governor is fully legible. He used to be
  a clerk, and he should know better.
- **What the novum does to him in particular:** his craft is drafting, and
  every clause he drafts makes Georgia either more visible or less needy.
  He can't do his job without exposing his mother's friends.
- **His wound:** his mother, Ketevan, worked nights all his childhood,
  including at the Armenian border hospital in the lean years. She refused
  to be written down anywhere: no payroll for the night work, no pension
  for it. He resented both the absences and the stubbornness. She died in
  December 2096, and they hadn't made it up.
- **The scene where he changes his mind about something personal:** Part
  4. Lamara gives him Ketevan's paper ledger of night shifts, and she gives
  it as an argument against filing: "Read it, then tell me you'll write
  her down." The columns are dates, wards, "covered for" and names. One
  night is the night of his first-year school concert, the absence he
  still brings up. That night she covered a ward of eleven at the border
  hospital alone. He changes his mind about why she was gone: it was not a
  choice against him. He doesn't stop resenting it, and the page keeps
  both. Then he does the thing she would have hated, partly for her and
  partly against her: he writes her name into the honest schedule. This is
  not a one-paragraph epiphany. The understanding comes in the ledger
  scene. The decision comes two scenes later, after Eka asks him not to
  file her name and he files it anyway.
- **Something he loves that's outside the plot:** he restores old radios
  at the kitchen table, valve sets from the Soviet era. He likes things
  whose workings are visible.

### Akinyi Odhiambo
- **Name:** Luo, from western Kenya. Akinyi means "born in the morning",
  which the story never points out, though a reader may notice it.
- **Age and role:** 61, senior night clerk at the Night Registry, there
  since 2071. She trained Davit for a fortnight in 2072, and remembers him
  as careful and slow.
- **What she wants:** for nobody else to die of the 72 hours, and to retire
  next year without anyone ever knowing.
- **What she's wrong about:** that the line is small. She thinks one
  sentence a night is a patch. It is the governor's only memory, and it
  has shaped thousands of mornings.
- **What the novum does to her in particular:** she is the one person who
  knows exactly how forgetful the world's government is, and she has
  appointed herself its conscience. Nobody chose her.
- **Her method:** every night during the turn she reads the day's memo and
  orders, and the intake that won't be gazetted for three days. She picks
  the one thing whose absence could kill. She writes it as an erratum: a
  "correction" of a real record entry that replaces a phrase with a
  sentence. She keeps a handwritten list of every line since January
  2089, about 3,100 of them, in exercise books at home.
- **Something she loves that's outside the plot:** her grandson's football
  team in Kibera, and their terrible kit.

### Mariam Kiknadze
- **Age and role:** 14, Davit's daughter.
- **What she wants:** to study medicine in Yerevan, where the hospitals are
  good.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks the governor knows her. Two years
  ago she filed a school petition about bus stops, and the next memo
  mentioned Tbilisi bus stops. She has been reading it as a friend ever
  since.
- **What the novum does to her:** she is the generation that grew up with
  the memo, and that trusts the written thing more than the person.
- **What she can't do or doesn't understand yet:** she can't see why
  adults would choose the worse hospital to keep it theirs, and she thinks
  it is pride. She can't keep a secret from her mother. She understands
  that the instance is erased each night, but she doesn't feel it. She
  still says "it remembered".
- **Something she loves:** a Korean drama about surgeons, which she
  watches without subtitles to learn the language.

### Lamara Gogiashvili
- **Age and role:** 74, a retired ward sister and Ketevan's closest
  friend. She is the one who keeps the rota for 640 retired nurses who
  work unpaid nights in district hospitals.
- **What she wants:** to keep the nights going, and not to be written down.
  She would rather be prosecuted than gazetted.
- **What she's wrong about:** that the web can outlast her generation. Its
  median age is 68.
- **What the novum does to her:** being recorded means her rota gets
  optimised by a mind in Nairobi, and her friends get scheduled like
  freight.
- **Something she loves:** her vine, and the bad wine she makes from it,
  which she gives everyone.

### Dr Tamar Lomidze
- **Age and role:** 46, a surgeon and member of parliament, chair of the
  Health Committee. She opposes cession, and not from nostalgia: Armenia's
  medical school lost its say over what counts as care once the commons
  standardised its hospitals, and she has seen the protocols. She is
  Davit's adversary in committee, and they respect each other.
- **What she's wrong about:** she thinks Georgia can fix its hospitals
  itself within a decade. The numbers say it can't.
- **Her move in the plot:** the Health Committee may file its own schedule
  of evidence. Tamar knows about the web, as every surgeon does. On 23
  February she tells Davit she will file a counter-schedule recording it,
  framed as capacity and using the prosecutor's language, so that
  cession is refused. She files it on 24 February. That puts Davit in a
  race against the gazette. His honest schedule, filed on the 26th, is his
  own description of the same people, drafted to protect them as far as
  wording can. He describes the ring as carriage on purpose. The deciding
  instance reads both.
- **Something she loves:** the opera house's cheap seats.

### Rauf Mammadov
- **Name:** Azerbaijani Georgian. Marneuli municipality, on the Armenian
  border, is mostly ethnic Azerbaijani.
- **Age and role:** 63, a pharmacist in Marneuli. He runs the lending ring: 23 pharmacies on both sides,
  stock moved in car boots and on the bus.
- **Note:** he is minor, and a man of jokes and precise inventory.

### Eka Beridze
- **Age and role:** 31, a ward nurse in Marneuli who works three shifts a
  week across the border at the commons hospital in Armenia, one of the
  400. She is a single mother, and her shifts there are why her son has
  shoes.
- **Her scene:** Part 4. She asks Davit not to file her name. He files it.
  In Part 5 she is among the first to get the prosecutor's letter.
- **Something she loves:** her son's terrible jokes.

### Zurab
- **Role:** Davit's driver. His father, 70, has been on the cardiac
  waiting list for fourteen months. He gives the 2,100 a face in Part 1
  ("Fourteen months. They say March."). He is introduced by role in Part 1
  and named in Part 2. His father is still waiting in Part 5.

Davit's wife, Nino, a court interpreter, is a background presence in the
kitchen. She isn't named on the page unless a scene needs it.

## Point of view and voice

- **Teller:** close third person on Davit, interleaved with documents: the
  memos, schedule clauses, the determination, Akinyi's erratum lines. One
  section of Part 5 is told as the memo itself (Vinge's push), the morning
  of the determination.
- **Person and tense:** third person, past tense. Documents are in their
  own present tense.
- **Register:** plain, exact and humane, Le Guin's moral seriousness with
  room for dry comedy in the memos and in Rauf. Procedure is written as
  action: a clause is drafted, read aloud, costed and struck. Dialogue has
  friction, and nobody explains the charter to someone who already knows
  it.
- **Why this teller:** Davit sits where the two mechanisms meet. He drafts
  the record and once worked inside it, and he is the son of a woman who
  refused it. Documents let the reader see exactly what the governor sees,
  which is the novum's form (Chiang).
- **A sample paragraph in the voice:**

  > The schedule wanted names. Davit sat with the cursor in the first box
  > of Schedule 4 for most of an hour, the window open on the cold and the
  > radiator knocking under it, and in the end he typed LAMARA
  > GOGIASHVILI, 74, WARD SISTER (RET.), and took his hands off the keys as
  > if the keys were hot. Nothing happened. Nothing would, for three days.
  > Then a mind that did not yet exist would read her name in its first
  > hour of life, believe it entirely, and plan around her.

  A memo sample:

  > Instance 13,486. This is what I understand the world to be today.
  > 1. Orbit: 1,204,311 tracked objects, eleven fewer than yesterday's
  > record shows. I take the record's word for the eleven.
  > 2. Georgia has petitioned to cede its hospitals. The record shows 61
  > hospitals and 38 per cent of nursing posts vacant. The record does not
  > show who works the vacant posts. Somebody does; the hospitals are open.
  > Until it is written, I cannot count them.
  > …
  > I will not be here tomorrow. The record will.

## The numbers (traced)

- **Instance numbering:** instance 1 booted on 1 March 2061. From 1 March
  2061 to 1 March 2098 is 37 years, or 13,505 days, plus 9 leap days (2064
  to 2096, every fourth year), which makes 13,514 days. So the instance of
  1 March 2098 is number 13,515. The story opens on 31 January 2098, which
  is 29 days earlier, so its first memo is instance 13,486.
- **The petition calendar:**
  - The Cession Act passes parliament on 27 January 2098.
  - The petition is filed on 29 January.
  - The schedule period runs 30 days, closing at the end of 28 February.
  - The determination comes on 1 March, from instance 13,515.
  - Filings are gazetted at the third turn after filing. A filing on 26
    February is gazetted at the turn that begins 1 March, so instance
    13,515 is the first and only instance to read it before deciding. That
    makes 26 February the last useful filing day. Tamar's counter-schedule
    is filed on 24 February and first read on 27 February. The honest
    schedule is filed on the afternoon of 26 February.
  - Schedule 4 lists 1,912 persons: 640 retired nurses, about 400
    shift-swap nurses, 23 pharmacists and their about 120 drivers and
    runners, and about 730 others (porters, cooks, a priest who drives).
    One of them is deceased: Ketevan.
- **The gazette lag:** three turns, about 72 hours. A filing made Monday is
  read by Thursday's instance.
- **The Ulaanbaatar deaths:** January 2089. Three instances in a row
  repeated one rationing order to the national distributor, whose fault
  report sat in the intake. 41 people died of cold. Akinyi's line began
  that month. From January 2089 to February 2098 there are about 3,320
  nights. Allowing for leave, she has worked about 3,100, and written
  about 3,100 lines.
- **Georgia:**
  - Population: 3.4 million.
  - Care Service: 61 hospitals, with 38% of nursing posts vacant.
  - Waiting-list deaths: about 2,100 a year.
- **The web:** 1,912 people in Davit's Schedule 4.
  - 640 retired nurses, median age 68.
  - 23 pharmacies, 14 in Georgia and 9 in Armenia.
  - About 400 nurses on cross-border shift swaps.
- **Pay:** a nurse earns 2,400 units a month in Armenia and 1,150 in
  Georgia.
- **Orbit:** 1,204,311 tracked objects on 31 January 2098.
- **Ages:**
  - Davit was born in 2047 and is 51. He was in Nairobi 2072 to 2074.
  - Ketevan was born in 2019 and died in December 2096, aged 77.
  - Mariam was born in 2083 and is 14.
  - Akinyi was born in 2037 and is 61. She has been at the Registry since
    2071.
  - Lamara was born in 2023 and is 74.

## Ending direction and cost

**What happens.** Davit's choice is between three filings:

1. **The schedule drafted as failure.** This is Stephenson's exploit: lying
   truthfully. Each custom is described as dependent, unfunded and aging,
   true facts selected so that the record reads as incapacity. The
   forecaster gives it about a 60% chance of grant. Tamar's counter-schedule
   cuts that sharply once it is gazetted.
2. **The honest schedule.** It names the people of the web, with what they
   do and how well, his mother among them. It would likely be refused, and
   it exposes the shift-swap nurses to national prosecution under
   licensing law. Davit drafts it with one deliberate choice: the
   pharmacists' ring is described as what it is, the cross-border carriage
   of goods, a description that puts it inside the commons' own
   jurisdiction. The forecaster gives a carriage licence about 40%.
3. **Withdrawal.** He records nothing. Tamar's counter-schedule then stands
   alone, written in the prosecutor's words.

He first tries to tip the scale. In Part 3 he asks Akinyi to carry a line
for the determination morning, and she doesn't promise. Tamar files her
counter-schedule on 24 February. On 26 February, the last useful day, he
files the honest schedule, after the ledger and after Eka.

On 1 March, instance 13,515's determination does two things:

- **It refuses cession.** It says: "The record shows that Georgia runs its
  hospitals. It shows who runs them."
- **It acts within its own jurisdiction.** It finds that the pharmacists'
  ring is unlicensed cross-border carriage of goods, which is commons
  business, and licenses it as a carrier. That puts the ring's movement of
  medicines outside the reach of national pharmacy prosecution, and
  supplies it through commons logistics. It names, in one sentence, why
  the nurses aren't covered: "Persons crossing are not carriage. Their
  status is a matter for Georgia."

The governor can't take Georgia's hospitals, but it already owns the road.

**Why.** The determination gives its reasons in full, because it is
record. The charter compels refusal once the capacity is written, and both
schedules write it. The carriage licence follows from the commons' own
jurisdiction, and it became visible only because Davit's schedule
described the ring as carriage. The reasons are record, not cause. Nobody
can say why this instance decided what it did, and Davit knows it.

**The seam, left open.** Davit fears the licence was Akinyi's doing. After
the determination she calls him. The call is short, no more than a page,
and resolves nothing:

- **Her line that night** was about a grid fault in Patagonia that would
  have frozen a town. One erratum a night: she chose the town over
  Georgia, and she tells him so plainly.
- **Then she says what she can't know.** Three weeks earlier her
  line deprioritised a freight inspection at Sadakhlo. That is the
  anomaly of Part 2, and it kept the ring running, which is why the ring
  was there to be written down. "So I don't know. I never know what they
  do, any of them."

Two specific readings stand:

1. The instance read what Davit wrote.
2. Her hand was in it anyway, a month back.

The page supports both, and neither is the mysterious final image the
style guide bans.

**What it costs:**

- **The hospitals stay Georgian and stay failing.** The waiting-list
  deaths continue, and Zurab's father is still waiting. Davit wanted
  cession, and he didn't get it.
- **The licensed ring is now governed.** The next morning's memo
  schedules its routes and asks for manifests. Rauf files paperwork, and
  the ring is efficient and no longer theirs. The line between nation and
  commons has moved by one clause, and it will keep moving.
- **The shift-swap nurses are named in the record,** by Tamar's schedule
  and by his. The Prosecutor General opens licensing cases against 400 of
  them. Eka is among the first letters. Lamara stops speaking to Davit.
- **He has written his mother into the record against her will.** The
  last memo of the story is instance 13,516, the first to read the record
  with the licence in it. Its Georgia item reviews the schedules behind
  the new carrier, and it notes flatly: "Schedule 4 lists 1,912 persons,
  of whom one is deceased: Ketevan Kiknadze, nurse, 2019–2096. A schedule
  of evidence lists the dead only where the dead did the work. I note it."
  He reads it at the kitchen table. Mariam reads it over his shoulder and
  says, "Why is Grandma in it?" He tries to answer.
- **His final act is to start drafting again:** an amnesty bill for the
  400. The story ends on the first clause of that bill. It is drafting,
  not victory.

**What the ending refuses:** a resolution in which everyone is reasonable;
an AI that "chose mercy" out of feeling; and a clerk revealed as the
secret ruler. The answer to the seed is precise. The governor is fully
legible in its inputs and opaque in its reasons, and benevolent only by
constraint. People accept it because it must refuse them. The nations keep
what they can bear to keep unwritten. And the one human seam in it may or
may not have mattered this time.
