# Comprehension answers (from memory, story text only)

1. A Georgian deputy minister, Davit, has to decide whether to write down the unpaid night nurses who keep the country's hospitals running, knowing a new machine mind in Nairobi reads each day's record and will refuse to take over the hospitals if it sees they cope. He names them, and a clerk in Nairobi secretly alters the record in small ways to protect them, so the hospitals stay Georgian and some of the people he named are punished.

2. He stands to lose the hospitals (and his own ministry's 214 posts if they are taken, so his job). He could also lose the people he names, who risk prosecution, and the trust of Lamara and the nurses. If he fails to make the case for taking over, about 2,100 people a year keep dying on waiting lists, including Zurab's father with his heart. If he succeeds in showing Georgia copes, the hospitals stay Georgian and nothing improves for those on the lists.

3. The founders built a ruler that forgets everything each day, so it can't be bought or build a tomorrow of its own. One new mind each day, erased at four in the morning, reading only the gazetted record after a three-day delay. Nobody trusted rulers who remembered. They also made the human clerks read every filing, so a human head touches the record. I understood the aim, but I'm not sure why the clerks' power to correct things was left in.

4. That the machine doesn't read the intake; Akinyi, a clerk, writes one "erratum" a night. That recasts the memo line "relief stock" and the "longer than usual correction". Also, late, the mother's ledger: she wasn't silent against Davit but was keeping her nights for herself, and she was absent the night of his concert because she was alone with two bad patients. That changed how I saw his anger from the first pages.

5. The machine refused to take over the hospitals because the record now showed who runs them. It also licensed the pharmacists' ring as carrier 88,412 because it counted as cross-border carriage of goods, which is the commons' business. It said the nurses crossing the border are people, not carriage, and are Georgia's problem. So the pharmacy ring became legal, and the prosecutor sent letters to about 400 nurses, including Eka. Akinyi didn't carry Davit's last-minute addendum because nobody would die of it by Thursday; she used her form for a town in Patagonia. Davit put his mother's name into the schedule, and the radio read her name aloud. He didn't tell Mariam why. He started drafting an amnesty for the nurses.

6. Davit Kiknadze: deputy health minister, former Night Registry clerk, our protagonist. Mariam: his fourteen-year-old daughter who reads the memo aloud. His wife: a lawyer, barely appears. His mother, Ketevan: night nurse, dead, kept the ledger. Lamara Gogiashvili: retired ward sister, mother's friend, still works nights unpaid. His deputy: unnamed, thirty-four, ex-Gazette Office. Zurab: his driver, whose father waits for heart surgery. Rauf Mammadov: Marneuli pharmacist running the medicine lending. Eka Beridze: young nurse with a plait and an eight-year-old son. The woman of fifty: carries the bag, has a daughter in university. Tamar Lomidze: committee chair and surgeon, filed the committee's schedule. Akinyi Odhiambo: Nairobi clerk who has written corrections for nine years. The minister: former mayor of Kutaisi, takes the blame. Zurab's father: old man on the bakery wall. The Armenian ward sister: knew his mother. The Telavi director. The instances: the daily minds, numbered.

7. Whether the machine read Davit's schedule or Akinyi's old patch when it licensed the ring. Whether Akinyi's lines are right, given the Lagos vaccine was wasted and Patagonia is unknown. What happens to Eka and the 400. Whether the ages and dates of Davit and his mother line up. Why the Armenia shifts were "referred to administration" and what that does. Whether another clerk exists.

8. "Instance": the numbered daily mind; I got that from the memos and the erasure. "Gazette/gazetted": made public in the official record; got it from context. "Turn": the moment at 3am when the batch is sealed; from the Registry scene. "Erratum/errata": corrections; explained by the story. "Cession": handing hospitals to the commons; from Armenia example. "Supra": a Georgian feast with toasts; context only, guessed. "Schedule of evidence": the filing; clear. "Forecaster": the cheap imitation machine; explained. "Mandazi": fried bread, guessed from a woman selling it. "Tetri": small coin, guessed. "Glargine": insulin, from the context of pens.

9. Beyond the main characters, the change means that for ordinary people, being counted means being helped, charged and scheduled, and that being unwritten, as the old night nurses were, gave them privacy and freedom as well as hardship. People in Armenia got shorter lists and more pay but lost their own say. The commons gets routes and rotas for the pharmacists, who become bored and well. The 400 nurses and other unlisted helpers are exposed. For the world, it shows that one forgetful ruler is only as honest as the humans who feed it, and that quiet human corrections hold up much of the world, unseen.
