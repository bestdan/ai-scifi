# First Reader report: "Paper Is Private", draft 1

One note first. The file view showed me the drafter's notes at the end. I did not read them as part of the story, and nothing below depends on them.

## Where I got lost

1. **The first page, the paragraph about the mind that "did not yet exist"** ("Nothing happened. Nothing would, for three days"). I didn't understand why a name typed into a form would be read by a mind that isn't born yet. The next paragraphs about Nairobi and the erasure at four in the morning half explained it. I understood it properly only when the first memo came on the radio the next morning. I worked it out from the memo and from Mariam reading it aloud.

2. **The forecaster scenarios** (Davit and his deputy, the 31, 9, 60 and 57 per cent). I didn't know whether a high number was good or bad for them. I assumed high meant "the hospitals get taken", and that fit once the deputy said "That's the schedule" about the 60. But I was unsure for several exchanges. I also couldn't tell why naming the unpaid nurses *accurately* scored 9 while describing the same people as unsustainable scored 60. I took it as the point of the scene, but I didn't feel I'd got it.

3. **The dates.** The twenty-sixth, the twenty-eighth, "three turns", "read Thursday", "gazetted Monday". I lost track in the first meeting-room scene and again later (the addendum filed on the 28th, "read on 3 March"). I stopped trying to follow the arithmetic and trusted that there was a deadline. I couldn't tell whether the dates were consistent.

4. **The three identical memo extracts in Akinyi's sleeve** (Mongolia, repeated three times). At first I thought it was a pasting mistake. I worked it out a few lines later, from "he read the three of them across, and then down, and they were the same" and her explanation.

5. **Davit's mother's dates.** Akinyi says "a birth registered in Tbilisi in 2047, Kiknadze" and then "a death in 2096, Ketevan". I couldn't tell for a moment whether the 2047 birth was his mother's or his. I decided it was Davit's. Later his mother is "2019–2096" in the schedule. I wasn't sure of this and didn't go back.

6. **Zurab at Dubai.** "Zurab had met him off the night flight, and in the transit lounge at Dubai he had looked for the next lot." I couldn't make that sentence work. I didn't know whose lounge, or why the driver was there. I moved on.

7. **The names count.** 212 names, then 1,912 persons in the speech, then "one thousand nine hundred and eleven names long" on the screen. I noticed the mismatch and couldn't tell if it mattered. The memo later says 1,912.

8. **The Carriers' Code logic in Part Four** (why naming the runners makes the ring "carriage", why the nurses themselves are "not carriage", and the "services" addendum). I followed the shape: they make the pharmacies legal and expose the nurses. I didn't follow the legal steps. I understood the result only when the determination said it plainly.

9. **Who put the ring in front of the machine in time.** I worked out in Part Three that Akinyi wrote the line. But at the end I wasn't sure whether the determination read Davit's schedule or her old patch. Akinyi says the same in the call, so I decided that was meant. I mention it because I was confused for a bit before she said it.

## Where I got bored

- **Part One, the long meeting-room scene** (the deputy, the forecaster, the rewording of "unable" to a count of 70 per cent). I liked the idea that a sentence should be a count and not a claim. But it ran long, and I was waiting for a person to walk in. I started skimming at the second forecaster run.
- **The committee hearing**, in the costing stretch ("the transfer of buildings, at no charge… 0.9 per cent of GDP… 214 posts"). I was waiting for the corridor conversation with Tamar. That was good.
- **The description of how the Registry works** before the first night scene (the paragraphs on how a clerk reads a filing, then the errata form and its five boxes). I was waiting for Akinyi to say something. I later needed the five boxes, but I didn't feel that while reading.
- **The Telavi call and the costing note in Part Four.** The director reading out names was good. The costing note read like a memo and I wanted to get past it.
- **The long memo in Part Five** before the Saturday kitchen. I skimmed items 1 to 8 for the news, then read the determination closely.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- The student's sign on the lamppost: OUR HOSPITALS, OUR MISTAKES. "The best-drafted thing he had seen all week."
- The driver Zurab telling Davit about his father, then "He did not want anything from Davit, and that was worse than if he had."
- Lamara's wine in the two-litre water bottle with cling film under the cap. "It's last year's. It's terrible."
- The pharmacist and the border arch: "It logged the cognac."
- Mariam: "That's what remembering is." Later, "It says it doesn't say. That's different." I laughed and then felt something.
- The young nurse laughing at the cow-in-a-lift joke on the bus, and the boy who never finishes it ("I don't know how it ends"). This was the line that got me.
- The ledger and the sixteenth of May 2054: "Alone. Two post-op bad… Both kept. Home 08:40." Then the empty chair with the handbag on it at the concert. That was the best thing in the story for me. It made me angry at her and then not.
- "At the supra after her funeral… I drank to her nights."
- Akinyi: "That's what a good night looks like. Exactly like a wasted one." And her saying the form had five boxes and which one she used.
- The woman of fifty with the Prosecutor's letter: "It was my week, Davit. I'd do it again. Write that down too."
- The ending: the radio announcer reading Ketevan Kiknadze's name out loud in the kitchen, and Davit not telling Mariam. I was not sure he was right and wanted him to tell her. It stung.

## Who I cared about, and whether I'd have kept reading past page 2

I cared most about **Lamara**. She was typed and deleted on the first page, and I wanted to see her. Then **Eka**, the young nurse, and her unfinished joke. Then **Zurab and his father** with the March, then April, then May date. Then **Davit's mother**, who is dead and in a book. I cared about **Mariam** every time she opened her mouth.

I came to care about **Davit** only around the mother's ledger. Before that he was a careful, decent man doing sums, and I admired him more than I felt for him. I was interested in **Akinyi** but kept her at a distance. I believed her, and I wasn't sure I liked her.

Yes, I'd have kept reading past page 2. Page 2 is the radiator, the name typed and deleted, and the driver's father. By then I wanted to know what he'd do about Lamara. The place I'd have been tempted to put it down is the Part One meeting-room scene with the forecaster. I'd have gone on because of the sign and the driver. If the story had opened on that meeting, I'm not sure I would have.

## The question I'm left with

Was it right? Tamar's text asks "which of us was wrong", and I don't know either. I also keep wondering whether the machine read what Davit filed or what Akinyi wrote, and whether Akinyi would have believed it either way. And what happens to Eka, and what Davit will one day tell Mariam about her grandmother.
