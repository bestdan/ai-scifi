# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A Georgian civil servant, Davit, has to write down the unpaid old nurses who keep the country's hospitals running, in order to make a case to a world-governing machine. But the machine only takes over a country's hospitals if the country can't run them, so naming the nurses proves Georgia doesn't need help. He discovers a Nairobi clerk secretly slips lines into the machine's record, and in the end he files the names anyway, including his mother's. The machine refuses the takeover but licenses the pharmacists' medicine smuggling as legal carriage, and the nurses who cross the border get prosecutor's letters.

2. He wants Georgia's hospitals taken over so nurses get paid and waiting lists end (2,100 deaths a year, the driver's father waiting for a heart operation). He stands to lose his ministry post, and the people he names, Lamara and the unpaid women, lose their privacy and risk prosecution. He also stands to lose their trust and his family's. If he fails, the deaths go on and the names go to the prosecutor anyway. In the end the takeover is refused, the ring is licensed and the nurses are charged.

3. The charter's drafters distrusted every government, so they built a governor that is a new mind each day, erased at midnight, with a three-day lag before anything filed is read. That way it can't be bribed, hold grudges or keep secrets, and it must refuse to take what a nation can still do for itself, so it never swallows the nations. Georgia's habit of keeping things on paper and off the record came from its own distrust of being counted.

4. Late on, I learned that Akinyi, a Nairobi clerk, has been slipping one "erratum" a night into the record for nine years, because she reads the intake three days before any instance can. The "relief stock" line in the memo, which I took for the machine remembering, was hers. The story's idea that the machine can't remember was not quite the whole truth. Another late shift was his mother's ledger, which showed her unpaid nights were deliberate care, not an absence from his childhood.

5. The machine refuses the cession: the record shows 1,912 named people running Georgia's hospitals, so Georgia can still do it. It licenses Rauf's pharmacies and the runners as a carrier, so the ring becomes lawful and Nairobi will schedule it. It says persons crossing are not carriage. The prosecutor sends letters to 406 nurses, and the Armenian hospital sends the Georgian women home. Akinyi didn't carry his late addendum. Lamara won't open the door. His mother's name is read out on the radio and he tells Mariam only part of it. He begins drafting an amnesty clause. The result follows from his choice to name everyone so the carriage licence would be valid, and from Tamar's committee naming them first.

6. Davit Kiknadze: deputy health minister, former Night Registry clerk, narrator-centre. Mariam: his fourteen-year-old daughter who reads the memo aloud. His wife: a court lawyer, mostly in the background. His mother, Ketevan: an unpaid night nurse who died 2096, wrote the ledger. Lamara Gogiashvili: her friend, a retired ward sister, 74, still working nights. Zurab: the ministry driver whose father waits for a heart operation. His father: seventy, sits on the bakery wall. Davit's deputy: thirty-four, from the Gazette Office, built the schedules. Tamar Lomidze: surgeon and chair of the Health Committee, files the nurses by name. Rauf Mammadov: Marneuli pharmacist who runs the cross-border lending. Eka Beridze: young nurse with a plait who carries the bag and has a boy with a joke. The woman of fifty: another bag carrier, "It was my week." Akinyi Odhiambo: Nairobi Registry clerk, desk 12, does the errata. Her grandson plays football in brown. The ward sister at Bagratashen: Armenian, knew his mother.

7. Whether the machine ever noticed Akinyi's pattern or kept the corrections on purpose. Whether Akinyi's lines help or harm. Whether the nurses will be prosecuted or get amnesty. Why the machine granted Armenia and not Georgia, in a way that anyone can tell. The fate of the Patagonian town and of the Lagos children. What exactly "a long erratum" and "the turn" are, in full detail.

8. "Schedule of evidence": explained in the story's second paragraph. "Cession": I took it from context as handing over a function. "Erratum/errata": explained by Mariam and Akinyi. "The turn": explained by Davit and Akinyi. "Instance": from context. "Supra" (the funeral table): I guessed from the description. Khinkali, mandazi: guessed they are food. Valve radio: from context. "Priority 2/5": inferred. Tetri: guessed a small coin.

9. Beyond the main characters, the licensing means a shadow economy becomes legal and administered from Nairobi. It is safe from seizure but no longer theirs, and it turns informal favours into numbered manifests. The ordinary unpaid carers become "evidence" and are liable to prosecution, since the machine says persons are not its business. Countries that stay "able" stay on their own, with their unpaid workers unprotected, while countries that fail get paid staff and fixed dates. For everyone, the story suggests that being counted brings help and being unseen brings freedom, and you rarely get both.
