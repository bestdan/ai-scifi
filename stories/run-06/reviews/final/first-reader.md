# First Reader report: "Paper Is Private" (final.md)

One straight read, start to finish.

## Where I got lost

1. **Opening, the paragraph beginning "The schedule wanted names."** I had to hold a lot at once: a schedule of evidence, a petition, a mind in Nairobi that is new every morning, a three-day delay, an Act. I did not know what "schedule" meant until the definition sentence. I thought the definition arrived a little late. I worked it out two paragraphs on, from the paragraph about the file going down the wire to Nairobi. I was still unsure why a name was dangerous until the "To ask for help, Georgia had to write down the help it already had" paragraph. After that I was fine.
2. **The forecaster scene (10:30, Friday 31 January).** I followed the idea that facts about the future make Georgia look like it is failing. The jump from thirty-one to sixty per cent, from adding "no succession, no legal basis", felt like being told rather than shown. I never worked out how the machine could be fooled by that. I just accepted it.
3. **The forecaster numbers later on.** Sixty, fifty-seven, back to sixty, twenty-five, forty, then "single figures" and a zero. By the Tuesday night scene I was no longer tracking which number meant what. I trusted that the direction was bad or good and read on.
4. **Sadakhlo vs Bagratashen vs "the Debed crossing."** I took these to be one crossing with two names. I never checked.
5. **Part Three, the bit where he decides it's a person and not the machine** ("Either a mind in Nairobi was reading what it was forbidden to read, or a person there was writing"). I understood it on the spot, and I'd already guessed a clerk was doing it from the "errata" talk. But "he knew, because he had sat beside her for a fortnight in 2072... which desk" threw me for a second. I didn't know who "her" was until Akinyi appeared, and I worked it out then.
6. **Numbers of people near the end.** 640, 400, 406, 212, 1,482, 1,912, 23 pharmacists. I added 1,482 + 406 + 23 and got one short of 1,912. I assumed the last one was his mother, and the story later confirms it. I did not check the other counts against each other.
7. **Who knows what about the 640.** The ministry's counts, the deputy's 212, the committee's 640, and the ministry's list "naming the same 640". I lost track of whose list was whose, though I could still follow the story.
8. **The 2089 Mongolia memos (Part Three).** I was confused for a moment about why three identical memos mattered. I worked it out from her telling: each instance was right on its own record, and none could know. It then hit hard.
9. **The Part Five ending, the last page.** The draft clause about "no proceedings" and "too wide" was clear. I wasn't sure whether Lamara forgave him. I think I was meant not to be sure.

## Where I got bored

- **The committee hearing (1 February), the long speech about the Armenian cession** (stroke protocol, nurse pay doubling, "measurably worse"). I knew this was setting up the argument against handing over. It felt like a briefing paper being read to me. I got interested again at "I would rather be wrong by half a point and have it be ours to fix."
- **The middle of Part Four**, the deputy's phone calls with the hospital directors and the Tamar phone call about filing. I was waiting for Davit to decide something. The Telavi director reading his night women was good and kept me; the stretch around it was a run of reports.
- **Memos read a third or fourth time.** The first two were great. By the later ones I skimmed the numbered items and went looking for item 9 and item 5.
- **Rauf's long explanation of lending between pharmacies** was a bit of a lecture until the phone call in three languages and "Not in money."

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **Opening: typing LAMARA GOGIASHVILI, 74, WARD SISTER (RET.) and taking his hands off the keys "as if the keys were hot."** Then deleting it. That got me.
- **The memo's item 9, "The record does not show who works the vacant posts. Somebody does; the hospitals are open."** And Mariam: "Who does?" "Like people."
- **Mariam, "That's what remembering is."** I loved the argument about remembering versus reading. I loved her all the way through ("It says it doesn't say. That's different.").
- **The driver's father on the wall by the bakery, "They say March."** And later the cherry blossom line ("He's never looked at the cherry blossom in his life"). I laughed and it hurt.
- **Rauf and the cognac: "It logged the cognac."**
- **The cow in the lift joke the boy never finishes.** It came back at the end and I felt it each time.
- **The hypoglycaemic man at 2 a.m. and Lamara turning his head before anything else; the Armenian-label pen behind the milk. "Don't."**
- **"Relief stock" showing up in the memo after he'd withdrawn the filing.** That was a real jolt. I thought for a moment the machine did remember, and so did he.
- **Akinyi's desk, and the three printed memos with the white creases.** The ledger of 3,106 lines. "Mostly nothing happens, and nothing happening looks the same whether I did it or not." "That's what a good night looks like. Exactly like a wasted one."
- **Her not promising him anything, and him knowing the question was not a principle.**
- **His mother's ledger and the entry for the night of the school concert, the chair with the handbag on it.** Lamara raising her glass at the funeral supper "to her nights." This was the best part of the story for me.
- **The woman of fifty: "It was my week, Davit. I'd do it again. Write that down too."**
- **"Persons aren't my department."**
- **The radio announcer reading his mother's name out loud in the kitchen, and him not telling Mariam.** And the radio valves, "everything visible." The ending worked for me.

## Who I cared about, and would I have kept reading past page 2

I cared most about **Davit**, then **Lamara**, then **Mariam**. After those, **Zurab and his father**, **Eka with the plait and the boy who can't finish the joke**, and **Akinyi**. Rauf too. I cared about his mother, whom I only meet through other people.

Yes, I would have kept reading past page 2. The first two or three pages hooked me with the typed name and the delete, and I stayed for the people. If I'm honest, the part that made me sure I'd stay was the first memo scene with Mariam, because up to then the setup was heavy. I was less sure about the rules of the world than about the people, but I trusted the people.

## The question I'm left with

Did Akinyi's one line a night help or harm? She says herself she can't know. I'm also left with whether the nurses with letters from the Prosecutor General are going to be all right, Eka especially. And I want to know what happens to the coal town in Patagonia.
