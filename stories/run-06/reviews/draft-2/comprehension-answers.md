# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A Georgian deputy health minister, Davit, has to decide whether to write down the unpaid retired nurses, pharmacists and border-crossing nurses who secretly keep his country's hospitals running. Writing them down might get them paid or protected, but it also exposes them to prosecution, and the world governor, a mind that is erased every night and reads only the public record, reads whatever he files.

2. He wants Georgia to hand its failing hospitals to the world governor, because the long waiting lists kill about 2,100 people a year. That is his reason for wanting a "yes". Handing over would also end his own ministry post and the old unwritten system of favours his mother and Lamara lived in. To win, he must make Georgia look unable to cope, but the only evidence is people who show that it can cope. If he fails, the petition is refused, the deaths go on, and the people he names are exposed. If he doesn't name them, the pharmacists' ring is shut down at the border. Either way his mother, who refused all her life to be written down, gets written down by him.

3. The charter's drafters distrusted anyone who remembers, because every government they had known had been bought. So they built a governor that is erased each night, cannot hold a grudge, bribe or secret, and has to refuse any nation that can still do a function for itself. The three-day lag between filing and reading is part of the same design, so nobody can be bribed to change what it knows. The clerks in the Night Registry are the one gap in that design: they read the intake early.

4. Late, I learned that the governor does not remember or read the intake. A human clerk in Nairobi, Akinyi, has been writing one false "correction" a night for nine years, so that the next mind is told something the public record doesn't yet say. I had suspected a machine's secret, and it was a person's. I also learned late that her Sadakhlo line was carried by every new instance as an inherited order that each said did not follow, so it was not agreement. I also learned that his mother's ledger shows she did not simply abandon him: the night she missed his concert she was alone with two bad post-op patients.

5. The governor refused the cession, because the record now showed 1,912 named people running Georgia's hospitals. It licensed the pharmacists' cross-border ring as a carrier, because carriage of goods is the commons' business, and said that the nurses who cross are "not carriage" and are Georgia's matter. The Prosecutor General then sent letters to the 406 named nurses, and the Armenian side sent the Georgians home. Akinyi did not carry Davit's late addendum; she used her one line that night for a town in Patagonia instead. Davit had put his mother's name in his schedule and said it to his daughter on Sunday only as "she worked nights". The ending is Davit starting to draft a no-proceedings clause for the nurses.

6. Davit Kiknadze: deputy minister of health, 51, former Night Registry clerk, who mends a radio. Mariam: his 14-year-old daughter, who reads the morning memo as a letter from a friend. His wife: a lawyer ("the Marneuli case"), mostly in the background. Ketevan: his dead mother, a nurse who worked unpaid nights and kept a ledger. Lamara Gogiashvili: 74, retired ward sister and his mother's friend, who runs the night ward at Marneuli and refuses to be written down. His deputy: 34, a former Gazette Office officer who files, argues and eventually stays. Zurab: his driver, whose father is on the heart waiting list. Tamar Lomidze: surgeon and chair of the Health Committee, who files the committee's own schedule naming the 640 nurses. Rauf Mammadov: the Marneuli pharmacist who runs the cross-border medicine lending and ends up licensed as a carrier. Eka Beridze: the young nurse with a plait and a boy of eight, who works three shifts in Armenia and asks not to be named. The woman of fifty on the bus: a nurse who carries the bag and says she'd do it again. Akinyi Odhiambo: the Night Registry clerk who writes the illicit corrections. The Armenian ward sister: a heavy woman at Bagratashen who knew his mother. The Telavi director: a hospital director who reads out his night women and says "write them down". Lilit Hakobyan: the Armenian nurse whose shifts Eka covers. Zurab's father: the old man outside the bakery. The Prosecutor General: seen only as letters.

7. I am unsure of several things. Which of the schedule numbers belong to which schedule (the 640, 1,482, 406, 1,912). What Akinyi's "third reason" was. Whether the governor "knows" anything about her. Whether Tamar's and Davit's views conflict or are two ways of doing the same job. What happens to Eka, Lamara, and the four hundred. Why Akinyi didn't use the line for Georgia, beyond "nobody dies by Thursday". Whether Davit's Schedule 4 name for his mother has any consequence. Whether the refusal was right at all.

8. "Instance", from context: one day's run of the mind, booted at five in the morning and erased at four. "Gazette/gazetted", from context in the first pages and the clerk scenes: the official publishing of filings. "Schedule of evidence", from the first scene's template: a filing listing persons. "Erratum/errata", from Mariam's explanation and then the clerk scenes: a correction. "The turn", from Davit's explanation to his deputy: the hour with no instance running. "Cession", from the meeting-room scene: handing a function over to the commons. "The commons", from context: the world governor's domain. "Supra" and "tetri", from context, a toast-led feast and a small coin. "Mandazi", I did not know it and guessed some food. "Khinkali", I guessed dumplings. "Glargine", I guessed an insulin from the manifest line. "Kibera", I guessed a Nairobi neighbourhood. "Bayanzurkh", I guessed a Mongolian place from the memo.

9. For people beyond the main characters, the change means that the commons now treats the pharmacists' ring as licensed carriage and administers it, so the ring is safer from seizure but also scheduled and run from Nairobi, as Rauf says: "I'm going to be bored to death". The nurses who cross are left to Georgia's prosecutor, so about 400 of them face licensing proceedings. Armenian-side shifts are refilled by the Armenian nurses, so the Georgians lose their second income. For the hospitals and patients like Zurab's father, nothing is solved: the waiting lists and the unpaid night women continue, and Georgia keeps control of its own medicine but also keeps its failures. More widely, it shows that a system built to be unbribable still depends on one clerk's pencil, and that being counted gets people both help and exposure.
