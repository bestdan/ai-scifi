# First Reader report: "Paper Is Private" (draft 2)

Note on the read: the file came in three chunks because of its length. I read it once, in order, and did not go back.

## Where I got lost

1. **The opening pages, about the "instance" and the three days (Part One, first scene).** I met "instance 13,485", "booted at dawn in Nairobi", "erased at midnight" and "the world's clock turned over three times" before I knew what any of it was. I didn't know whether the instance was a person, a program or a government. I worked it out by about the fifth paragraph, when it said the file would reach "a mind that did not yet exist" and that it would "believe it entirely". I was still not sure why the three days mattered until the later scenes showed it.
2. **"Gazetted" and the filing chain.** The Gazette Office, the Registry, the Night Registry and the National Gazette Office blurred together early on. I worked it out from the clerk scenes much later. For the first third I just nodded at the words.
3. **The petition, the Act and what Georgia was asking for.** The "Cession Act (Hospitals)" and the "determination" were unclear to me until the meeting-room scene with the deputy and the Armenian precedent. Only then did I see that Georgia wants the world government to take its hospitals, and that it has to look as though it can't cope. After that I was fine. Before it I was guessing.
4. **The forecaster.** I took it for the same thing as the governor for a page. Then I got that it was a cheaper guessing machine. I worked that out from "there was no copy".
5. **"Errata" and the line in each memo that says "One is longer than a clerical correction usually is".** Mariam's explanation made it sound like a joke or a school anecdote. I filed it as texture and didn't suspect it of mattering. When the Sadakhlo order showed up as an erratum, I had to hold two ideas at once, and I was slow. I did understand it once Davit's reaction and the "he could report it" paragraph laid it out.
6. **Why Davit goes to Nairobi (end of Part Two).** I followed him wanting to know whether the machine remembers. Then the story says the machine isn't the problem, it's a person. That went by quickly. I had it from "which desk the Caucasus intake had always gone to".
7. **Akinyi's "A clerk is only a crime."** I didn't understand what she meant. I guessed it meant a clerk's act only counts as a crime, not as a thought. I never settled it.
8. **"And the third reason?" / "I retire next year."** I don't know what the first two reasons were. I read it as something she'd said that I had missed, and it made me feel I'd skipped a line. I did not go back.
9. **The numbers near the end.** The schedule counts (Schedule 4, Part A, Part C, 640, 400, 406, 1,482, 1,912) got hard to track. I couldn't have told you which group was in which schedule. In the memo it says two schedules name the same 640 persons "in different words". I got that Tamar's list and Davit's list overlapped, but not exactly how Davit came to hold them.
10. **Why the pharmacies and the nurses are treated differently by the licence.** "Carriage of goods" is licensed but "persons crossing are not carriage". I got the logic once Davit explained it to Rauf. I still felt I was being walked along a tightrope of definitions, and I was following the rope rather than the people. I worked it out by following the "people aren't goods" lines.
11. **The memo on 17 February and after (Part Three/Four): "The order does not follow from the log. The order stands."** I didn't understand the point until Akinyi's phone call on 1 March. There she explains that each new instance inherited the line and didn't agree with it. Before that I read the sentence as the governor quietly agreeing with her. After her explanation I felt a bit stupid, and a bit moved.
12. **The Świecko timber licence.** It was set up in the first memo as a throwaway and came back as the idea that saved the ring. I enjoyed the return, but at the moment he opens Schedule C I wasn't sure what he was seeing. I got it a few paragraphs later.

## Where I got bored

- **The Registry hall paragraph in Part Three** ("The Registry's work had not changed. The intake came in all day from a hundred and ninety gazettes…"). It's a long explanation block about how the stamping and the batches work. I had mostly understood it already and was waiting for Akinyi to turn up. I skimmed to the clerk with the groundnuts, which I liked.
- **The three identical memos from January 2089** (10,181 to 10,183). I read across them as I was told to, saw they were the same, and wanted to move on. The point was clear in a second.
- **The committee hearing in Part One** (the costing line by line, the 0.9 per cent of GDP, the motion deferred). I kept waiting for something to happen. The corridor conversation with Tamar afterwards woke me up.
- **The two-hour drafting scenes** (the "facing forward" facts, "unable" changed to a count, "collapsing"). They were clever, and the first one I liked a lot. By the second and third I was ahead of him, because I'd learned the trick, and I wanted the scene to go somewhere else.
- **The costing note and the percentages** on the night of 25 February (licence 0 per cent, 40 per cent, Code s. 14(2)). I was waiting for him to decide, and the numbers just sat there.

## Where I was moved, surprised or delighted

- **Page one, the name:** he types LAMARA GOGIASHVILI, 74, WARD SISTER (RET.), takes his hands off the keys "as if the keys were hot", and then deletes it. I was hooked there.
- **Mariam: "That's what remembering is."** And later "It says it doesn't say. That's different." I laughed at the first and felt a small lurch at the second. She is the best voice in the story.
- **The driver: "They say March."** And later the father: "He said, good, he'll see the cherry blossom. He's never looked at the cherry blossom in his life." That one got me.
- **The bus.** The bag of insulin handed from knee to knee by rota, and the young nurse laughing at a voice message about a cow in a lift. When she plays it later and the boy "dissolved, and did not come back" and she says she doesn't know how it ends, I was moved. Then it came back on the other bus and I was moved again.
- **The pharmacist counting to himself: "If I stop, I start again from one."** And the cognac on the belt: "It logged the cognac." Delightful.
- **The mother's ledger.** Columns ruled with a ruler. "Not worked. D. measles." Then 16 May 2054, and the handbag on the chair at the school concert. That was the strongest thing in the story for me. "I raised mine to her nights" was almost too much, but it worked.
- **Akinyi's exercise books and the "After" column** ("Not known." "Not mine to save, or mine and missed."). And "That's what a good night looks like. Exactly like a wasted one."
- **The brown football kit** that the boys hate and "play better in it out of rage".
- **The vaccine turning up in the memo three days later** ("It was correct on the record it had. It does not show for whom"). It stung because I had been agreeing with her reasoning on the night. I resented being made to agree.
- **Lamara through the window** writing next week's rota in a cheap book, and not turning round, and Davit waiting five minutes on her step. I wanted the door to open and I was glad it didn't.
- **The ending in the kitchen.** The radio, now fixed, reading his mother's name aloud in the uncle's voice. Mariam: "Why is Grandma in it?" Then him not saying it. That was the best moment in the ending for me. The valves "everything visible" matched the first mention of the radio, and I noticed.

## Who I cared about, and would I have kept reading past page 2

I cared about **Lamara** first, though she is on the page only in a few scenes. Then **Mariam**, then **Davit's mother**, whom I never met and mourned anyway, then **Eka** with the plait, and **Zurab and his father**. **Akinyi** was fascinating, and I trusted her and was uneasy about her at once, which I think was the point. **Davit** I liked mostly because the others liked or fought with him. By myself I was on his side only some of the time. He is clever and sorry, and sometimes I wanted him to be more annoyed.

Would I have kept reading past page 2? **Yes, but only just.** The first two pages are slow and cold: a man in a coat, a radiator, a form, and a lot of rules I didn't understand. What kept me was the deleted name and the first memory of Lamara in the kitchen, and the line that two thousand one hundred people die on waiting lists. Then the car scene with the driver pulled me in, and by the memo scene with Mariam I was in. If I had been a stranger picking this up, I might have put it down in the middle of the instance and the Nairobi explanation, and I think the stranger would be a reader who needed more of a push.

## The question I'm left with

Was he right to put his mother's name in, when she had refused all her life to be written down and had told Lamara not to give him the book? He did it, and the machine said "no action". I can't tell whether that was a kindness, a betrayal, or both, and the story doesn't tell me. Under that: what happens to Eka and the four hundred, and whether the one line of Akinyi's that governed Sadakhlo for three weeks was mercy or only luck. Akinyi herself says she can't weigh it. I can't either.
