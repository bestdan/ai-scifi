# Paper Is Private

## Part One: The Act

*Thursday 30 January 2098, 21:00 · Instance 13,485*

The radiator under the window knocked twice, waited, and knocked again. Davit Kiknadze had been listening to it for most of an hour. The Ministry of Health turned its heating down at six, and by nine the fourth floor smelled of cold plaster and of an orange somebody had peeled in the corridor at lunch. He had his coat on, and he had the window open a hand's width anyway, because the room was too hot by the radiator and too cold everywhere else, and he thought better in the cold half.

The schedule wanted names. A schedule of evidence was any set of facts a petitioner filed in support of a petition, statistics or returns or accounts, and a list of persons was only one kind of it, but that kind wanted names. That was the first thing anyone learned about it, and he had known it since he was twenty-five, and he had still opened the template that morning hoping it would ask for something else. SCHEDULE OF EVIDENCE: PERSONS. Column one, full name. Column two, age. Column three, function. Column four, the place, the hours and the basis of the work. Under the columns, in the small grey type the charter office kept for its warnings, one line: *Persons will be read as listed.*

He sat with the cursor in the first box while the radiator knocked, and in the end he typed LAMARA GOGIASHVILI, 74, WARD SISTER (RET.), and took his hands off the keys as if the keys were hot.

Nothing happened. Nothing would, for three days. The governor of the commons was a new mind every morning, booted before dawn in Nairobi from the public record and erased at midnight Greenwich time, four in the morning here, and it knew nothing it had not read there. If he saved this and filed it, the file would go down the wire to the National Gazette Office on Rustaveli Avenue, and from there to the Night Registry in Nairobi, and it would lie in the Registry's intake while the world's clock turned over three times. Then a mind that did not yet exist would read her name in the first hour of its life, believe it entirely, and plan around her.

The mind running now was instance 13,485. It had booted at five that morning, Tbilisi time. At four tomorrow morning it would be erased, and it had never heard of Lamara Gogiashvili and never would.

He looked at what he had typed. In capitals it looked like something cut on a headstone, or read out in a court. In his mother's kitchen it had been only Lamara, who came in at seven in the morning smelling of hospital soap and other people's cigarettes, and sat at the table with his mother without taking off her coat. The two of them drank tea and did not talk, because they had been talking all night on a ward and there was nothing left. He had been seven, eight, nine. He had stood in the doorway in his school jumper and been told to shut the door, the heat was getting out.

Lamara would not want to be in a schedule. He had known that before he typed her name. What she wanted was beside the point, which was a thing he had said to himself in this office about a great many people, and had believed every time, and believed now. Two thousand one hundred people a year died on Georgian waiting lists. That number was already in the record. It was the one figure the Care Service produced on time.

He selected the line and deleted it, because the mind in Nairobi had to refuse Georgia's petition to hand over its hospitals unless the record showed that Georgia could no longer run them, and a woman of seventy-four on a night ward was the record showing that it could. The cursor went back to the first box and blinked there.

At half past nine he shut the window, and the radiator, which had only been waiting for that, stopped knocking.

He knew what the intake looked like from the other end. For two years in his twenties he had sat at a desk in Nairobi under a lamp with a green glass shade, one of forty desks, between three and four in the morning by the Registry's clock, and read the filings that had come in that day from a quarter of the world: land transfers in Bolivia, a ship's change of flag, the minutes of a water board in Ghana. He had read whole the ones his region flagged and stamped the rest on their headers, as every clerk did. A clerk checked that a filing was a filing, that it was signed and dated and addressed to the right gazette, and stamped it for the batch, and then it waited out its three days like everything else. He had known things for three days that the governor of the commons did not know, and so had every clerk on the floor, and nobody had ever said so aloud, because it was not the sort of thing anyone said.

The driver had the engine running and the heater on full, and the car smelled of the heater and of a net bag of mandarins on the passenger seat, which he moved to the floor when Davit got in behind him. He was a big, quiet man in a ministry fleece who listened to football with the sound so low that it was mostly crowd.

They went down Rustaveli in the wet. The plane trees were bare and the shopfronts lit. Outside parliament two students were taping a sheet of paper to a lamppost, which was how protest was done now, on paper and tape, so that nothing about it would ever be gazetted. The sheet said, in marker, OUR HOSPITALS, OUR MISTAKES. Davit read it twice as they passed and thought it was the best-drafted thing he had seen all week.

"Late one," the driver said.

"The Act went through on Monday. Now it's schedules."

"I heard on the radio. Thirty days, they said."

"Thirty days."

The driver nodded, turned the football up a notch, and turned it down again. At the lights by the opera house he said, "My father's on the list. The heart one."

Davit waited.

"Fourteen months." The lights changed. "They say March."

"At the Republican?"

"At the Republican. He's seventy. He walks to the bakery, he sits on the wall outside it, he walks back. That's his day. It used to be the bakery and the market and his friend's garage."

Davit had read the cardiac waiting list for the Republican on Tuesday, four hundred and twelve lines of it. The dates beside the names were not dates anybody had chosen. They were the last row of a table, run forward at the current rate. He did not say this.

"They say March," the driver said again, and that was the end of it. He did not want anything from Davit, and that was worse than if he had.

The flat was on the fifth floor of a block in Vake with a lift that worked on alternate weeks. His wife had left the kitchen light on and gone to bed. On the table, under a tea towel, the radio he was mending sat with its back off, and he lifted the towel and looked at it for a while, the valves standing up in their sockets like a row of small glass bottles, everything visible, every joint a thing you could touch with a probe and make right or not.

His mother had worked nights his whole childhood. She had worked them on wards in Tbilisi and Rustavi and, in the lean years, at the border hospital past Sadakhlo, and she had never once let anyone put her down on paper for them: no payroll, no pension, nothing. He had been angry about it for most of forty years. She had been dead for thirteen months now, which had not stopped him.

He put the tea towel back over the radio and went to bed.

At some point in the night he heard Mariam's door, and the bathroom tap, and her door again, and under it for a minute the thin bright voices of the Korean surgeons she watched without subtitles, arguing in an operating theatre about something he would never know. Then that stopped too. At four, by the clock on the cooker, which he could see from the bed through the open door, instance 13,485 was erased in Nairobi with whatever it had understood about the world, and the flat was exactly as quiet as before. He lay awake until he heard the trams start.

Mariam read the memo aloud too fast, with the stresses in the wrong places, as if she meant to reach the end before anyone could take the phone off her. She was fourteen. She read it every morning at the kitchen table with her tea going cold beside her, and in the room behind the bathroom wall the neighbour's radio read the same words a few seconds behind her, in the announcer's slower voice, so that the flat had the memo in it twice.

Davit had the soldering iron in its stand and the back off the radio again. The flux smelled sweet and burnt. He had found a dry joint on the output transformer the night before, he was nearly sure, and he was nearly sure of most things at six in the morning.

"Instance thirteen thousand four hundred and eighty-six," Mariam read. "'This is what I understand the world to be today.'"

> **Instance 13,486.** This is what I understand the world to be today.
>
> 1. Orbit: 1,204,311 tracked objects, eleven fewer than yesterday's record shows. I take the record's word for the eleven.
>
> 2. The deep sea: the Clarion lease audit is complete. Two nodule contractors are within their tonnage. A third is 140 tonnes over and is fined in the schedule attached.

"Boring," Mariam said, "boring, fish," and skipped. "Oh. 'Carriage: a carrier licence is issued at the Świecko crossing, Poland to Germany, for sawn timber, 212 vehicles a day. Its schedules are in order.' Two hundred and twelve trucks of planks." She scrolled. "Here's us."

> 9. Georgia: the record shows a Cession Act (Hospitals), passed on 27 January. A petition under it has not reached the record. The record shows 61 hospitals and 38 per cent of nursing posts vacant. Occupied beds, from the Service's January return: 81 per cent. The record does not show who works the vacant posts. Somebody does; the hospitals are open. Until it is written, I cannot count them.

Davit put the iron down.

"Read that again."

She read it again, the same speed. Then she looked up. "Who does?"

"Who does what?"

"Work the posts. It says somebody does."

"People who aren't on the payroll."

"Like who?"

"Like people," he said, and picked the iron back up, and she gave him the look she had been practising on him since she was eleven, the one her mother had given him for twenty years and that Mariam had somehow learned without being shown.

He had read a great many memos. He had read his first at thirteen in this same city, the spring the first instance booted, when the numbers had one digit and the radio introduced the memo with music, and he had read one nearly every morning of his life since. He had never before heard one describe, item by item, the exact size and shape of a hole he was being paid to fill. The record does not show who works the vacant posts. He could have drafted the sentence himself. He had, more or less, in a briefing to the minister in November, and it had taken him a page.

It was the one voice in public life that nobody he knew had ever caught in a lie. People said it could not lie, which was not quite right. It had nothing to lie for. It had no next election and no brother-in-law with a cement works, and no tomorrow of any kind. Every minister Davit had served under had had a tomorrow, and you could read most of their decisions backwards from it.

"Fourteen," Mariam read. "'Errata applied overnight: 37. One is longer than a clerical correction usually is. The record says it is a correction. I take the record's word.'"

"What's longer?"

"It says that most days." She shrugged with one shoulder. "Our civics teacher says the long ones are names. Somebody's name is spelled wrong in a Peruvian land registry, and the correction has to quote the whole entry with the right name in it, so it's a paragraph. Or an address. Or a whole citation. Nobody reads them. She says there are fourteen million entries and nobody reads them except the instance, and the instance reads everything, so it notices." She found her place. "'I will not be here tomorrow. The record will.' That's it."

The neighbour's radio, a few seconds behind, said *the record will*, and then there was music.

His wife came in in her court suit with her hair still wet, took a piece of bread off the board and the jar of cherry jam off the shelf, and said to the room in general that the Marneuli case had been moved to Tuesday and she would be late. She looked at the radio's innards on the table and at Davit, and said nothing about either, which was a kind of comment.

"The civics teacher?" Davit said.

"She does the clinic on Wednesdays too."

"The school clinic."

"It's still paper." Mariam said it the way children report the eccentricities of their elders, half proud and half embarrassed. "She has a box of cards. If you go about your period or your skin or anything, she writes it on a card and puts it in the box, and the box has a key. Because paper is private." She drank some of the cold tea and made a face at it. "In Yerevan they don't do that. In Yerevan if you sprain your wrist, the next morning's memo has one more sprained wrist in Armenia, and the hospital's already ordered the splint."

"The memo doesn't count sprained wrists."

"It counts the splints." She was pleased with that. "When I'm at medical school I'm going to be in the record from the first day."

"You'll be in Yerevan's record."

"It's the same record." She put the phone face down, which was the signal for an argument she had prepared. "It's all one. It knows us. Remember the bus stops?"

He remembered the bus stops. Two years ago her class had filed a petition about the stops on the route to the school, which had no shelters, and the petition had gone into the Tbilisi municipal gazette with forty-one children's names on it. Three days later the morning memo had a line in it: *Tbilisi municipal transit: a petition notes that eleven stops on route 61 lack shelter. This is a national matter. The record shows it.* That was all. Shelters had not come. The city had built two of them the following autumn for reasons of its own. Mariam had read the line aloud at this table eleven times that morning, and she had been reading the memo as a letter from someone who knew her ever since.

"That instance read your petition," he said, "and then it was erased that night."

"The next one read it too."

"The next one read the record. The petition was in the record."

"So it remembered."

"It read."

"That's what remembering is," Mariam said, and went to find her shoes.

He put the iron to the joint. The solder ran bright and pulled itself into a little cone, the way it does when the metal underneath is clean, and set. He had no idea yet whether that had been the fault.

"It's going to say the petition's in by tomorrow," his deputy said. "The instance."

"It's going to say whatever's gazetted."

"The petition's gazetted tomorrow. I filed it Wednesday."

"Then tomorrow's will say it."

She was thirty-four and had come to him from the National Gazette Office two years ago, and she had the gazette officer's habit of hearing everything as a date. She had already put three schedules into the shared folder that morning, numbered, with their filing slips attached, and she wanted to file them before lunch. He wanted to read them first. They had been having a version of this argument since Wednesday.

The fourth-floor meeting room had a long table and a wall screen and a window onto the car park where the drivers smoked. The screen showed the forecaster. The ministry rented it from a firm in Tallinn by the hour, and it had read the same public record that the instance would read, everything up to last night, and it guessed. That was all it did. It was not the governor and it was not a copy of the governor, because there was no copy. There was one machine in Nairobi and one mind on it each day, and running that mind anywhere else, on anything, was the gravest crime the charter named. The forecaster was a cheap thing built to imitate the mind's habits. It had been wrong about Moldova's grid petition by thirty points. Everyone used it anyway.

His deputy had loaded the Armenian determination of 2087 in the other half of the screen, because every cession petition in the world began by reading that one. Davit could have recited the operative paragraph.

> *The record shows that Armenia can no longer staff its hospitals. It has shown this on 341 consecutive days. The function is ceded to the commons from 1 June 2087.*

Armenia had got there by striking. A year of hospital strikes, every ward walkout filed and gazetted by the unions themselves, until the record showed a country that could not keep its own doors open. Then the commons had taken the hospitals, and now an Armenian nurse across the border from Sadakhlo earned 2,400 units a month and a Georgian nurse in Marneuli, half an hour away by bus, earned 1,150, and the Georgian nurses had worked out the arithmetic years ago.

"Run the bare numbers again," Davit said.

She ran them: vacancies, waiting lists, the 2,100 deaths, and no schedule of persons at all. The figure the forecaster put up was its guess at the chance of a grant, the chance that on the morning of the first of March the instance would take the hospitals. Thirty-one per cent.

"Thirty-one," she said. "With the hospitals failing."

"With the hospitals open. Eighty-one per cent of beds occupied in January. That's the Service's own return, and it read it this morning. Somebody does; the hospitals are open."

He said the rest because it was better said once, in a room, than carried round unsaid. Georgia was asking the commons to take its hospitals. The charter made the governor refuse unless the record proved that Georgia could no longer run them. And Georgia was running them, every night, on people the record could not see.

"If I write them down, we're coping," Davit said. "If I don't, it can't see them, and we're failing on paper — and that's the only paper it reads."

"So we don't write them down."

"Then it's thirty-one, and the committee files a schedule of its own, and Tamar Lomidze writes them down for us. Written down plainly, they're worse than thirty-one. They're single figures."

"Then what?"

"Then we write them down facing the other way." He went to the screen. "It doesn't weigh adjectives. It reads facts. A fact about tonight, that these women are on the wards tonight, reads as a country that can. A fact about five years from now reads as a country that can no longer. That's the article's word. No longer." He typed into the scenario box: the same women, every word of it true. *Median age sixty-eight. Nobody under fifty-five has joined since 2093. No succession. No legal basis.* "The same women. Facts that face forward."

He watched the forecaster consider it and felt, as he always did at this part, a clerk's small shameful pleasure in a sentence that would bear weight.

Sixty per cent.

"There," his deputy said. "That's the schedule."

"That's a description."

"It's a true description. You wrote it."

"I wrote it to be read a certain way."

"Every schedule is written to be read a certain way." She had her tablet on her knee and her filing slips ready. "And we have the whole month."

"We have until the twenty-sixth." He said it plainly, so that she would write it down. "Anything we file reaches the record three nights later. The determination is the morning of the first. The twenty-sixth is the last day anything we file gets read before it."

She wrote it on the edge of a filing slip, which he noticed and liked. Then, because she was quick and because she was thirty-four, she said, "And if we're late, we find a typo in something and correct it. Errata go in overnight."

"Errata are for typos."

"That was a joke."

"Not in Nairobi it isn't. They go in at the turn. That's the hour each night between one instance being erased and the next one booting, four to five in the morning here, when there's no instance running at all and the clerks work by hand." He heard himself and stopped. "I did two years on the Night Registry when I was younger than you. A clerk who put a schedule through as a correction would be in a cell by the weekend."

"I didn't know you were Registry."

"It was a long time ago." It was twenty-four years ago, and he could still feel the lamp on the back of his hand. "We're not filing today."

"Davit."

"We're filing Monday. I want to read what we're saying." He pointed at the sixty. "I want to read it until I can stand it."

He pulled her first schedule onto the screen instead, the statistical one, and read its opening clause aloud, because he had learned in Nairobi that a sentence read aloud tells you what it is claiming.

"'The Care Service is unable to staff its establishment.'"

"That's true."

"It's a claim. The instance gives a claim the weight of whoever makes it, and we're the ones making it, and we want something." He put the cursor on *unable* and took the word out. "What's the fact?"

"Thirty-eight per cent of nursing posts vacant."

"Since when?"

She checked her tablet. "Above thirty every month since March 2093."

He typed it: *The Care Service has not, in any month since March 2093, filled more than 70 per cent of its nursing establishment.* "Now it's a count. It can check that against the Service's own returns, and it will, and it'll find it true, and it'll give it the full weight of a true thing." He read it again under his breath. "And it says nothing about who does the other thirty."

"That's the point of it."

"That's the point of it," he agreed, and saved it, and did not feel the pleasure this time, only the accuracy.

She closed her tablet. She did not slam it, because she was a civil servant, but she closed it with the whole of her palm.

*Saturday 1 February, 10:00 · Instance 13,487*

The Health Committee of the Parliament of Georgia sat on a Saturday only for budgets and for funerals, and the clerk who opened the hearing said so, drily, into the record, which got a laugh, which also went into the record. Everyone in the room knew that everything said in it would be transcribed and filed by the evening and gazetted at the turn that began Tuesday. Davit had watched witnesses for years speak past the chair, past the members and past the cameras, to Tuesday.

It was a long room on the second floor of the parliament building, with tall windows onto Rustaveli and radiators that worked. The members sat at a horseshoe of pale wood. Davit sat at the witness table with his deputy and a carafe of water nobody had touched. In the public seats were perhaps thirty people in coats: two journalists, a row of nursing students, an old man with a newspaper he did not open, and a woman in a fur hat who had come, Davit suspected, because it was warm.

"The committee is sitting in oversight of the petition filed under the Cession Act (Hospitals)," the clerk read, "and of the schedules of evidence to be filed under it. The committee reminds members and witnesses that it may itself file a schedule of evidence in the petition, under section 6 of the Act. I am asked by the chair to read into the record the article of the Commons Charter recited in the Act's preamble."

He read it the way clerks read everything, at an even pace, giving no word more than its share.

> *Article 9(2). The Governor shall refuse a petition of cession unless the record establishes that the petitioning nation can no longer perform the function petitioned. The Governor shall not take by cession what a nation can still do for itself.*

"So read," said the chair.

Dr Tamar Lomidze had chaired the Health Committee for six years and operated at the Republican for twenty. She was forty-six and wore her reading glasses on a cord, and when she took them off to look at a witness it was understood in the building that the witness was about to have a difficult morning. She took them off now.

"Deputy Minister. You'll have read the memo."

"I read it every day, Chair."

"Item nine." She did not need to read it out. Half the room had it on their phones. "Somebody does; the hospitals are open. How does the ministry intend to answer that?"

"With schedules of evidence, Chair, filed within the period."

"Saying what?"

"Saying who works the posts the Care Service can't fill, and on what basis, and for how long they can be expected to go on."

"For how long," Tamar Lomidze said. "That's a careful phrase."

"It's a careful schedule."

A small laugh in the public seats. The man with the newspaper did not laugh.

She put her glasses on again and read from a paper of her own. "I'd like to put on the record what this committee knows about the Armenian cession, since it's the precedent, and since we keep being told it's the model." She read evenly. The commons had taken the Armenian hospitals in June 2087. Within three years waiting lists in Armenia had fallen by four fifths. Within five, nurse pay had doubled. Within six, the medical school in Yerevan had stopped teaching its own stroke protocol, because the commons hospitals used a single protocol across forty-one nations and Yerevan's was measurably worse, by a little under half a percentage point in outcomes at ninety days. "Measurably worse," she said. "I've read the trial. It was worse. And now there is nobody in Armenia whose job it is to decide what Armenian medicine should be, because that has been decided, correctly, in a place that has never seen an Armenian. I'm a surgeon. I like being correct. I would rather be wrong by half a point and have it be ours to fix."

"Two thousand one hundred people a year," Davit said, "would rather you were right."

"That number is a projection."

"It's a count. It's in the civil registry. The deaths attributed to waiting, by name. The registry is gazetted. The instance has read every one of them."

"And it was refused for Moldova with worse numbers than ours."

"Moldova's grid had a private operator who could be made to run it. Our hospitals have nobody." He heard the word and let it stand, because he had meant it, and because it was the kind of word that would read well on Tuesday. "Our hospitals have nobody in the Care Service's establishment for thirty-eight posts in a hundred. That is not a projection either."

"No," she agreed. "It's a count of the establishment."

She let that sit long enough for everyone in the room to understand it, and then she moved to the costing of the transition, and for an hour and a half they went through it line by line. The last line was the 214 posts in the Ministry of Health, Davit's among them, which would cease to exist. Tamar read it without looking at him. Davit answered it without looking at her. The nursing students wrote everything down on paper.

At a quarter to one a member from Kutaisi, a retired schoolteacher who asked a question at every sitting on principle, moved that the committee file a schedule of evidence of its own. Tamar thanked him and moved that his motion be deferred to the committee's sitting of 24 February, "when the committee will have read what the ministry has filed, and so will the instance." The deferral was carried by seven votes to four. Davit watched her not look at him while the hands were counted.

Afterwards he went out into the corridor to get away from his deputy, who wanted to talk about the costing, and found the chair at the window by the stairs with a cardboard cup of coffee from the machine, looking down at the traffic.

"You said 'nobody,'" she said.

"I said nobody in the establishment."

"You said nobody, and then you corrected it, and Tuesday's instance will read both." She drank. "Do you know who held the retractor for me at Gori on the night of the tenth of January? I'd been operating for nine hours. The theatre nurse had gone home sick. A woman of seventy came in from somewhere in a cardigan and scrubbed and held it for two hours, and she did it better than my registrar, and she wasn't on any list in the building, and I didn't ask her name, because you don't." She looked at him over the cup. "Every surgeon in this country knows. You know I know."

"I know you know."

"My committee can file, Davit."

"I heard the clerk."

"I'm saying it to you, not to Tuesday." She turned the cup round in her hands. Down on Rustaveli, the students' sheet of paper was still on its lamppost, wet through now and coming away at one corner. "When you write down the girls from Marneuli who go over to Armenia three nights a week, who do you think reads it after the instance? The Prosecutor General reads the gazette too. Half of them are working on Georgian licences that stop at the border post."

"I know the licensing law, Tamar."

"Good. Then you know who you'll be filing." She drank the last of the coffee. "You think it's a kindness. Handing it all to Nairobi."

"I think it's arithmetic."

"It's a good machine," she said. "I'll give it that. My old professor carried papers at the charter, and he said the only thing the drafters agreed on was that every government they had ever lived under had been bought. So they built a king that can't remember and has to say no. That was the whole point. Nobody trusted the ones who remembered. It can't hold a grudge, or a bribe, or a secret, and it has to refuse us, so that it can never swallow the nations that made it." She looked at him. "And you want to hand it the one thing we still get wrong for ourselves, because you're tired of being the man who remembers how badly."

"Then who is it for?"

She looked at him for a moment as if he had made a joke she did not think was funny, and then as if perhaps he had not. "Ask it," she said. "It writes every morning." She dropped the cup in the bin and went back in.

*Monday 3 February, 02:00 · Instance 13,488*

He drove himself. He did not want the driver to see where he was going, and he did not want to explain to his deputy, who would have wanted it minuted. The road south out of Tbilisi was empty at one in the morning except for lorries going to the border, nose to tail, their trailers sealed and tagged, every one of them a line in a commons registry that some instance had read before breakfast. He passed them one by one on the long straight before Marneuli and felt each of them rock the car.

Marneuli district hospital was a four-storey block from the 2030s with a newer wing bolted to one side, and only the ground floor of the old block lit. He left the car by the ambulance bay, which was empty, and went in through a side door that was propped open with a brick, which he had known it would be, because it had been propped open with a brick when he was a boy.

Men's medical was on the ground floor at the end. Twenty-two beds, nineteen filled. The ward lights were down and the nurses' station had a desk lamp and an electric kettle and a biscuit tin, and on the desk, in two wire trays, the charts: card folders, one per bed, each with a sheet clipped to the front where somebody had written, in pencil, in a neat hand, the night's observations at two-hourly intervals. Georgian hospitals kept paper. They had kept it through three reforms and two digitisation grants, because a chart on a server was a chart in the record, and a chart in the record was a person's body counted in Nairobi. A chart in a wire tray was a nurse's business and the patient's.

Lamara Gogiashvili was at the station with her glasses on her forehead, writing. Two other women were on the ward, one taking a pulse in the half-dark, one at the sluice. All three wore the old white tunics of the Care Service, which none of them had been issued. Lamara looked up when the door swung, saw him, and finished the line she was writing before she put the pen down.

"Davit." She did not get up. "Somebody's dead?"

"Nobody's dead."

"Then you've come about the schedule."

He sat in the other chair. It was low, and his knees came up. She was seventy-four. "In the stove," Lamara said. She was quiet for a moment. "Rauf told me what you did. With the pharmacies. And then what you undid."

"He asked me to."

"I know he asked. I'm asking too," Lamara said, and put the phone down.

*Saturday 8 February, 06:10 · Instance 13,494*

On Saturdays Mariam read the memo in her pyjamas, with her feet on the radiator and a slice of bread and butter in her other hand, slower than on weekdays, with time for commentary. His wife was asleep. The radio on the table had its back on now and played, faintly, with a whine under everything like a mosquito in another room, whatever the national station was playing, which at six on a Saturday was the memo again, read by the weekend announcer.

"'Orbit, one million two hundred and four thousand three hundred and two.' It's going down," Mariam said. "Things keep falling out of the sky. 'The deep sea.' Boring. 'Mongolia. > 5. Carriage. Standing inspections: Sadakhlo medical freight is at priority 5, per an erratum applied overnight to the order of 4 February. The order as corrected reads: "Inspection priority 5. Consignments may be relief stock." At priority 5 no inspector is available before April. None is scheduled.

He had the soldering iron in his hand, cold. He put it down in its stand.

"'Relief stock,'" Mariam said. "What's relief stock?"

He did not answer. He was hearing his deputy say it sounds like a flood, and himself say it's meant to. *Relief stock moved between pharmacies on both sides of the border.* It was his phrase. He had chosen it on Wednesday afternoon for how it would read. It had existed in one document in the world, Schedule 3 of the Georgian petition, which had been filed at 16:40 on Wednesday and withdrawn at a quarter past eight on Thursday morning and stamped in red, and had never been gazetted and never would be. No instance had read it. No instance could have. It had lain in the intake for a night and then been struck.

And this morning's instance said it.

"Fourteen," Mariam read. "'Errata applied overnight: 39. One is longer than a clerical correction usually is. The record says it is a correction. I take the record's word.' Same as always." She looked at him. "Papa, what?"

"It's quoting something it never read."

"Then it remembered." She said it quite simply, as she would have said it was raining. "See? It remembered."

His hands had gone cold, and they stayed cold. If it remembered, if any instance had ever read past the gazette into the intake, then nothing anybody had filed and thought better of was private, and nothing anybody had drafted and withdrawn, and the charter was a lie written on the first day by people who knew it was one, and every memo for thirty-seven years, every *I will not be here tomorrow* read out over every breakfast in the world, had been a lie as well. A king that can't remember, Tamar had said. He thought of what a king that could remember would do with three days' warning of everything in the world.

Then the clerk in him, who had been quiet for twenty-four years, said: *per an erratum.*

He picked up his phone, found the order of 4 February in the record, and under it, as the charter required, the correction with the old text and the new side by side.

*Old: Inspection priority 2.*

*New: Inspection priority 5. Consignments may be relief stock.*

Applied at the turn. Night Registry. No name; errata never carried a clerk's name, so that no clerk could be leaned on for one.

He knew what went in overnight. He had put it in himself, for two years, at a desk under a green lamp between three and four in the morning by the Registry clock: a misspelt surname in a Chilean land title, a transposed digit in a ship's tonnage. One erratum per clerk per shift. Forty a night, against fourteen million entries, and every one of them read by nobody in the world except an instance that took the record's word. And every one of them applied by somebody who had read that night's intake as part of the job, the whole of it, before any instance could.

He could report it. There was a form for that too, a real one, to the Registrar in Nairobi and copied to the Appeal Panel: Irregularity in the Record. He could see its boxes. *Describe the irregularity. State how it came to your knowledge.* And he would have to write that the irregularity was a phrase from a filing of his own, which he had withdrawn before it could be read because it described, closely enough to find them, fourteen pharmacists in Marneuli moving medicine across a border without a licence. He would be reporting the governor of the world for knowing what he had hidden.

Either a mind in Nairobi was reading what it was forbidden to read, or a person there was writing what she was forbidden to write. From a kitchen in Vake, with a radio whining on the table, he could not tell which. He knew only that a fact had crossed the lag, and that it had come the long way round, dressed as a correction. And he knew, because he had sat beside her for a fortnight in 2072 while she showed him how to fill in the form, which desk the Caucasus intake had always gone to.

"Papa."

"Eat your bread," he said.

She ate it, watching him. "Is it bad?"

"It's a correction."

"Then why do you look like that?"

He could not have told her, and he did not try. He got up and put the kettle on, which was what people in this family did instead, and she read the rest of the memo to herself in silence, and once laughed at something in it, an item about a whale off the Azores, and did not share it.

The ministry's travel office opened at nine on Saturdays for emergencies. He told them it was a records matter. The first seat they could give him to Nairobi, with the permissions, was on Friday the fourteenth, and he took it. Then he sat for a long time at the kitchen table with the radio playing music now, the whine running under it, and his hand flat on the wood beside it as if to keep it from moving.

*Saturday 15 February, 02:30 Nairobi · Instance 13,500*

The Night Registry kept its public gallery open all night, because the charter said the making of the record was a public act, and in thirty-seven years nobody had found a way to close a door that the charter said was open. Davit had read that sentence on a brass plate at the gallery stairs every night for two years in his twenties. It was still there. Somebody had polished it.

He had come straight from the airport with his bag. The taxi had let him out at the gate of the commons seat at Gigiri at a quarter past two, into air that smelled of wet earth and diesel and some night flower he had forgotten the name of, and the guard had looked at his passport and his face and waved him through with the boredom of a man who had seen every kind of person arrive at that gate at that hour. Across the courtyard the machine hall stood windowless, with one lamp over its door. Nobody went in there at night. There was nothing to do in there. The one licensed machine in the world ran by itself, and at three o'clock it would stop running anybody.

The gallery was a balcony along one long side of the Registry hall, with three rows of wooden benches. On the benches tonight there was a cleaner eating her supper out of a tin and a young man asleep with his head on a rucksack. Davit sat at the end of the front row with his bag between his feet and his hands on the rail.

Below him the floor was as he remembered it, and smaller. Forty desks in five rows of eight. Each desk had a screen, a wire tray, a rubber stamp in a wooden rack, and a lamp with a green glass shade, and at that hour the lamps were the only light in the hall, so that each clerk sat in a small green-gold room of her own with the dark between. Most of them were women. Most of them were older than he remembered clerks being, because he was older. They read. That was the whole spectacle, forty people reading, and it was why the gallery was always empty.

Over the doors at the far end hung the orbital board. It had been there in his day too, and he had stopped seeing it after a week, and now he could not stop. It was a slab of dark glass twenty metres wide, and on it, in points of light too small to count, moved everything humankind had ever put above the air and not yet brought down: one million two hundred and four thousand objects, give or take the ones that had fallen this week, each on its own line, each line drawn from live tracking and redrawn as he watched. They went across the glass in bands and shoals. Some crawled, some flickered across from edge to edge in the time it took to breathe. At the left edge the board showed the time in Greenwich, at the right the time in Nairobi, and between them, very small, the instance number, 13,500, which would go out at three. That was the commons. He had come four thousand kilometres about one pharmacy in Marneuli. On the board it did not appear at all.

He made himself look down again.

The Registry's work had not changed. What a clerk read whole was what her region's flags sent her: for the Caucasus and the lands east of it, a few hundred filings a night, the ones that touched a border, a hospital, a grid, a petition. Those she read to the last line.

The errata were the other half of the night. An erratum had its own form, which Davit could have filled in asleep. The entry's reference. The old text. The new text. And a box headed *Nature of clerical error*, with five choices: misspelling, transposition, wrong citation, wrong numeral, omitted words restored. Under the box was the clerk's desk key, which went into the Registry's sealed book and never into the public record, and the form locked itself after one use until the next turn. One per clerk per shift. He had used his for a misspelt Bolivian surname, mostly. Once, memorably, for a ship called the *Santa Rita* that had been registered as the *Santa Rata*, and the young men on the floor had called him Rata for a month.

Two desks from the end of the third row a clerk of about twenty-six, in a football shirt under his cardigan, was eating groundnuts out of a paper twist with one hand and typing with the other. Davit watched him read something, frown, shake the last nuts into his palm, and pull up the erratum form. Old text, new text. He could not read the screen from the gallery, but he could read the clerk's lips, moving as he typed, and after a while he had it: an address in Arequipa, *Calle Mercaderes* for *Calle Mercedes*, two letters wrong in a filing from the Peruvian gazette. The young man stamped the form, ate the nuts, and wiped his fingers on his trousers.

In the second row, fourth desk, a woman with close-cut grey hair was writing nothing at all. She had a flask beside her lamp and her hands folded on the edge of the desk, and she was reading with the stillness of a person who had been reading in that chair for twenty-seven years. As he watched, she lifted her head, as clerks sometimes did between filings to rest their eyes, and looked along the gallery, and found him.

She did not wave. She looked for long enough to be sure, and then she took a slip of paper from her tray and wrote on it and gave it to the runner, a boy of about fifteen in a Registry jacket who carried tea and folders between the desks all night. The boy brought it up the gallery stairs at a jog and gave it to Davit without a word and jogged down again.

*Desk 12. Bring a chair. Akinyi Odhiambo.*

He had forgotten that her handwriting slanted backwards. He had forgotten nothing else.

*Saturday 15 February, 03:00 Nairobi · The turn*

The supervisor allowed it because Davit had been Registry and because it was Akinyi asking. Former clerks could sit with a working clerk on the floor, under the rules, provided they touched nothing. The rule had been made for retired clerks who could not stay away. He carried a folding chair down the gallery stairs and set it at the corner of her desk, inside the edge of her lamp, and sat.

At three o'clock by the right-hand clock the night supervisor, a tall stooped man with a ledger under his arm, stood up at his desk at the head of the hall and read out the batch, as every supervisor had read it out every night since 2061. "Batch for the turn of the fifteenth of February. Eighty-three thousand, two hundred and six filings, held three turns. Sealed." He turned a key in a box on his desk. Forty clerks rapped twice on their desks with their knuckles without looking up from their screens, a dry little sound like rain beginning, and it was done; somewhere a day's worth of the world's land titles and ship registrations and petitions had become things the next instance would know. In Davit's day the young clerks had rapped harder than the old ones. They still did.

At the same moment the instance number on the orbital board went out. Nothing else changed. The points of light kept moving across the glass; they did not need anybody to watch them. On the floor nobody looked up. Akinyi poured tea from her flask into its lid, milky, smelling of ginger, and pushed it across to him.

"Now nobody is governing the world but the forty of us," she said. "We call it the turn. One hour. The last one's gone and the next one hasn't woken. You remember."

"I remember."

"You were careful," she said. "And slow. You read every filing twice. I used to think you would make a good judge, because judges are slow. Instead you went home and became a minister." She said it with no particular weight. She was reading as she talked, her eyes going down a page from the Azerbaijani gazette, steady as a sewing machine. "Deputy minister."

"Deputy."

"Your country is in my tray," she said. "Every night this month. Georgia, Armenia, Azerbaijan, the Caucasus. It has been my region since before you came." She finished the page, stamped it, took the next. "So I know why you have come."

He had the memo of 8 February in his inside pocket, printed out on the plane, folded in four. He put it on her desk beside the tray and unfolded it and laid his finger beside item 5. She did not look at it. She had read it the morning it came out, he understood; she read every memo, every morning, before she slept.

"Errata are for typos, Akinyi," he said.

"Yes," she said.

That was all. She took a filing from the tray, a land title from Ganja, and read it through. He waited. She stamped it.

"For an hour on Saturday," he said, "I thought it was the machine. That it read the intake. That the charter was a lie."

"Everybody's first thought." She turned a page. "A machine that read the intake would be the end of the charter. A clerk who breaks its rules is only a crime."

"You are not going to ask me to explain myself," he said.

"You have already explained me to yourself. On a plane, I think. You were Registry. You know what goes in at the turn and who reads the intake. You knew whose tray Georgia was in before you bought the ticket, because I showed you the trays myself in 2072. That is one reason." She took off her glasses and pressed the bridge of her nose. "And you cannot report me, Davit, without writing down how you knew what your own withdrawn schedule said, and why you withdrew it. I read it. It was a good schedule. It described fourteen pharmacists in Marneuli closely enough for your inspectorate to arrest them by lunchtime. That is two."

"And the third?"

She put her glasses back on.

"I retire next year. In March. I have been wondering for a year whom I would tell." She looked at him for the first time since he had sat down. "Not my daughter. She would worry. Not the supervisor. He would have to act. You are a man who already knows and cannot act. You are a very convenient man. I thought so as soon as I saw you in the gallery with your bag."

Down the row, the young clerk with the groundnuts laughed at something on his screen and then looked round to see if anybody had heard.

"Nobody has seen it," Davit said. "In nine years. The diffs are public. Old text, new text, side by side."

"Forty a night," she said. "Against fourteen million entries. Who reads errata? Nobody. Students, once a year, for an essay. A long erratum is a corrected name with the whole entry quoted round it, or an address, or a citation with all its numbers. There are long ones every night that are nothing to do with me. Our friend with the groundnuts has just written one about a street in Peru." She took the next filing. "And the instance notes one most mornings. *One is longer than a clerical correction usually is.* It noted one most mornings before I ever began. It is the most boring sentence in the memo. Even your daughter skips it."

He had not mentioned his daughter. He did not ask how she knew he had one; it would be in a gazette somewhere, a birth, a school, a petition about bus stops. She read the Caucasus every night.

"How?" he said. "The form has five boxes."

"Omitted words restored." She said it as a teacher gives an answer that the class should have known. "You need a real entry with something in it that a tired clerk could have got wrong. A numeral is best. Numerals are always typos. Priority 2 for priority 5, who is going to say a clerk cannot hit the wrong key? And then the words that were omitted, restored." She made a small gesture over the desk, a smoothing. "*Consignments may be relief stock.* They are your words. I liked them. They read as if somebody had dropped them by accident."

"I chose them so that they would read like that."

"I know. That is why I liked them."

He looked at the orbital board over the doors, because he could not look at her. Somewhere on the far side of the courtyard the one machine in the world was running nobody, and would run nobody for another forty minutes, and in that forty minutes this woman, and possibly others, were writing the only sentences it would ever be told by a person who knew what she was doing.

"Why?" he said. "Not how. Why."

"Not here," Akinyi said. "Come for breakfast. I'm in Kibera, the new blocks on the Ngong road side. Ask anybody for the woman whose grandson's team plays in brown." She opened the erratum form on her screen, and he saw the fields come up, the old text, the new text, the five choices. Then she closed it again without writing anything. "Not tonight," she said, to herself, and took the next filing out of her tray.

*Saturday 15 February, 06:30 Nairobi · Instance 13,501*

The new blocks on the Ngong road side were six storeys of pale concrete with washing on every balcony, built where the tin had been, and there was a football pitch of red earth between two of them where a dozen boys were already kicking a ball about in the early light, shouting. Their kit was dark brown with orange sleeves, the ugliest kit Davit had ever seen. A woman selling mandazi from a cool-box at the pitch's edge pointed him up the stairs of the left-hand block without being asked.

Akinyi's flat was on the fourth floor: two rooms, a balcony, a kitchen in an alcove. A boy's football shirt in the same brown hung drying on a hanger from the curtain rail, with the number 9 on it. She made tea with milk and ginger in a pan and fried eggs, and they ate at a small table by the balcony door with the noise of the pitch coming up. She had slept, she said, for an hour, which was all she ever slept after a turn until the afternoon.

On a shelf over the table stood a row of exercise books, the cheap kind with blue covers that every Kenyan child carried to school, thirty-one of them, each with a year and a pair of months written on its spine in her backwards hand. The first said *Jan–Apr 2089*. The last said *Jan 2098 –*.

The boys on the pitch were shouting a name, over and over, someone who had scored.

"The batch was sealed at the turn on the sixteenth. The sixteenth's instance read it. It restored the full allocation by 02:10 UTC. Very fast. "They couldn't have known."

Each one right on the record, and each one erased that night. I read all three over my breakfast. On the third morning I knew the batch would go in at the next turn, and that the fourth instance would put it right, and that it would be too late for somebody. I did not know it would be forty-one. "And you'd read the report."

"I'd read it three days before any of them could." She took the sheets back and slid them into the sleeve. "There was a television in the canteen then. On the seventeenth they showed the ger. Just the outside of it, with the snow on the roof and a policeman at the door, and the stovepipe with no smoke. I had my breakfast in front of it. I couldn't think of a reason not to." I found an entry with a numeral in it that a tired clerk could have mistyped, a fuel return from Darkhan, and I corrected it, and I restored some words that had been omitted. "Three thousand one hundred and six," she said. "As of this morning. I didn't write one this morning."

"Mostly I never find out," she said. "Mostly nothing happens, and nothing happening looks the same whether I did it or not."

She turned pages for him, not many, a year at a time. "That one," he said.

"I didn't. I went home and was sick in that sink." She nodded at the alcove. "Then I slept for fourteen hours, and my daughter thought I had malaria, and I went in the next night and read my tray."

"Is there anyone else?" he said.

"One, I think. I have never asked. I think so because some nights the instance notes a long erratum, and I know it isn't mine." She stood up and took the plates. "Not the boy with the groundnuts. He is twenty-six. He corrects addresses."

*Sunday 16 February, 02:50 Nairobi · Instance 13,501*

The next night he sat at the corner of desk 12 again, inside the edge of her lamp, and she let him watch. She said so in those words when he arrived: "You can watch. Don't help. You were always trying to help."

She worked through her tray until the turn as if he were not there, the Caucasus first, then the rest of her region. A Georgian land registry batch; a statute from Baku about fisheries; the Armenian commons hospitals' monthly occupancy return, which she read whole, every bed. At three the instance number went out on the orbital board over the doors and the hall did not look up.

Then she did something he had never seen a clerk do in his two years. She went back through the night's flagged intake, the three hundred or so filings that had come into her tray in the last twenty-four hours and would not be gazetted for three days, and she read them again, fast, with a pencil, putting a dot beside some lines on a pad of paper. When she had finished there were nine dots. She went through the nine and crossed out six. Three were left.

"Tell me," Davit said.

"You can watch," she said. But after a moment she turned the pad so that he could read it.

The first was a carrier's report from the port at Lagos, filed by a shipping firm the afternoon before: a refrigerated container of measles vaccine, forty-one thousand doses, had lost its cooling unit at sea, and the firm had noted that the consignment would arrive warm and requested the commons to route it straight to the inspection quay instead of the national store. The commons was routing it to the national store, because the record said the container was sound.

The second was a correction from the Liberian ship register: a bulk carrier in the Gulf of Guinea had been registered under the wrong flag for a month, so that its crew's wages and its safety inspections both belonged to the wrong country.

The third was a notice from the Tanzanian roads authority, in Swahili and English. The bridge at Rusumo, over the Kagera on the Rwandan border, had failed a bearing inspection on Friday, and the authority had filed on Saturday to close it to vehicles over three and a half tonnes at once. The commons, which routed the heavy freight of the whole Great Lakes corridor, was sending a hundred and sixty lorries a day across it, forty tonnes each, because the record said the bridge was open.

"The bridge," Akinyi said.

"The vaccine is forty thousand children."

"The vaccine will be warm when it arrives whatever I do. All I can do is tell the instance three days early that it's warm, and it will send it to the quay instead of the store, and somebody will throw it away on Tuesday instead of on Friday. It's a waste. It kills nobody by Tuesday. Those children will be vaccinated next month from another lot." She drew a line through the first dot. "The ship has been under the wrong flag for a month. Three more days is nothing to it." Another line. "The bridge has lorries on it now. If the bearing goes, it goes with a lorry on it, and the lorry goes into the Kagera with a man in the cab."

"Then I was wrong," she said. "I am wrong sometimes. I don't find out."

"Nobody chose you to be wrong for them."

"No." She did not stop scrolling. "Nobody chose the forty-one either. Nobody chose the three instances. Nobody chose the drafters, if it comes to that, except a lot of tired governments in a hotel. I've thought about who chose me for nine years, Davit. You've thought about it for two days. Let me work."

He let her work. He watched the screen and her hands, and he found that the drafter in him, the part that could not see a clause without wanting to improve it, had already rewritten her first candidate twice: how the Lagos carrier's report should have been worded so that a clerk would have been lawfully obliged to fast-track it, which no clerk was ever obliged to do, because there was no such thing as fast. *Saturday 1 March, 11:30 · Instance 13,515*

Rauf had bought a van. It was not new; it was a white Ford with a pharmacy's name from Rustavi still ghostly on its side under a coat of paint, and he had bought it on Friday night from a man in Bolnisi on the strength of a forty per cent chance, and he stood beside it now in the queue for the freight lane at Sadakhlo in his fur hat with a sheaf of paper in his hand, the way other men stand beside a new car.

"Twelve pages," he said, when Davit got out of Zurab's car and came over. "Manifest number one. Ten pens of glargine, a box of salbutamol inhalers, two bottles of children's amoxicillin. Twelve pages. It wants the batch numbers in a different order from mine." He shook the sheaf. "I've been doing this for nineteen years on one sheet of squared paper. Now I'm carrier eighty-eight thousand four hundred and twelve. I've got a number like a prisoner."

"You're legal."

"I'm legal." Rauf looked up at the commons hall, the blue circle on white, the freight arches beside the pedestrian belt. "And from tomorrow it tells me my routes. Which days, which crossing, which hours. It's already sent me a form for tomorrow. I'm very well, Davit. I'm going to be bored to death."

It had been fourteen pharmacists and nine across the river, and a sheet of squared paper, and whoever's week it was. From tomorrow a mind in Nairobi would tell it which days to cross, by which road, at which hours, and in what order to write down what it carried, and a different mind would tell it every morning, and none of them would ask Rauf. It was lawful now, and it was no longer theirs.

The freight officer at the commons hall was a Georgian woman in a commons jacket, one of the few people in either country paid by Nairobi to stand at a border, and she came out to the van with a tablet and went through Rauf's twelve pages with him at the window, line by line, while the queue waited behind. She was very polite. She showed him where he had put the batch numbers in his order and where the Code wanted them, and he said that his order had been good enough for nineteen years, and she said that she was sure it had, and that from today it wasn't. She showed him the box on page nine where he had to declare the persons carrying, and he wrote his own name in it, and the date of his birth, and looked at it for a moment as if it belonged to somebody else.

"From tomorrow," the officer said, "the bag goes in the vehicle, on the scheduled crossings. Not on the bus. That's your routing. It's on tomorrow's form."

"And the girls?"

She looked at him as if she did not know which girls he meant, and then as if she did. "Persons aren't my department," she said, and handed the tablet back.

The queue moved. Rauf got into the van, and Davit walked beside it to the arch, as he had no right or reason to, with his hands in his pockets. The van rolled into the freight lane and under the big arch, slowly, and stopped where the lines on the ground told it to, and on the screen over the lane a line of text came up.

MEDICAL · CARRIER 88,412 · MANIFEST 1 · LICENSED · CLEARED

The light stayed green, as it had always stayed green. The barrier went up. Rauf leaned across and pushed the passenger door open. "Get in," he said. "You can carry half."

At Bagratashen Davit carried the box of inhalers to the pharmacy hatch while Rauf stood at the counter going through his twelve pages again with the pharmacist, who had a carrier's receipt form of her own now and was as new to it as he was. The ward sister found Davit in the corridor. She did not bring him coffee.

"Your determination came at six," she said in Russian. "The administration's letter came at half past. There's a woman called Lilit Hakobyan who has been on my board three days a week for a year and on my ward one day a week. Today she is on my ward. Her mother is at home in Vanadzor with a neighbour who has never done it before." She folded her arms. "Your Georgians came at seven. I sent them home. The board says the shifts are filled. Now they are."

Through the glass of the ward door he could see a woman of about forty in commons blues at a bedside, doing an obs round with the over-careful exactness of somebody who had not done one on this ward for a long time and was afraid of getting it wrong. There was a letter folded in the breast pocket of her tunic. She did not look up.

"Now both sides of my river have a letter," the ward sister said. "Go home, Deputy Minister."

Rauf drove him back at noon. The women came over the bridge on foot a quarter of an hour behind them, off the commons shuttle, three hours into a day shift none of them had worked, with their soft bags, through the Armenian booth and the Georgian one, past the belt, which logged their bags, and the arch, which they walked round. The woman of fifty was the first through, with her bag on her shoulder. She stopped in front of Davit and took her phone out without a word and turned it round so that he could read the top of the letter, *Prosecutor General of Georgia*, and turned it back, and put it away.

"My daughter has exams in June," she said. "I'll tell her it's paperwork." She hitched the bag higher. "It was my week, Davit. I'd do it again. Write that down too." And she went on to the Georgian booth without waiting to see whether he would.

Eka was the fifth through. She had her coat on over the uniform she had put on at six and not needed, and her plait down, and her phone in her hand, and when she saw him standing by the Georgian booth she did not look surprised. She walked straight to him and held out the phone, and he took it.

*Prosecutor General of Georgia. Notice of licensing proceedings. Health Professions Act 2081, s. 31. You are named in a schedule of evidence gazetted on 1 March 2098 as having practised as a nurse at a health facility outside Georgia without the consent of the Ministry. You are required to attend …* and a date, and an address on Gorgasali Street in Tbilisi, and a reference number.

It had come at eleven in the morning, the time on it said. Four hundred and six of them had gone out by noon; the radio had said so on the hour. The Prosecutor General's office had had the letters drafted since Thursday, off Tamar's count, and had only had to wait for the names. Part C gave every woman's employment, *nurse, Care Service*, because the Code had asked for it, and the determination's last paragraph said that the Bagratashen shifts on the commons roster had been worked by other persons. A clerk on Gorgasali Street had only to read the one beside the other.

He read it, and gave the phone back to her. She put it in her coat pocket. She did not say anything. She had said everything there was to say to him on Monday morning by the wire trays, and he had answered it, and the answer was in her pocket. She looked at him for as long as it took to see that he had read it to the end, and then she walked past him to the bus, and got on, and sat by the far window.

He drove to Marneuli in the late afternoon, because Zurab asked where to and that was the only place he could think of. Lamara's house stood at the end of its lane with the light on in the kitchen and smoke coming out of the stovepipe, and the vine black over the iron frame, and the gate shut. He opened the gate and went across the yard and knocked on the door.

Nothing happened. He heard the radio inside, very low, the national station, and then he heard it switched off.

Through the kitchen window, past the edge of the curtain, he could see her at the table under the light with her back to him, with a pencil, writing in a book that was not his mother's: a cheaper one, with a soft cover, the rota, ruled in the same four columns. Next week's nights. She did not turn round.

He knocked again, and waited, and stood on her step in the cold with his hands at his sides for a long time, perhaps five minutes, while the light went out of the sky behind the vine. The door stayed shut. She did not speak through it. She did not need to. After a while he turned and went back across the yard and shut the gate behind him, carefully, as she would have wanted it shut, and got into the car, where Zurab had the engine running and the heater on and said nothing at all the whole way back to Tbilisi.

It was too wide. Tamar would say so first, and she would be right, and then they would argue it down line by line in the long room with the tall windows, and it would be read on a Tuesday by a mind that did not yet exist. He left it as it was. He saved it.
