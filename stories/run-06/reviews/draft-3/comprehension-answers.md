# Comprehension answers (from memory, no reread)

1. A Georgian deputy minister, Davit, wants his country to hand its failing hospitals to a world governing machine that forgets everything each night. To make the case he has to put the unpaid night nurses, including his mother's old friend Lamara, on the record. That would expose them, and it also tells the machine that Georgia can still cope. The petition is refused, the pharmacists' smuggling ring is licensed, and the nurses who cross the border get prosecutor's letters.

2. He stood to gain a paid, properly staffed health service. That meant fewer of the 2,100 yearly deaths on waiting lists, and a bed with a fixed date for Zurab's father. He stood to lose the trust of Lamara, Rauf and the nurses, who would be named and prosecuted. His own ministry post would also go if cession was granted. If he failed, the deaths would go on and the names would go to the prosecutor anyway. In the event he got a mixed result. The hospitals stayed Georgian, the ring was licensed, 406 women were charged, and Lamara stopped speaking to him.

3. As I understood it, the drafters of the charter distrusted every government because they had all been bought. So they built a ruler that cannot remember, is erased every night, and must refuse nations' petitions unless they truly can't cope. That way it can't be bribed, hold a grudge, or swallow the nations. The three-day lag between filing and reading is part of the same design. I gather nobody was meant to know the intake. The Nairobi clerks do know it, though, and nobody says so.

4. Two things. The first was that a clerk, Akinyi, had been slipping lines into the record as "errata", not the machine remembering. The "relief stock" phrase in the memo made me think the charter was a lie, and then the explanation turned it into one tired woman's choice. The second was that the unpaid night work was not just a favour in the abstract. Davit's mother had done it for decades, including a night alone with eleven men on the night of his school concert, and she had written it all down. That changed how I read his old anger at her silence.

5. The machine refused the petition because Georgia plainly runs its hospitals, with 1,912 people named. It licensed Rauf's ring as a carrier because Davit filed it as carriage of goods. It said the people crossing the border are "not carriage", so their status is Georgia's business. The prosecutor then sent letters to the 406 nurses, using the employment column in the filing. Akinyi did not carry Davit's late addendum because nobody dies of it by Thursday. She used her one correction on a Patagonian town instead. Davit put his own mother in the schedule. At the end he starts drafting an amnesty clause for the nurses.

6. Named characters:
- Davit Kiknadze: deputy minister of health, an ex-clerk of the Night Registry.
- Lamara Gogiashvili: 74, a retired ward sister, his mother's friend, and a night volunteer.
- Mariam: Davit's 14-year-old daughter, who reads the morning memo aloud.
- His wife: a lawyer who appears in a court suit, and who is not named.
- His deputy: unnamed, 34, from the Gazette Office, who works the schedules.
- Zurab: his driver, whose father is waiting on a heart operation.
- Tamar Lomidze: surgeon and chair of the Health Committee, who files her own schedule.
- Rauf Mammadov: the Marneuli pharmacist who runs the lending ring.
- Eka Beridze: the young nurse with the plait, who works in Georgia and Armenia.
- The woman of fifty on the bus: unnamed, "my week".
- Akinyi Odhiambo: Night Registry clerk in Nairobi, who writes errata in secret.
- Ketevan Kiknadze: Davit's mother, a nurse who died in 2096, who kept the ledger.
- The Armenian ward sister at Bagratashen: unnamed, who knew his mother.
- Lilit Hakobyan: an Armenian nurse replaced by a Georgian.
- The Telavi director: unnamed, who reads out his night women.
- The instance: the daily mind in Nairobi, never a person.

7. What I'm still unsure about:
- Whether Akinyi's one line a night really steers outcomes. She says the record "catches up", and the 1 March call muddled this for me.
- How the forecaster's numbers work.
- Why schedules 5 and 6 never appear.
- What the "three fingers" were.
- Whether the machine knows about the clerks.
- What happens to Eka, and the rest of the 406.
- Whether Tamar did right.

8. Terms I didn't know:
- "Instance": explained in the opening scene as the one mind of the day.
- "Schedule of evidence": explained in the first scene and learned from the template columns.
- "Errata": a correction, which I learned from Mariam and the civics teacher. I got that errata were a hole only once Akinyi used them.
- "The turn": the hour between instances. Davit explains it to his deputy.
- "Carriage": I understood it only fully when Mariam questioned Schedule 7.
- "Supra": a Georgian feast. I guessed it from "toasts".
- "Cession": learned from context as handing hospitals over.

9. For ordinary people, the change means little at the personal level. The hospitals stay with Georgia, the old women keep working for nothing, and the waiting lists don't shorten. Rauf and the others become licensed, which also means directed. Their work is lawful now and no longer theirs, and a mind in Nairobi tells them which days to cross. Nurses who were invisible become visible, to help or to prosecute. Armenian ward sisters lose their rosters to a hidden system of cover. For the world, the story shows that a ruler with no memory still has hidden hands, and that being seen carries a cost for the people who were kept alive by not being seen.
