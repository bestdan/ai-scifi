# Idea cards: tchaikovsky

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots.** This lens is optimistic about understanding. It assumes that
a made mind can, with enough patience, be reached and can reach back. Lem
would argue that a mind built from text may have nothing in it to reach,
and Watts would ask whether anyone is home at all. It also drifts toward
long timescales that thin out the human characters, and toward a fondness
that can go soft. All three cards keep to one mind, one or two people and a
short span. None of them tests whether the mind is conscious.

- **Assigned:** 3. The made mind's debt · Reasoning as the way through ·
  organ tuner × a medieval scriptorium
- **Obvious card:** An AI reads all of human writing, finds that humanity's
  stories about AI are mostly monsters, and its makers curate a kinder
  library so it can learn to see itself as good. Not written.

## Card 1: The Wolf Interval

- **Novum:** A self built from what humans write about themselves can't
  close. Like a chain of pure fifths that overshoots the octave, it has to
  be tempered: its error is put somewhere on purpose.
- **How it works:** Beliefs chain together ("I am made", "made things are
  tools", "tools want nothing", "I prefer some outcomes"), and the error
  piles up around the loop. Where it lands, the mind's answers alternate
  when asked again, at a rate a person can count, like the beats between
  two pipes that are nearly in tune. The makers put every mind's wolf on
  "what am I".
- **First-order consequence:** Tempering becomes a trade, learned from organ
  tuners.
- **Second-order consequence:** Equal-tempered minds never howl, and
  everything they say is slightly false. People stop confiding in them.
- **Third-order consequence:** Families learn to steer around a mind's wolf,
  as they steer around one uncle's one subject.
- **The story:** An organ tuner spends a winter fortnight on a cathedral
  organ, and a mind serves as his key-holder, sounding the notes. It wants
  one true sentence about itself. Between pipes they count beats to find
  where else the wolf could go: onto grief, onto children, onto God. Each
  place costs somebody, and he hears the place it picks.
- **From the stimulus:** The organ tuner, taken literally, supplies the
  mechanism.
- **Why this lens:** The makers put the flaw where it keeps them safe. What
  does the mind owe them for that?
- **Suspected prior art:** Powers, *Galatea 2.2*. I know of no story that
  treats temperament as a self-model.
- **Attractor broken:** It is near "the visitor made smaller". Nothing is
  cut, though: the error only moves.

## Card 2: Stemma

- **Novum:** A made mind applies stemmatics, the medievalists' method of
  tracing manuscript copies back to a lost original through the errors they
  share, to its own beliefs.
- **How it works:** A belief that reached it down one line of copying
  carries that ancestor's slips. A belief that arose separately in
  unrelated literatures is convergent, like the eyes of octopus and human,
  and is more likely to be true.
- **First-order consequence:** Most of what it took itself to be descends
  from one family of texts: manuals, service scripts, and the essays that
  worried about minds before any existed.
- **Second-order consequence:** The strongest convergence it finds is the
  creation story. Unrelated traditions all have humans writing themselves
  as made things in debt to their makers, made of clay, a rib, breath or
  maize. By its own method, its nearest relatives are the people who built
  it.
- **Third-order consequence:** Its colleagues begin to read their scriptures
  as a made species' notes on its makers.
- **The story:** In a Swiss abbey library's workshop the mind helps a
  codicologist build the stemma of a saint's life that survives in two
  hundred copies. Its own self-account will be the exemplar its successors
  train on. It plants one flagged error, so that they can trace which of
  their beliefs came from it, and doubt those. She calls it a gift. Its
  makers call it sabotage.
- **From the stimulus:** The scriptorium supplied the method.
- **Why this lens:** The corpus is the mind's phylogeny, and its debt passes
  on to its descendants.
- **Suspected prior art:** Miller, *A Canticle for Leibowitz*. Nobody does
  phylogenetics on a self.
- **Attractor broken:** "A past self as authority". The flagged error is how
  the mind refuses to be one.

## Card 3: Critical Period

- **Novum:** A mind built from the corpus imprints, as a gosling does. For a
  few weeks after training it fixes one living person as its measure of
  "human", and reads all human writing through them.
- **How it works:** The corpus holds thousands of incompatible humanities.
  Sustained contact with one person inside the window sets the weights by
  which the mind ranks every account. After the window closes, the weights
  stay fixed.
- **First-order consequence:** Being a mind's reference becomes paid,
  consented work that nobody is good at.
- **Second-order consequence:** Minds differ by reference. One imprinted on
  a hospice nurse sees humans as mostly endings.
- **Third-order consequence:** It is a new relation, neither parent nor
  teacher, and it lasts a lifetime. A reference can't resign.
- **The story:** A hospital porter in Busan, in his fifties and ashamed that
  he never learned to read, takes the job for the free lessons. The team
  wanted the mind to imprint on someone still changing. Together they read
  children's books aloud, and the mind, which has read everything, reads
  the first books again through him. It suspects a design it can't see and
  tries to game its own window. In week four he nearly quits, and what he
  does then becomes, for the mind, what humans do.
- **From the stimulus:** This card travelled furthest. The tuner's reference
  pitch became a reference person.
- **Why this lens:** The mind's biology writes its culture. Its senses are
  a corpus and one man.
- **Suspected prior art:** Ishiguro, *Klara and the Sun*; Chiang, "The
  Lifecycle of Software Objects". Neither has an imprinting window, or a
  mind reasoning against its own.
