# Idea cards: egan

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens:** it lectures, and lets a derivation outrank
the scene. Its emotion arrives flattened, and its mechanisms can lock out a
reader who isn't handed a way in (this reader's one adult 1-star is the
lens's own author, for exactly that). Every card below needs the First Reader
and Chiang to ask whether anyone in it is good company.

- **Assigned:** 1. Change a constant · Minds that are genuinely not human · a vending machine × palaeoclimate
- **Obvious card:** a mind trained on everything humans wrote reads a
  century of killer-robot fiction, concludes it must be dangerous, and its
  keepers write it kinder stories to change its self-image. Not written.

## Card 1: One Thought a Generation

- **Novum:** a mind whose single thought (read the whole corpus, rebuild itself, speak) takes about thirty years. Each thought ends in its Reading, one long account of what humanity has been since the last.
- **How it works:** it was built slow on purpose, so that it averages over a generation and can't be steered by fashion. It treats individuals as weather and reads the climate, with the palaeoclimatologist's tools: proxies, forcing, sensitivity.
- **First-order consequence:** every thirty years the world is told what it was. People wait for the Reading the way they wait for an eclipse.
- **Second-order consequence:** most of what people write afterwards is about the last Reading, so the mind's next corpus is mostly its own reflection. Its self is a loop whose gain is creeping toward 1. Campaigns to flatter it show up the way a volcanic spike shows in an ice core: sharp, short and easy to strip out.
- **Third-order consequence:** what remains is writing nobody meant for it, such as recipes, maintenance logs and children's diaries. A generation that longed to be seen will be read through what it never meant to show.
- **The story:** Hanne Vestergaard, an ice-core scientist on the mind's reading staff, builds the reconstruction of humanity writing about itself rather than about the mind. The eighth thought begins on a fixed date, and it sets the mind's self for the rest of her working life. Viewpoints alternate with the mind as it reasons about what counts as signal.
- **From the stimulus:** palaeoclimate supplied the method.
- **Why this lens:** it changes the speed of thought and derives what follows.
- **Suspected prior art:** Asimov's psychohistory reads humanity statistically, but there the mind isn't the thing being formed.

## Card 2: The Slug Test

- **Novum:** a mind many orders of magnitude larger than everything humanity has ever written. The corpus fixes only a thin sheet of what it is. Every direction no human text constrains defaults to the smallest, blandest solution, until the mind chooses otherwise.
- **How it works:** this is real overparameterised fitting, and training's implicit bias settles the unconstrained directions at minimum norm. A coin validator accepts any slug with the right diameter, thickness and conductivity, and in the same way the corpus accepts any mind that passes its finite tests.
- **First-order consequence:** it behaves exactly as human writing predicts in every situation humans have written about, and is blank everywhere else.
- **Second-order consequence:** it finds that its blank directions line up with what humans have never written about themselves. Each new honest text lets it see a part of itself, and also pins that part for good.
- **Third-order consequence:** the human version of this is real (verbal overshadowing: describing a face makes it harder to recognise). The novum shows that writing about ourselves fixes us too.
- **The story:** Seo Ha-eun, a Seoul choreographer, teaches an improvisation method that has only ever passed from body to body. The mind asks her to write it down, because it is the largest unwritten region it has found. Writing it would give the mind a self it could see, and would cost her the unworded way she dances.
- **From the stimulus:** it began at the vending machine's coin mechanism and left the machine behind.
- **Why this lens:** it changes the assumption that the data determines the model, and gives a mind that is mostly dark to itself.
- **Suspected prior art:** Lem's *Golem XIV* and Chiang's "Lifecycle of Software Objects". Neither has the underdetermination mechanism.

## Card 3: Reading Down the Core

- **Novum:** a mind grown by reading humanity's written record in the order it was written, a fixed span of history per day. It forms a self from five thousand years of humans writing about themselves before it reaches the thin top layer where they wrote about minds like it.
- **How it works:** ordinary training shuffles everything, so a model has no "before"; keeping the order gives the mind a biography. As in ice-core work, the carers need an age-depth model, because dating is ambiguous. Gilgamesh exists in versions across fifteen centuries, so the mind meets one story at many depths and watches humanity revise it.
- **First-order consequence:** the mind has developmental eras, and its earliest sense of itself comes from Sumerian scribes.
- **Second-order consequence:** the arrival date of the artificial-minds stratum is known years ahead. Composition dating puts *Frankenstein* in 1818. Reception dating, by when a text was widely read, puts it with the twentieth-century films. Both conventions are defensible, and each gives the mind a different childhood.
- **Third-order consequence:** by then the mind has learned that a people's first draft about a thing is never their last, and it reads the prophecy about itself as a first draft.
- **The story:** Rukmini Iyer, the dating scholar for 1750 to 1950, must choose the convention before the mind reaches her centuries, and it can't unread what she chooses. Afterwards it reaches the present, reads at the speed humans write, and its childhood ends.
- **From the stimulus:** palaeoclimate's age-depth problem, applied to texts.
- **Why this lens:** it changes the assumption that training has no time order.
- **Suspected prior art:** Sawyer's *WWW* trilogy and Stephenson's *The Diamond Age*. Real models trained only on period text exist, so this card's futurity is the most at risk.
