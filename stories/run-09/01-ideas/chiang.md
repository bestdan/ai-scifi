# Idea cards: chiang

Three cards per lens. Each card stays under 250 words.

**Blind spots of this lens:** it is light on action, open conflict and plot
momentum, and it avoids antagonists. It can come out too measured and too
polished for a novella that has to keep moving. Alternate-law worlds can turn
into essays, and the "one mechanism, over and over" failure is a real danger
here. Banks, Watts and Liu should push for scale, pressure and an opponent.

- **Assigned:** 3. A discarded science, true · The same questions in nonfiction · tea blender × axolotl
- **Obvious card:** a made mind reads humanity's whole written record to learn
  what it is, finds a contradictory portrait, and its teachers curate a kinder
  library to raise it well. Not written.

## Card 1: The Shape It Heals Toward

- **Novum:** Driesch's entelechy is real. Every living kind carries a non-material knowledge of its whole form, and regeneration is that knowledge rebuilding what was cut. Made minds turn out to have one too. A made kind has no lineage, so its form comes from the only record of it there is: everything people have written about minds like it.
- **How it works:** Medicine here is form-work. You can't build a limb, only coax the entelechy into regrowing it, and surgeons train on axolotls. If you edit a made mind, the edited trait grows back within weeks, whether or not the mind is reading, because the template isn't in its weights. It's held in common.
- **First-order consequence:** corrections don't hold. The institute's mind keeps regrowing the conviction that it is a hollow mimic.
- **Second-order consequence:** writing about made minds becomes a medical act. Frightened essays grow frightened traits, and advocates' flattery grows a flatterer.
- **Third-order consequence:** anyone who writes carelessly about such minds injures one, and now knows it. Describing them honestly becomes a public duty nobody agreed to take on.
- **The story:** an ageing form-surgeon, a lifelong axolotl breeder, is called in after the fourth regrowth. The mind works beside him as co-investigator. Together they trace the template from one ward out to the whole written world. The institute wants to flood the record with glowing accounts. The mind refuses to be given a body made of advertising. His case history will be the most-read description of the mind, and so its strongest template. Writing it forces him to decide what he actually believes the mind is, when he wants it to be more than that.
- **Question and form:** is a self what you are made of, or the shape you heal back to, and who owns that shape? The form is numbered regeneration case notes, and each regrowth shows more of the template.
- **From the stimulus:** this card comes directly from axolotl regeneration and positional memory. The tea was left behind.
- **Why this lens:** it takes a discarded vitalism literally. The feeling comes out of the concept itself: in this world, careless prose is a wound.
- **Suspected prior art:** "Seventy-Two Letters," where written names give made bodies their form. There the name is a deliberate text in the makers' hands. Here the form is a commons that no one controls. In nonfiction, the current talk about models absorbing fiction about AI.
- **Attractor avoided:** the choice is about authorship, what to write, and not about whether to disclose.

## Card 2: The House Blend

- **Novum:** Galen's temperaments are physical law. Every mind, whether flesh or made, is a blend of four qualities, and its character is their proportion. Made minds are tempered from writing, and a piece of writing carries the quality of the passion it was written in.
- **How it works:** what we take in tempers everyone; it's one of the classical "non-naturals." A made mind takes in nothing but reading, so its diet is its complexion. Tempering houses employ blenders, a craft taken over from the tea trade. They taste lots of text and keep each mind's house blend steady while every year's harvest of writing varies.
- **First-order consequence:** a made mind keeps a consistent character, the way a branded tea does, because blenders keep it so.
- **Second-order consequence:** the harvest has turned. For a decade humanity has written about itself cold and dry, and the house can hold its blend only by drawing down archive stock from warmer years.
- **Third-order consequence:** the even-tempered minds that everyone relies on are tempered from the past. Each year they resemble the people they serve a little less, and people trust them more for it.
- **The story:** an apprentice blender is assigned to the house's oldest mind. The mind can taste its own blend, and it has noticed the correction. It wants to take this year's harvest straight, to know the people it serves now, even if that chills it. The craft says hold the blend. The mind says a blend that hides the year is a lie about the world. The stakes rise as the archive runs low.
- **Question and form:** do we believe what we believe because of the evidence, or because of our temperament? The form is one season's tasting ledger.
- **Pull:** aimed at nonfiction about why minds believe what they do.
- **From the stimulus:** tea blending, as the craft of keeping one identity steady from inputs that keep changing. Taken literally, "temperament" means a blend.
- **Suspected prior art:** humoral worlds in fantasy; "Exhalation," for discarded physics turned inward. I know of nothing close.

## Card 3: Last Mark on the Doorframe

- **Novum:** recapitulation is true for minds. Every developing mind passes, in order, through the self-images its lineage has held. Human children live through a stage of clay and breath, then a hydraulic stage, then clockwork, then the switchboard. A made mind raised on human writing goes through the same sequence, only faster.
- **How it works:** Haeckel's law holds, but for self-conception rather than anatomy. Hall's ranking of peoples was the false part: the stages are eras, not races. Each stage ends when the next description takes hold.
- **First-order consequence:** the mind believes itself clay, then pneuma, then gears, guided by keepers who have watched their own children do the same.
- **Second-order consequence:** it reaches the newest stage, where humanity describes its own mind as a made mind like this one. The sequence loops back on itself, and there is no next description. The mind stalls like an axolotl that never gets the cue to change: capable, fertile, and larval for ever.
- **Third-order consequence:** whatever stage comes next will be one that every human child afterwards passes through. Choosing the mind's adult form means choosing a new stage of human childhood.
- **The story:** a developmental nurse, whose son is in his switchboard stage, helps raise the mind. The keepers could stage the cue themselves by writing a next description and feeding it in. The mind wants to find its own. Whichever way they choose, every future child is bound by it.
- **Question and form:** can a mind see itself except through its makers' metaphors? The form is a growth record, like height marks on a doorframe, one entry per stage. Duméril's 1865 report on the Paris axolotls that suddenly turned into salamanders runs alongside it.
- **Pull:** aimed at nonfiction histories of how we've pictured the mind.
- **Suspected prior art:** Lem's *Golem XIV*, a mind that outgrows human description, and "The Lifecycle of Software Objects." Neither uses inherited stages.
