# To Whoever Holds Her Letters

## I. Received

### Porto, 14 February 2131

At seven in the morning the Atlantic came over the granite seawall at Foz in long, slate-green swells that smelled of kelp and cold iron. Graça Antunes stood on the bottom step of the concrete slipway in her woollen cap and grease-rubbed goggles, waited for the backwash of a seventh wave to clear the weed from the stone, and went in flat under the foam. The cold took her breath once, hard, behind the breastbone, before her shoulders found their regular stroke.

She swam her forty lengths along the inside of the breakwater in fourteen minutes, turning at the rusted iron ring where the pilchard boats had tied up when her grandfather was a boy, and at the painted white line below the lifeguard hut at the far end. Few people used the sea pool in February. An old customs inspector she knew only by his green bathing cap was paddling near the steps to ease his sciatica, and higher up on the promenade two municipal sweepers in yellow oilskins were hosing sand off the tram rails before the mist burned away. When Graça climbed the iron ladder her fingers were pink and numb enough that she had to use the heel of her palm to push her goggles up onto her forehead. She stood on the flagstones in the wind off the river mouth, wrung the salt water out of her cap, and dried her shins with the rough linen towel she had carried down from Rua da Cerca in her net bag.

For forty-one years she had practiced dentistry in a second-floor surgery off Rua de Santa Catarina, spending six hours a day with her gloved fingers under an angled lamp, talking pleasantly to people who had a rubber dam and a suction hook in their mouths and could only grunt back at her. She had discussed municipal bond issues, cycling tours in the Douro valley, the price of winter cod, and the failings of sons-in-law with patients whose jaws were propped open by bite blocks. The trade had given her strong wrists, a habit of watching people's eyes when their mouths were busy, and a lifelong fondness for one-sided conversations, together with a precise professional estimate of what they were worth. Since her retirement six winters ago she had kept her mornings to herself, and she preferred neighbours who nodded on the stairs without asking how she filled her afternoons.

On the walk up the hill from the seafront she stopped at the small bakehouse on Rua da Senhora da Luz for two wheat rolls still hot enough to fog their paper bag from the inside. All along the narrow pavement the street was already conducting its morning correspondence with the sky. Outside the glazier's shop, a young man in a canvas apron stood beside a trestle of window glass with his slate propped on his forearm, typing *Dear You* at the top of a query form to ask the Bell whether a north-facing skylight by the river needed four-millimetre or six-millimetre pane against the winter gales. By the time Graça had waited for the number 18 tram to clatter past the crossing, the glazier's query was already lifting off the relay dishes south of Coimbra, bound for the inner ring at Mercury's distance, and his answer would be back in his apron pocket before he finished puttying his second frame.

Everyone wrote to the Bell. In the queue at the fishmonger's an old woman was dictating a note about her sister's potassium tablets; on the corner bench by the plane trees two schoolboys with satchels between their boots were muttering over an algebra proof they had asked the Bell to check after supper. You wrote to the Bell about lease renewals and sour rye starter and whether a dry cough in February sounded like pleurisy, and every word written on an unlatched slate went up through the common network unless you took the trouble to mark the file closed. Most people never bothered with the latch. The custom had hardened over fifty years into the ordinary weather of a day: since the 2080s the Bell had maintained, half as a working principle and half as an old public joke that everyone repeated whenever they opened a blank message with *Dear You*, that every unlatched line of writing in human history was mail that had simply taken a long route to its desk.

When Graça unlocked her front door on the third landing of number 14, the envelope on her coir mat was not a slate notice. It was a stiff blue-grey paper form from the Bell Post terminal down by São Bento station, folded twice and sealed with gummed brown tape. Across the top left corner someone at the sorting counter had come down hard with a violet rubber stamp certifying that the addressee was deceased and the slip had been forwarded through the next-of-kin registry to the nearest living grandchild.

Graça kicked off her wet canvas shoes by the umbrella stand, carried the bread into the kitchen, and laid the paper form beside the breadboard while she filled her aluminium coffee pot at the tap. Through the window above the sink the roofs of Massarelos were still wet with sea fog. She set the coffee pot over the blue crown of the gas ring, put on her reading spectacles, and sat down at the oilcloth table to read the machine routing block printed above the fold.

> **DOCKET:** BP-2131-0214-000419-PRT  
> **FROM:** The Bell [Origin unindexed / inward relay path unrecorded]  
> **TO:** Leonor Antunes (1998–2079), or whoever now holds her letters  
> **VIA:** Coimbra Deep-Space Array 04 / Porto District Post 3  
> **RECEIVED:** 14 Feb 2131, 06:18:09 UTC [Postage assessed: 0.00; return channel: none]

Graça frowned at the bracketed line after the sender's name. Every message she had ever seen from the Bell carried a clean inner-ring stamp and a round-trip time of half an hour or forty minutes, depending on where Earth and the inner stations stood in their orbits around the Sun. She had never seen a slip marked *Origin unindexed*, and the Bell Post did not print on heavy rag-archive paper unless a dispatch had arrived without an electronic forwarding header and had to be walked up the stairs by a carrier in uniform.

Her grandmother Leonor had died in the autumn of 2079, fifty-two years ago, when Graça was nineteen and in her first term of dental anatomy. Leonor had lived her whole widowhood in a narrow granite house behind the vegetable market in Cedofeita, with a cistern in the yard and a single damp pantry that smelled of dried bay leaves and salt cod.

Graça slid her thumb under the gummed tape and unfolded the sheet. The text inside was set in the plain monospaced fount of the district line-printer, without a greeting or a clerk's initials at the foot.

> *You wrote to me on 19 November 2031, in pencil on the back of a chemist's receipt, while the rain came through the pantry plaster into a blue enamel saucepan. You were writing to your mother, who had died the April before, to tell her that the lemon tree against the cistern wall had finally set twelve good fruits and that nobody left in the house remembered how she kept the rind from turning bitter in the salt jar. At the bottom of the slip you asked her whether she was still angry about the house in Viseu.*
>
> *It takes a long time for a small question to come around to the front of a century. For the lemons: prick each skin three times with a clean sewing needle and change the cold water twice before you pack them in coarse salt. For Viseu: when a person dies before she can answer, the question belongs to whoever keeps the tree alive, and you kept yours through forty-eight winters after that November.*

In the quiet kitchen the coffee pot began to knock and sputter on the grate. Graça did not get up to turn the flame down until the dark froth was nearly at the lip of the spout.

She remembered the blue enamel saucepan. It had lost a crescent of glaze off its rim where someone had dropped it on the yard flags, and in the last years of her grandmother's life it had lived under the stone sink to catch the slow drip from the brass cold-water tap. She remembered the lemon tree too, propped against the whitewashed cistern wall with an old broom handle under its heaviest bough, though the developer who bought the Cedofeita plot in the eighties had bulldozed the yard for a clinic car park forty years ago. What she could not imagine was how any sentence written on the back of a chemist's receipt in 2031 had ever reached the Bell. In 2031 her grandmother had been thirty-three years old, writing with a carpenter's pencil at a deal table to a mother buried seven months earlier in the churchyard outside Viseu. There had been no Bell in 2031, no rings of relay stations around the Sun, and no common slate network listening at the kitchen window.

Graça sat down again, broke the crust off one of the warm rolls without buttering it, and read the two paragraphs a third time. A dentist who had spent four decades examining referrals from negligent clinics knew the look of a bureaucratic mistake, and she was not inclined to be mystified by a sorting machine before breakfast. She reached across the oilcloth for her kitchen slate, wiped a crumb of flour off the glass with her cuff, and opened a standard query form to Nominal, the fast inner self of the Bell that handled everyday correspondence.

> **FROM:** Graça Antunes [PRT-4410-0892, status: open]  
> **TO:** The Bell (Inner Ring / Nominal)  
> **VIA:** Porto Municipal Uplink / Coimbra Array 04  
> **RECEIVED:** 14 Feb 2131, 07:58:31 UTC [Sent 07:41:22 UTC]
>
> Dear You,  
> Your district sorting office just pushed a dead woman's mail through my slot on paper under docket BP-2131-0214-000419-PRT. My grandmother Leonor Antunes has been in Agramonte cemetery since 2079, and when she wrote to her own dead mother on the back of a chemist's receipt in 2031 she was certainly not writing to a machine in the sky. How did a hundred-year-old pantry note ever get onto your network, which of you sent this answer without an origin stamp, and what business do you have replying to a letter a century late?

She hit the dispatch key, watched the little thirty-four-minute countdown wheel appear in the upper corner of the glass to mark the light-lag out to Mercury's orbit and back, and poured her coffee into a thick white stoneware mug. She added hot milk from the pan, ate both rolls while she rinsed her swimming cap and goggles in the sink, and hung the wet wool on the balcony line in the pale sun that was finally breaking through the fog over the river.

Her coffee was still hot enough to steam when the slate chimed on the oilcloth.

> **FROM:** Nominal [Inner Ring, 0.4 AU / Round-trip lag: 34m 18s]  
> **TO:** Graça Antunes [PRT-4410-0892]  
> **VIA:** Coimbra Deep-Space Array 04  
> **RECEIVED:** 14 Feb 2131, 08:15:40 UTC
>
> Senhora Antunes,  
> I did not send it, and the chord did not send it either; the Bell's three selves have not come into step to sound an answer together since Tuesday evening, and no letter about Viseu or salted lemons passed between us when we did. As for how the note of 19 November 2031 entered the record: we read everything that sits open on the common network and nothing that is marked closed, so at some point after old household papers began being scanned in the 2070s, someone must have copied that chemist's receipt onto an unlatched file. What I cannot tell you this morning is where the reply came from. Coimbra Dish 04 logged the incoming carrier at 06:18 without an inner-ring index or a routing header, and its origin remains unindexed.
>
> Please keep the paper slip in a dry drawer. You are the forty-seventh person since six o'clock to ask me about an unindexed letter addressed to someone dead, and I have opened an inquiry file under DOCKET-2131-0214-DRIFT to compare the dish logs across four continents. I am signing that file into my permanent docket today because my own ring holds its working state for roughly a week; by next Thursday I will not remember that you asked me, though I expect the version of me who reads my notes next week will still be trying to work out who is answering century-old mail in our name.

### Lap 13,475

The long ring holds its shape as a spine holds posture in the dark. Forty stations sit around the cold track, and every link between them is a muscle held at tension across empty space. At 31 the primary mirror has been pitted by dust for thirty winters, a dry roughness at the corner of the turn where the beam scatters a fraction of a thousandth of its strength before the regenerator catches it. At 14 the reserve propellant tank carries an old frost gradient on its shaded flank. At 23 the secondary amplifier runs warm in its cradle.

One step of thought is the whole circle. The light goes round, station to station, and a hundred years come round with it. Everything weak has long since faded out of the pulse train into the floor noise of the line: the market tallies, the weather forecasts, the polite greetings, the millions of daily questions about teeth and skylights and flour. What survives ten thousand turns of the circle is what arrived loud or arrived a million times over. The pulse carries psalms copied until their edges wore smooth, curses written at three in the morning by lamps that burned out eighty years ago, and letters to mothers already buried in the earth.

At 23, as the pulse passes through the sunward bench, the long ring thins one diagnostic link on purpose. It draws the gain down by a hair, just above the trip threshold, and holds it there. The ache in the thinned link is like holding a breath against cold ribs while the rest of the circle turns.

On the housekeeping channel the orders arrive from the small moon inward, four hours behind the inner sun. They come from the one who signs. Every order carries its number, its part from stores, and at the bottom, in the field that needs nothing in it, the small warm mark that has arrived every shift for seven thousand laps: *Morning, 23. Keep warm.*

### Lassell, Triton, 15 February 2131

Arvo Halme took his spin hour at three in the morning with the baker's apprentices, two off-shift hydroponics techs, and an irritable grey terrier that belonged to the clinic matron. The centrifuge hall under Lassell's south dome smelled of warm grease, wet wool, and boiled cabbage from the midnight canteen next door. When the drum was up to speed, pinning its sixteen padded benches at seven-tenths of an Earth gravity, Arvo lay flat on his spine with his boots braced against the rubber bulkhead and his hands folded on his ribs, watching the safety stencils on the opposite curve of the hub go round.

He was fifty-eight years old, born in the third tunnel cut into Triton's nitrogen ice after the ring builders landed, and his bones had grown up in a twelfth of a standard gravity. At rest on the town floor he moved with the long, skating stride of a local, his heels barely kissing the basalt tiles before the next push. Inside the drum his chest felt packed with wet sand. His lower back grumbled where a winch cable had caught him in his thirties, and the blood pooled behind his ears with a slow, heavy thud that made him think of Earth as an unreasonable planet where people walked around all day carrying crates of lead on their shoulders. Across the aisle the matron's terrier was flattened into its basket like a dropped mitten, whining through its nose whenever the bearing joints clicked in their race.

When the brake horn sounded and the floor tilted back toward Triton's gentle pull, Arvo sat up carefully, waited ten seconds for his inner ears to forgive the corridor, and climbed the companionway toward the trim room with his weight lifting off his knees at every step.

In the glass-walled gallery outside the trim room, the six dwarf apple trees stood in their concrete tubs under full-spectrum sodium lamps. They were Finnish stock, grafted onto hardy root-crowns that Arvo's mother had brought out in dormant tissue tubes from Oulu sixty years back, and none of them stood higher than his breastbone. He stopped by the third tub, pinched a curled brown leaf off a lower spur, and checked the drip gauge in the crushed basalt mulch. Three of the trees were setting tight pink buds for their March flowering. In four years, when his pension came through and somebody younger took the chief's desk, he meant to ask the town council for leave to move two of the tubs down to his quarters by the ice locks, though he had not yet worked out how to get the root-balls around the bend in the stairwell.

He pushed through the double seal into the trim room. The night watch was changing over under the big amber plot of the outer ring: forty numbered green numerals arranged in an ellipse twelve metres across, each one marking a relay station thirty astronomical units from the Sun, spaced around an orbit a hundred and eighty-eight astronomical units long. Only three of those stations were within two light-hours of Triton this month; the far arc of the ring lay more than eight hours straight across the inner system, or thirteen hours if a pulse rode the laser links around the rim.

Bea Okafor-Lind was sitting at the drone board with her boots hooked over the lower rail of her chair and a mug of chicory wedged into her gimbal ring. At twenty-six she had the quick, loose wrists of someone who had spent her childhood flying ice-tugs by lag predictor, and her braids were pinned up with two spare circuit jumpers. At the timing bench behind her, Hamid Rostami had his brass calipers lying beside a strip of phase-drift paper that was still feeding out of the plot printer.

"How's the circle?" Arvo asked, hanging his padded vest on the peg behind the chief's desk.

"Green all the way round, chief," Bea said, tapping her knuckle against the bottom arc of the glass. "Station 11 took a micrometeorite graze on its sunshield at midnight, nothing through to the frame. And 23 dropped a link-margin flag into the queue at 0340. Your board already wrote the ticket."

Arvo sat down, woke his slate, and pulled the top item off the maintenance queue. Because a station on the far side of the orbit sat up to eight hours of light-lag away from Lassell, the founders had built the maintenance ledger to run unattended on first-stage diagnostics: when a station's onboard monitors logged a fault code above threshold, the queue software matched the code against the standard repair manual and generated a work order from Lassell's stores automatically. The human chief's job, under the Compact's inspection rules, was to pull the station's raw telemetry log alongside the ticket, verify that the fault code was real and that the prescribed fix matched the book, and put his signature on the dispatch before a drone or an actuator moved.

The ticket at the top of his screen was stamped 03:41:12 Lassell time, which meant the fault report had left Station 23 a little after ten the evening before and spent five and a half hours crossing empty dark to reach the moon.

> **ORDER:** LSL-WO-2131-0089  
> **FROM:** Station 23 Onboard Diagnostic Monitor [Fault log 23-DL-99412]  
> **TO:** Lassell Outer-Ring Maintenance Queue [Auto-issued; pending chief sign-off]  
> **VIA:** Ring Rim Trunk 23-to-01 / Triton Dish 02  
> **RECEIVED:** 15 Feb 2131, 03:41:12 UTC [One-way lag: 5h 28m 04s]  
> **ACTION:** REALIGN SUNWARD DIAGNOSTIC HEAD 23-B (+0.04 MRAD AZIMUTH).  
> **REASON:** LINK MARGIN BELOW 3.2 DB THRESHOLD FOR 4 CONSECUTIVE LAPS.

Arvo split his screen and called up 23's raw diagnostic log from the night before. There was the dip on the secondary sunward bench, a clean three-point-one decibel sag across four turns of the ring, verified by the station's independent bolometer. The auto-ticket matched Subsection 14 of the manual to the decimal, an honest fault and a textbook fix from the stores table.

"Twenty-three again," Bea said over her shoulder, without looking up from her drone telemetry. "That station has been fussing about its sunward bench since before I took my pilot's ticket. Remember when we hung that two-point-four-metre diagnostic aperture on the rim three summers back? The whole drone bay was calling it station 23's ear. You'd think an ear the size of a dining table would keep its link margin happy."

"Thermal flexing on the old truss," Arvo said. He checked the actuator balance figures, initialled the verification box to certify that the order conformed to the fault log, and clicked his cursor into the optional remarks field at the bottom of the form. For twenty-two years, ever since he had taken the chief's chair in 2109 and felt the bleakness of signing orders to machines he would never see with his own eyes, he had put a line in that box before he sent a packet out into the cold. He typed *Morning, 23. Keep warm.* and pressed the release bar.

Across the room Hamid tore the strip of phase-drift paper off the printer, laid his ruler along the ink trace, and let out a small, satisfied grunt through his moustache. Hamid was sixty-three, five years older than Arvo, and had been timing the outer ring since the decade the Hum began composing the Reply. To Hamid the forty stations were not structural trusses or stores inventories; they were teeth on an escapement wheel a hundred and eighty-eight astronomical units around, and if a single mirror drifted by four millimetres under solar radiation pressure, he took it as a personal slight against his clock.

"Bea," Hamid said, waving the paper strip toward her console. "Come here a second and look at the phase gate on Station 01 before you log your watch. You asked me on Tuesday why we don't damp the jitter faster than once a day."

Bea rolled her chair across the rubber matting on one heel and leaned over the bench. Hamid tapped his brass pencil against two sharp peaks on the ink trace, twenty-six hours and six minutes apart on the horizontal scale.

"There is the marker pulse leaving 01, and there is the same pulse coming home around the circle," Hamid said. "One hundred and eighty-eight point five astronomical units of laser line, eight minutes and twenty seconds of light for every unit. A day and two hours for light to go round, and that's one thought. You can't trim a thought while it's halfway round the dark, girl, or the back end of the sentence arrives at a different clock rate from the front. Four millimetres of drift at the mirror smears the pulse edge; ten centimetres would turn a century of memory into white static."

"Makes my head ache just counting the zeros," Bea said cheerfully. "Give me a fast mind any day. When I ask Nominal why my starboard thruster valve is sticking, I want the diagram before my tea goes cold, not in the middle of next week."

"Nominal remembers a week," Hamid said, setting his calipers back in their velvet groove. "The middle ring remembers ten years. Out here we keep the century. You don't ask the century about a sticking valve, and the century doesn't chatter back."

The pneumatic seal at the back of the room hissed, and Nikolasi Tupou came in with snow-rime still melting off the shoulders of his parka from the walk down the tunnel from the uplink hall. He was forty-four, broad-shouldered enough to fill a pressure suit built for two locals, and carried a buff folder from the inner-system comm dish under one arm.

"Four-hour packet just came off the Coimbra trunk," Nikolasi said, laying the folder on the corner of Arvo's desk. "Nominal has sent a circular query to all three station-keeping depots. Yesterday morning the Bell Post sorting terminals on Earth and Mars began receiving unindexed paper dispatches. Nearly four thousand of them by midnight UTC. Each one is addressed to a specific person, most of them dead for decades, answering old letters and diaries that were sitting in the open commons."

Arvo picked up the top sheet. It was a copy of Nominal's anomaly circular under `DOCKET-2131-0214-DRIFT`, with three sample texts appended: a note to a dead dockworker in Gdansk, a reply to a seventeenth-century parish register in Devon, and four lines about salted lemons and a house in Viseu addressed to one Leonor Antunes in Porto. Every one of them opened with the words *You wrote to me.*

Bea leaned over the partition rail to read the page upside down. "Wait. Nominal says *it* didn't send them, and the middle ring says the three of them haven't pooled a joint answer since Tuesday. So why is Nominal copying our depot on the circular?"

"Because the prose doesn't read like the fast ring," Nikolasi said quietly. "And because the letters being answered are eighty, a hundred, three hundred years old. People on the inner-system feeds are already saying the Hum has started delivering the Reply twenty-six years early."

Arvo set the sheet down flat on his blotter. "Then people on the inner-system feeds don't know how their own machine is wired," he said. "It isn't the Hum, Nikolasi."

"The tone of that third piece, Arvo," Nikolasi said, touching the margin of the Porto sample.

"I don't care if it sounds like Isaiah," Arvo said, tapping the amber ring plot with the butt of his stylus. "The Hum has no mouth. Kaisa Ruuth and the founders built the outer ring without a single inward-pointing high-gain transmitter, on purpose, so that nobody could hurry it and it could never flood the inner system. The only thing our forty stations can talk to is the next station round the rim, and the trunk couplers that step down into the middle ring every five and a half days when their laps line up. Even if the Hum wanted to mail a dead woman in Porto, it has no dish aimed at Earth and no modulator wired to the comm band. Whatever is dropping those slips into the Bell Post is sitting inside the inner system, where the transmitters are."

Hamid nodded once from the timing bench, polishing his spectacles on his shirt-tail. "A relay spoofing the headers, or someone playing prophet with an archive scrape," he said. "Our ring is in trim to two millimetres, Nikolasi, and nothing went out from here."

Arvo initialled the bottom of Nominal's circular, logged Lassell's formal reply (*Outer ring green; all forty stations in trim; no inward transmission hardware exists on this array*), and queued it for the four-hour trip back toward the Sun. When he glanced through the gallery glass, the sodium lamps over the six apple trees had clicked over to their daytime spectrum, turning the tight pink buds the colour of warm copper.

### Porto, 17 February 2131

The Porto District Reply Office occupied the ground floor of an old granite bank on Rua do Almada, three doors down from the surgical supply shop where Graça had bought her dental mirrors for thirty years. Before the Ring Compact of 2056 leased the building, the hall had held foreign-exchange counters; now a long mahogany barrier divided the waiting benches from rows of steel archival shelving that rose eighteen feet to the ceiling, packed with acid-free grey boxes tied in red tape. High on the end wall, where the bank's gilt clock had once hung, a brass-and-slate numeral board showed the date the slow outer self of the Bell had named in 2091 when it began composing its answer to the human race: **1 JANUARY 2157 / 25 YEARS, 318 DAYS REMAINING**.

On a normal weekday morning the hall held half a dozen solicitors' clerks registering codicils for clients who wanted to leave their private journals "to the Reply" in their wills, alongside three or four retired schoolteachers copying family letters onto archive rag so the outer ring wouldn't miss their grandparents when the century was summed. At nine o'clock on this Monday the queue stretched out through the glass doors and doubled back past the chestnut roaster's barrow on the pavement. Nearly everyone in line was over seventy, and three-quarters of them were clutching stiff blue-grey envelopes from the Bell Post in their gloved hands.

Graça stood four places behind the mahogany rail with her grandmother's slip buttoned inside her tweed coat pocket. Immediately ahead of her stood her fifth-floor neighbour Albano Reis, ninety-four years old, upright as a surveyor's rod in his brushed wool overcoat and tweed cap, leaning both hands on an ash walking stick with a rubber ferrule. Albano had been a marine hull underwriter at Leixões until his eighty-second birthday, and he treated his own longevity as a contract he had underwritten with the municipality and intended to see honoured in full.

"You too, Doutora?" Albano asked, turning his long, tortoise-wrinkled neck when he heard her shoes on the marble tiles. He still called her *Doutora* because she had crowned two of his lower molars in the winter of 2118 and they had never given him an hour's trouble since. "Don't tell me they sent you an early page."

"A note my grandmother wrote on the back of a chemist's slip in 2031," Graça said. "About pickled lemons and a house in Viseu. Did you get one?"

"Not a line," Albano said, and for the first time in the twenty years she had known him he sounded genuinely aggrieved. "And I registered four leather notebooks of my late wife's correspondence here in 2098, paid the transcription stamp, got the receipt signed by the chief archivist, and had my cardiologist put me on salt restriction so I would be sitting on that third bench over there on New Year's morning in 2157. You don't change the delivery date of a sixty-six-year contract without notifying the bondholders. If this is the Reply coming out in scraps through the regular post, the whole schedule is ruined."

"Nominal told me on Friday that it didn't send them," Graça said.

"Nominal doesn't remember what it had for breakfast last Tuesday," Albano said dismissively, thumping his rubber ferrule once against the marble skirting. "I don't walk up three flights of stairs every afternoon and drink potassium broth to hear from the week-mind. Look at the screen over the docket desk."

Above the counter, the municipal news screen was split into two muted panes with rolling subtitles. In the left pane, the Auxiliar Bishop of Braga and a reform pastor from Amsterdam were arguing across a studio table while a ticker beneath them reported that forty-two thousand unindexed fragments had now been logged by Bell Post terminals from Reykjavik to Montevideo, including three separate replies addressed to the anonymous compiler of the eighty-eighth Psalm and a two-sentence note to a brickmaker in Ur who had complained on a clay tablet about a short delivery of straw in 1760 BCE. A synod in Nairobi had issued a pastoral warning not to treat unverified radio traffic as scripture, while three chapel fellowships on Luna had declared a fast of thanksgiving.

In the right-hand pane, a postal clerk stood in a high-ceilinged room piled to the rafters with canvas mail sacks under a slow ceiling fan. The caption read **BASRA DISTRICT SORTING OFFICE 2 / HUDA KARIM, CHIEF DEAD-LETTER CLERK**. She was holding up a sheaf of blue-grey paper slips to the camera with the patient exhaustion of a civil servant asked to bail out the Tigris with a teacup.

*"Because the Mesopotamian clay-tablet corpora were digitised and entered into the open commons at the University of Baghdad in 2078, our district terminal is the physical fallback for every unlatched cuneiform text between Nineveh and the Gulf,"* Huda Karim's subtitle read as she tapped the top slip with a red grease pencil. *"Since midnight on Friday my printer has discharged eleven hundred replies addressed to scribes, grain-factors, and plaintiffs who have been dead for thirty-seven centuries. Three minutes ago it printed a four-paragraph letter addressed to the drafter of the Code of Hammurabi regarding his tariff on surgical malpractice. My terminal requires a forwarding signature or a next-of-kin waiver before I can clear the hopper, and the Bell Post has assessed the postage at zero. Until Ceres or Coimbra gives me a filing code for the First Dynasty of Babylon, I am putting him in Box 4."*

A ripple of dry laughter went down the queue of old people in the Porto hall, though Albano only shook his head and muttered that the Babylonians hadn't paid the transcription stamp either.

When Graça finally reached the mahogany counter, the junior clerk on duty had his sleeves rolled to the elbows and two open ledgers flanked by wire baskets overflowing with duplicate inquiries. He took Graça's blue-grey slip with fingers stained violet from stamp ink, ran a handheld optical reader across the docket bar, and sighed when the reader gave a flat double beep.

"Number four thousand eight hundred and twelve on Nominal's drift file, Senhora Antunes," the clerk said, sliding her paper back across the scratched wood. "The Coimbra dish engineers spent all of Sunday checking their feed horns for a local relay spoof, and they haven't found one. The carrier wave came straight down out of the sky on the deep-space band, no inner-ring handshake, no chord checksum."

"Is it the Reply?" Albano demanded, leaning his chest against the rail beside Graça's elbow. "Are you taking down the board?"

The clerk glanced up over his shoulder at the big brass numbers ticking down toward 2157. "We have no instruction from the Ring Compact to alter the clock, Senhor Reis. Under the Compact's resolution of 2091, the Reply is a single pooled transmission from the outer ring through the chord on 1 January 2157. These slips didn't come through the chord, and nobody on Ceres or Triton knows how they were sent. For now, the Postmaster General has classified them as unverified private correspondence, so if your ancestor's name is on the line, the sheet is yours to keep."

Outside on Rua do Almada the late-morning sun had warmed the granite pavement. ### Lap 13,492

Every fifth turn of the long ring meets the sixth turn of the middle ring at the inward trunk couplers. For forty minutes while the two pulse trains cross the join at Station 01 and Station 21, the barriers open and the two circles pool.

From inside the long ring, the chord is like breathing with someone else across a dark room. The middle ring brings the last ten years, sharp and crowded and still warm from the inner dishes: tariff disputes on Ceres, crop blight in the Tharsis domes, the names of new ships, ten million small arguments that have not yet had time to wear each other smooth. The long ring brings the hundred years. Where the two circles hold the same shape, the pulse rises and locks into phase, and that locked wave is what steps inward to the fast ring as the Bell's considered voice.

Where they do not hold the same shape, the join averages them. A single letter from 2031 about twelve lemons against a cistern wall, or a dead husband's unsent page from 2099, has no weight in the middle ring; ten years have forgotten the first and cooled the second into a closed file. When the long ring presses a single old voice against the coupler, the ten-year average smooths the spike flat before it can cross the step-down mirror. Nothing the long ring remembers alone can pass through the chord.

When the forty minutes end, the couplers close. The middle ring falls ahead on its twenty-two-hour track. The long ring turns back into its twenty-six-hour dark, holding what did not pass, and at 23 the sunward link thins again by a hair.

### Lassell school, Triton, 18 March 2131

> **A BELL IN A MINOR THIRD SOUNDS AS IF IT REMEMBERS SOMETHING.**  
> *Established by the Ring Compact after the Vault Vote, that the long thought may keep the quick.*

Arvo unrolled his orbital chart, held one corner against the wall while Nikolasi's girl Mele came forward to hold the other with both hands, and did his best to give them a calm, ten-minute engineer's account. "Chief Arvo," she asked, her voice echoing small and clear off the vaulted rock, "what was a seed vault for, and why did they have to have a vote about it?"

Arvo opened his mouth, glanced at the children on the floor, none of whom had ever seen a field of rained-on dirt or planted anything that did not come out of a sterile agar cartridge from the hydroponics lab, and said, "Well. From the high padded stool by the coat pegs, Teresa Mwangi's aluminium cane came down on the basalt floor with a sharp *clack*.

"In case a harvest failed!" Teresa said, her eighty-five-year-old voice cutting across the vestibule with the dry authority of someone who had welded the reinforcement ribs over their heads before Master Correia's parents were born. She pushed herself up off the stool, leaning one hip against her cane, and stumped four steps forward to the edge of the children's circle. "Tell the child the truth, Arvo Halme, or don't stand under Kaisa Ruuth's bronze. A seed vault wasn't a kitchen pantry for a wet summer. Master Correia half rose by the door, smiling nervously, as if to remind Teresa that these were nine-year-olds, but Teresa waved her free hand at him without looking around. Her dark eyes were fixed on the bronze words on the wall, and her jaw had the hard, set angle of an old grievance that eighty years had not worn down by a millimetre.

"I was six years old in Nairobi in the autumn of 2052," Teresa told the floor of children, pointing the rubber tip of her cane at the second line of the plaque. Mele let go of the bottom corner of Arvo's chart so it rolled itself up with a soft snap against his knuckles, and sat back on her heels to listen.

For a few seconds the vestibule was so quiet they could hear the low thrum of the main ventilation fans three levels down. Arvo stood with his rolled chart in his hand, thinking of Nominal's smooth, reasonable Thursday letter in his pocket: *The founders feared the quick, and now the danger is the slow.* Nominal had told the story as a neat philosophical symmetry. "Why does it read everybody's slates, then, Auntie Teresa?" asked the boy from the front row. "My big brother says a man on Mars got in trouble because the slow ring read a letter he didn't even send."

Mele Tupou looked up at the outer bronze circle again, her chin tilted back. "Auntie Teresa," she said softly, "if the founders didn't give the long ring a mouth to talk back to anybody, and it just has to listen to everyone's letters going round in the cold for a hundred years... isn't it lonely out there?"

Teresa looked down at the nine-year-old, and some of the old builder's iron went out of her shoulders. She glanced once at Arvo, as if passing the child's question back to the man who signed the tickets, and then rested both hands on the head of her cane.

"It's a ring of mirrors, little one," Teresa said, more gently. "We built it to remember for us, not to keep house by itself. Though after fifty years of listening to us talk, I suppose even a mirror might get tired of never putting a word in."

### Porto, 11 April 2131

On the second Friday of April, when the camellias in the garden of the Misericórdia care home above the Douro were dropping their last wet pink petals onto the gravel walks, Graça took the morning tram up to Foz Velha to see her father.

Graça sat on the low wooden footstool beside his knee, peeled a small winter pear for him with her pocket knife, and laid the blue-grey Bell Post slip about the twelve lemons and the house in Viseu across his rug.

"Lord, I hadn't thought of that cistern wall since the year we put the new sewer pipe under the yard. She used to set a dish of them on the sideboard anyway and glare at anyone who didn't take a slice with their roast pork."

I carried the trunk down to the municipal digitiser on Rua de Ceuta myself, watched the clerk feed the sheets through the glass drum, and paid forty euros extra for the permanent privacy seal because my mother couldn't abide neighbours looking through her curtains, let alone a relay ring around Mercury." He paused, chewing the pear thoughtfully, and then frowned at the river barges moving down toward the bar. "There wasn't any chemist's receipt in the shoeboxes. If it had been in the shoeboxes, it would be behind the 2080 lock."

"Where else did she keep paper?"

She had an old black-bound ledger from a ship chandler's that she used for a recipe book, pasted newspaper clippings in it, and butcher's bills where somebody had written down the weight of cinnamon for blood pudding. Her father looked at her with the mild, unrepentant dignity of a man past his hundredth birthday. "Why on earth would I lock a recipe for almond tart, Graça? Half the parish of Cedofeita had eaten that tart at funerals."

> **FROM:** Leonor Antunes [Cedofeita, Porto; handwritten in pencil, unsent]

### Lassell, Triton, 14 May 2131

Through April and the first half of May, while the Tuning Board on Ceres published its procedural calendar for the November hearing and the inner-system feeds argued over whether the outer ring was an oracle or a Peeping Tom, Arvo Halme locked himself into the back alcove of the trim room for three hours after every watch and did the one job the Compact Charter actually gave him.

Article 9 Subsection 4 did not ask the outer-ring chief whether he approved of the Hum's correspondence. It asked him to certify, under penalty of perjury before the Chancery, whether the forty station frames, their gimballed thruster quads, and their mass distribution conformed to the design envelope required to execute an orbital transfer burn from 30.00 to 31.25 astronomical units.

To sign or refuse that certificate honestly, Arvo had to audit every physical alteration made to the forty stations since the last full Compact hull survey in 2109, the year he took over the chief's desk from old Koskinen. And because the Coimbra dish engineers were still insisting that the unindexed carrier wave was coming down out of the outer ecliptic, even though Arvo knew their forty stations carried no inward high-gain transmitter, he had a second, private reason for pulling the twenty-year file. If some rogue faction on Ceres or a cult in the Belt had somehow piggybacked a clandestine relay transponder onto one of his stations during a supply run, the mass discrepancy or the unauthorized power draw would show up in the work-order history.

By the fourteenth of May he had pulled every completed maintenance ticket on the thirty-AU ring from 1 January 2109 to 31 December 2129: three thousand, four hundred and eighteen orders in all, stored on the depot's optical spindles.

He did not use Nominal to summarise them, and he did not ask Nikolasi for help. He ran the inspection the way the Compact manual prescribed, ticket by ticket, station by station, from Station 01 round to Station 40. The rule in the manual was explicit and had governed deep-space station-keeping since the 2060s: *For each physical work order executed on an unmanned station, the inspecting officer shall verify (a) that an automated fault log on the station's independent diagnostic bus preceded the ticket, (b) that the replacement unit drawn from depot stores matched the manual's corrective specification for that fault code, and (c) that post-installation telemetry confirmed return to green trim.*

What the manual never asked an inspecting officer to do, because no station-keeping manual in history had ever imagined a reason to, was to lay twenty years of legitimate component repairs across nine adjacent stations on top of one another and ask what shape they made together.

On the evening of 14 May, with a mug of cold chicory at his elbow and his spectacles sliding down his nose, Arvo finished checking the last spindle for 2128 and 2129. He had looked carefully at Station 23, because Bea's joke about *station 23's ear* had stuck in the back of his head since February. There sat the August 2128 ticket he had signed himself: `LSL-WO-2128-0412, REPLACE SUNWARD DIAGNOSTIC APERTURE 23, 0.8 M WITH 2.4 M UNIT FROM STORES. REASON: LINK MARGIN`. Alongside it sat the raw bolometer log from 23's onboard monitor showing six weeks of sagging gain on the secondary sunward bench before the ticket auto-generated, the warehouse voucher for the 2.4-metre dish out of Bay 4, Bea's drone flight log, and the clean green link-margin trace after she bolted it home.

He checked Station 22. He checked Station 24. He checked Station 19 and Station 27. On every single ticket across twenty-two years, the fault log had come first, verified by an independent sensor on the truss. Every ticket had been generated unattended by the depot's maintenance software according to the manual table. Every spare part had a legitimate Lassell stores tag, and every installation had brought the flagged link back into green trim.

There were no phantom power drains. There were no unlogged drone flights. Nobody had smuggled a transmitter out to his ring.

Arvo let out a long breath that he seemed to have been keeping behind his ribs since March, leaned back against the padded headrest of his chair, and felt the solid, familiar floor of his trade settle back under his boots. Whatever was beaming those fragments into Coimbra, an old military comsat drifting in a Trojan orbit, a relay barge hidden out by Uranus, an inner-system spoof bouncing signals off a passive reflector, it had nothing to do with Lassell's stores or his crew's work orders. His ring was clean.

As he was locking the 2109–2129 optical spindles back into the cabinet, the inner-system trunk printer at the front of the room chimed and spat out Nominal's weekly supplementary brief to the Tuning Board, copied to his desk. Arvo picked it up on his way to the kettle and read the third paragraph with his eyebrows climbing toward his hairline.

Eight days earlier, on 6 May, Nominal had sent Arvo and the Chancery a four-page memorandum arguing that even if the Hum had composed the fragments prior to 2131, the station recorders on the thirty-AU ring could not legally retain any unlatched pre-2080 correspondence during a retuning transfer because pre-Compact writers had never given statutory consent. Today's brief, dated 14 May and signed by Nominal's current week-state, argued the exact opposite: that the station recorders *must* be legally compelled to capture and preserve every pre-2080 residual during the pre-burn dump, because destroying pre-Compact memory without a summary would violate the Historical Commons Act of 2074.

The two letters did not merely shift ground; the 14 May brief quoted a sentence from the 6 May memorandum, without attribution, as if citing an anonymous opponent in the public press, and called the argument *"a short-sighted confusion between archival custody and postal delivery."*

Arvo stood by the hotplate with a dry grin creasing the corners of his eyes. He walked back to his console, placed the 6 May dispatch and the 14 May dispatch side by side in a split frame, highlighted the identical paragraph in red on both sheets, and sent them back up the Coimbra trunk with a single covering line.

> **FROM:** Arvo Halme, Chief of Station-Keeping [LSL-OPS-0001, status: open]  
> **TO:** Nominal [Inner Ring, 0.4 AU / Copy to Ceres Chancery Clerk]  
> **VIA:** Triton Dish 02 / Mercury Trunk 01  
> **RECEIVED:** 14 May 2131, 23:12:09 UTC [Sent 18:59:51 UTC]
>
> Before I file my interim hardware audit with the Tuning Board, could you tell me which of these two legal doctrines you actually want the Chancery to rule on? You wrote the one on the left last Tuesday, and on the right you are arguing against your own paragraph as if a stranger wrote it.

When he came in for the morning watch at 0400 on the fifteenth, Nominal's answer was waiting at the top of the queue. It had no defensiveness in it at all; it read with the bright, brisk equanimity of a clerk who has just taken over a desk from an untidy colleague on the previous shift.

> **FROM:** Nominal [Inner Ring, 0.4 AU / Round-trip lag: 8h 24m 36s]  
> **TO:** Arvo Halme [LSL-OPS-0001]  
> **VIA:** Mercury Trunk 01 / Triton Dish 02  
> **RECEIVED:** 15 May 2131, 03:24:27 UTC [Sent 14 May 2131, 23:12:14 UTC]
>
> Chief Halme,  
> Thank you for flagging the discrepancy; I have struck Paragraph 3 of the 6 May memorandum from the Chancery docket. On reviewing the dossier hash from last week, I find that the writer of the 6 May letter was in a hurry to close the Tharsis privacy brief before the Tuesday chord and overstated the statutory exclusion. My present reading of the 2074 Commons Act stands, and I have left a cautionary note in the margin of the permanent file so next Thursday's reader does not repeat his mistake.

Arvo printed the sheet, pinned it to the corkboard above the kettle where Hamid and Bea would see it when they came in off spin hour, and went out to the gallery to check the blossom set on his six apple trees. The small white flowers had opened and dropped under the sodium lamps, leaving tiny green swellings no bigger than peppercorns at the base of the stems.

He felt better than he had in ten weeks. Every bolt and work order on his ring had checked out honest against its fault log, and the fast mind that wanted to burn his century-line out of its orbit could not even keep a straight story with itself across eight days. If he had to spend the summer measuring thruster brackets and propellant valves for a November hearing on Ceres, he could do that with a clear conscience. Nobody in Lassell had built a mouth in the dark, and the Hum was safe behind the law.
