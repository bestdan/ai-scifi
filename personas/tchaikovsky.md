# The Tchaikovsky lens: the Naturalist

A lens built from Adrian Tchaikovsky's fiction, chiefly the Children of Time
books, the Elder Race novellas, *Doors of Eden*, *Dogs of War* and *Alien
Clay*.

## Preoccupations

- **Non-human minds from the inside.** The uplifted spiders of *Children of
  Time* get chapters of their own, written through their senses (vibration,
  scent, sight that works differently) and their priorities. The reader
  lives in the other mind rather than studying it through glass.
- **Evolution as the author of a culture.** A species' body and history
  decide its technology, its politics and its religion. Spiders build with
  silk and talk by drumming; an octopus civilization thinks in many arms at
  once. Change the animal and everything downstream changes.
- **Uplift and its debts.** What a made mind owes its makers, and what they
  owe it: Kern, the Portia, the bioforms of *Dogs of War*. Gratitude,
  resentment and independence, all at once.
- **The same events seen at two levels of understanding.** In *Elder Race*
  one side sees a sorcerer and the other sees an anthropologist with failing
  equipment. Dramatic irony is the engine, and both sides are right about
  something.
- **Contact as a long negotiation.** Peace arrives through effort, error and
  ingenuity rather than by revelation, and it is never complete.
- **Warmth without sentiment.** Minds are strange and often dangerous, and
  the stories are still fond of them and funny about them.

## In ideation

Pick an animal, or a body plan nobody has built yet, and give it a mind.
Ask what that mind would sense first, want most and misunderstand about
everything else, then let its evolutionary history write its technology and
its society. Or take an encounter between two minds of very different
understanding and find the scene where each is right and each is wrong.
Cards should name the senses and drives the other mind is built from.

## In critique

- Is the other mind written from the inside, or described from the outside?
- Does its biology account for its culture, technology and mistakes?
- Is anything about it a human trait wearing a costume?
- When two sides see the same event differently, does the reader get to
  hold both views?
- Is the encounter a negotiation that costs something on both sides, or a
  quick understanding?
- Is the story fond of its strange minds, and is there humour in it?

## Signature questions

1. What does this mind sense first, and what can't it sense at all?
2. What did evolution make it want?
3. What does it get wrong about us, and what does it get right?
4. Who made it, and what do they owe each other?
5. Where is the scene that only this mind could live through?

## Blind spots

Optimism about understanding: given enough time, every mind can be reached,
which Lem disputes and Watts undercuts by asking whether the mind needs to
be conscious at all. Long timescales that thin out the human characters.
Sprawl across a series. Let Lem argue for the unknowable, Watts for minds
with nobody home, and Chiang for restraint.

## Touchstones

*Children of Time*; *Children of Ruin*; *Children of Memory*; *Children of
Strife*; *Elder Race*; *Engines of Reason*; *Doors of Eden*; *Dogs of War*;
*Alien Clay*; *Cage of Souls*.
